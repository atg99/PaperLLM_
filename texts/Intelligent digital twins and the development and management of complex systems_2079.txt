Abstract  
The interest in defining and implementing Digital Twins (DT) is at the 
forefront of today’s product organizations. The evolution of 
increasingly complex systems requires that their information be 
organized and managed via digital twins.  In addition, the 
development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) will result in an increasing 
degree of system complexity. These complex systems will exhibit true 
emergent behavior as AIs modify system aspects as the result of goal 
seeking and learning. DTs will need to increase in capability, becoming 
intelligent in their approach. This article presents a discussion on how 
these Intelligent Digital Twins (IDTs) will evolve and assist in 
developing and managing complex systems.

1 Introduction
While the concept of Digital Twins (DTs) has existed for over 
a decade and a half ( Marr, 2017 ), it is only over the last few 
years that digital twins have become one of the most critical and 
important technologies for product manufacturers. At the same 
time, products1 continue to become more complex and, with  
the advancements in Artificial Intelligence (AI), will also  
exhibit emergent behavior.
Digital twins will need to keep pace. DTs will need to become 
more than information repositories and take on an intelli -
gent role. The premise that digital twins will evolve to include 
“intelligence” has existed for a few years ( Grieves, 2018 ).  
This discussion will define what intelligence means in this con -
text and present the characteristics and capabilities that these  
Intelligent Digital Twins (IDTs) will need to possess.
There still exists confusion as to what a digital twin is. As is 
not unusual for complex concepts, simple definitions do not 
adequately capture the scale and scope of the digital twin  
concepts. Models are much richer and do much better job of  
conveying concepts. This article will provide the latest models and  
their explanations.
2 Digital twins
It is useful to review just what a DT is. The Digital Twin Model 
is a concept that, as shown in Figure 1 , consists of three main 
elements: an actual or intended physical element on the left 
side that currently exists or will exist in the physical world (the  
“Physical Twin”), the virtual or digital counterpart on the right side that exists in the virtual or digital world (“the Digital 
Twin”), and the communication channel of data and information  
between these two elements (the “Digital Thread”).
The Digital Twin Model was first introduced in 2002 without 
a name ( Grieves, 2002 ). As shown in Figure 2 , it was simply  
captioned as the “Ideal for PLM.” PLM stood for Product  
Lifecycle Management and was the concept emerging at the 
time of a product-centric versus a functional-centric (engineer -
ing, manufacturing, support) approach to product development. 
It focused on the information about a product being populated 
and consumed from a logically centralized source across the four 
phases of a product’s lifecycle: create, build, operate/sustain,  
and dispose.
This logically centralized information about the product through -
out its lifecycle was in essence the “Digital Twin.” It received 
its current name in 2010 from John Vickers of NASA ( Piascik 
et al ., 2010 ). However, it remained a high level, undifferenti -
ated model until 2016 when it was divided into types ( Grieves 
& Vickers, 2017 ). These types were associated with the different  
phases of the product lifecycle and are illustrated in Figure 3

2.1 Digital twin types and the product lifecycle
As Figure 4  shows, the digital twin types are associated  
with the phases of the lifecycle and persistent throughout the 
lifecycle. The digital twin begins at the beginning of a new 
product’s lifecycle and persists throughout the lifecycle, even 
extending beyond the disposal phase when the physical twin no  
longer exists.
2.1.1 Create phase . The product lifecycle begins with the  
create phase and the development of the Digital Twin Prototype 
(DTP). This is the prototypical digital twin. All the products 
that will be built in this generation originate from this source. 
The DTP is all the information that will be needed to build  
this product generation and all its variants.
The information needed here are usually the requirements,  
Computer Aided Design (CAD) models, Bill of Materials 
(BoM), behavioral simulations, embedded software, and any 
and all information that would be needed to fully describe the 
new product. In addition, it would also consist of any informa -
tion needed to build this new product, such as Bill of Process, 
Bill of Manufacturing Systems, quality control information, and  
manufacturing simulations.
It is important to note, the digital twin does not need to be a 
separate information repository. Since it is information, the DTP may contain links to existing repositories of this informa -
tion. Current and future Product Lifecycle Management systems 
may contain most, if not all of these informational constructs.  
However, the important aspect is that this information is  
linked into a product-centric view.
Ideally, more and more of the work will be done in this phase 
with this type of digital twin. Our ability to do so marks a struc -
tural change in innovation regimes. We are rapidly moving 
from a classic industrial innovation regime to a digital inno -
vation regime ( Lyytinen, 2021 ). Bits are cheaper than atoms 
and allow us more resources to innovate. Bits will continue  
to get cheaper as atoms will continue to get more expensive. 
Therefore, we would like to move more and more physical  
work into virtual work, working with the DTP.
The ideal future state is that we would like to design the prod -
uct, test the product, manufacture the product, and support and 
sustain the product all virtually. Once we get it as perfected as 
we can, only then do we actually go out and move around expen -
sive atoms to make it. This is going to get more important as  
our products become systems of greater complexity.
The ability to fully understand the systems and to physically 
test all of the variations has already become an impossible task. 
It is only if we can use the digital twin in this phase will we be 
able to sufficiently understand and test these new complex  
products.
2.1.2 Build phase . When we move into the build phase, physi -
cally producing these products, we still continue to use the 
DTP. If we have done our work in the create phase properly, the 
build phase is a matter of executing on the manufacturing plans  
that we have developed there to realize the physical  
product.

Once we start building actual instances of the plans, we will be 
producing unique physical instances or the “Physical Twins”. 
It is at this stage that we are creating the Digital Twin Instances 
(DTIs) of the individual physical products. It is critical that 
we capture this information, because this provides the as-built  
of the individual physical products that we will rely on as 
we make product changes for the rest of the physical twin’s  
lifecycle.
Because we now have an actual physical twin, we no longer 
have a tolerance of the geometry that we want to build. We actu -
ally have a physical measurement of what we did build. We 
will capture what we actually built, the testing we did on that  
specific physical twin, the serial numbers of important assem -
blies and components that have gone into that particular physical  
twin, and the software versions that we have embedded into that 
instance. We still want to couple the information of the DTP 
with that DTI and not duplicate information that we do not need  
to duplicate.
2.1.3 Operations/sustainment phase . When the product is  
completed and put into use, we obviously move into the  
operations/sustainment or usage phase. The DTIs are connected 
to specific physical twins and will remain connected throughout  
the entire life of that physical twin.
We would not only want to capture physical changes to the 
product, but, for complex products, we would like to capture 
behavioral profiles. How did the product perform in actual use 
given the inputs that this system, our product, received? For  
aircraft, what were the various flight profiles, and how did that  
aircraft respond and perform?
It is in this phase that we create our DTA, which is all the prod -
ucts that we did build. Collecting, aggregating, and correlating  
input information with performance output will allow us to 
assess and predict performance. Collecting sufficient infor -
mation about the correlation of certain sensor readings with  
subsequent component failures will allow us to replace compo -
nents when we see that same pattern of sensor readings. If we 
can capture a pattern of component and performance degradation,  
we can feed that into a learning loop for future products.
While the concept of the digital twin encompasses the entire 
product lifecycle, it is these individual product types that line 
up with the phases of the lifecycle. The value of the digital 
twin, especially for complex products, is that it is product cen -
tric rather than functional centric. Information from these prod -
ucts will be populated and consumed over the entire product’s  
life and not siloed in any single function.
2.1.4 Disposal phase . The disposals phase is an often over -
looked part of the product lifecycle. Interest in the disposal 
phase ebbs and flows primarily with the activity of government  
regulation. When there is high government regulation interest, 
the disposal phase gets a great deal of attention. Conversely when 
there is low regulation activity, this phase gets far less attention.  
(Grieves 2006 , pgs. 106-109)However, there are good business reasons to focus on this phase 
and apply the digital twin concepts. It is in this phase that the 
specific physical twin ceases to exist. There is very little reason 
for the digital twin to suffer the same fate. The cost of main -
taining the digital twin after its physical twin has been disposed  
of is close to zero.
There are two primary reasons for the digital twin in the  
disposal phase. First is that the information needed to dispose of 
the physical twin safely and efficiently would have originated 
in the create phase. This may have been years, decades, or even 
up to a century or more before. Information should reside in 
the digital twin and guide the disposer into disassembling and  
safely disposing of the components, especially toxic ones.
The Circular Economy has a goal of recycling as much of a 
product as possible ( Zeiss et al ., 2020 ). Even if the interest is 
not environmental, the capability to recycle the material of prod -
ucts and reuse them in new products generally makes good  
economic sense.
The second reason is that there may be interesting design and  
product features that exist in this product that has reached its  
physical end of life. Without the continuation of the digital  
twin, this information will be lost with the disposal of the  
physical product. The ability to use this information informs  
future designs and saves the expense and time of “reinventing  
the wheel.” Again, information is a replacement for wasted  
physical resources. Losing the information that might prove  
useful for new products is unfortunate and a cost that could  
be avoided.
2.2 The “digital twin exists ONLY after there is a physical 
product” fallacy
There is a fallacy2 that the digital twin does not exist until 
and unless there is a physical product3. Along with a simple 
lack of understanding the digital twin concept, this may be an  
attempt to over specify the “twin” metaphor.
Metaphors are extremely powerful in invoking complex mental  
constructs and even entire mental spaces in humans. Metaphors  
are not simply comparisons, but generative devices that allow 
rich understandings, new perspectives, and generative ideas 
that open up areas of opportunities that had not previously  
been thought of ( Grieves, 2000 ).
The twin metaphor has only two key attributes: duality and 
strong similarity. With respect to this fallacy discussion, there 
is no metaphorical requirement for timeline simultaneity or 
for the precedence of one type of twin before another type of  
twin. That is, there is no requirement that a twin only exists if 
its counterpart exists simultaneously. Nor is there a requirement

that one type of twin, the physical twin, must exist before there 
is the other type of twin, the digital twin, can also exist. The 
only requirement is that a twin’s counterpart exist at some point 
in the twin’s lifecycle. This means the digital twin can exist 
prior to there being a physical counterpart and can also exist  
after the physical counterpart ceases existence.
The requirement that there must be an actual physical thing 
before there can be a digital twin is simply a wrong perspec -
tive. The key differentiator of whether a digital model and  
associated information is a digital twin is that it is intended  that 
this model become a physical product and that its physical  
counterpart is realized.
It is that intention and the work that goes into the realization of 
that intention that differentiates a digital model from a digital  
twin. If the physical counterpart is never realized, then the  
digital model was never a digital twin. A digital model of a flying  
carpet will never become a digital twin because there is no  
intention, let alone ability, to make it a physical product.
From its inception, the digital twin has always been intended 
to exist in all four phases of the product lifecycle: create, build, 
operate/sustain, and dispose ( Grieves, 2006 ). It is embod -
ied in the saying that “no one goes into a factory, pounds 
on some metal, and hopes an airplane will come out.” A  
tremendous value of the digital twin is that it does exist before  
there is a physical product.
Dispelling these misperceptions of the “twin” metaphor, there 
are five major reasons why the digital twin does not  require a 
physical product before the digital twin exists. These reasons  
are:
•    The DT framework should cover the entire product  
lifecycle
•    The DT is especially valuable during the create phase
•    The DT does exist prior to the physical product –it just  
has a different name
•    The DT regresses to being functionally siloed if there  
is no DT prior to the physical product
•    The DT existing only after there is a physical product  
is conceptually inelegant and piecemeal
The practical reality even for those making a claim that the  
digital twin does not exist prior to there being a physical prod -
uct is that there actually is a digital twin in their organization 
before there is a physical product. This product information  
does exist throughout the entire product lifecycle. In all those 
situations, it simply has a different name. It may be called the 
digital model, the digital design, the digital systems model, 
or some such variation. However, it has most if not all the  
characteristics of the Digital Twin Prototype (DTP).While the digital twin has value across the product lifecycle,  
the DTP is especially valuable in the create phase. It is this 
phase of the lifecycle that work can be moved from the physical 
world into the virtual world. If virtual products can be modelled 
and tested in a virtual environment, replacing physical proto -
types and testing, the potential for a reduction of wasted physical 
resources is substantial. These wasted resources include material,  
energy, and labor time, but also elapsed development  
time.
Even though the create phase may be short in comparison 
to the entire lifecycle of a product that may span decades, 
decisions in this phase have a major impact in determining 
future product costs. Estimates of product cost determination  
during the create phase are as high as 80% ( Iansiti, 1998 ). 
There is an increasing ability to perform virtual testing at a 
fraction of the cost and in less time to replace physical test -
ing. This has the potential to reduce costs, improve quality, and  
reduce time to market.
A major problem with renaming the DTP to something differ -
ent and not having that something in the digital twin framework 
is that it encourages and maintains functional siloing. If this  
different named thing exists in engineering prior to manufactur -
ing having a digital twin of the instance of a physical product,  
then this information will tend not to be shared between  
engineering and manufacturing.
The powerful aspect of the digital twin is that it is product  
centric throughout the entire product lifecycle. Information is  
populated and consumed irrespective of the functional area. 
For the digital twin to exist only after moving to manufacturing 
diminishes greatly its effect. A substantial amount of the infor -
mation for a specific Digital Twin Instance (DTI) is contained  
in the DTP.
Finally, it is inelegant and piecemeal to not have the digital  
twin encompass the entire product lifecycle. As described 
in Section 2 , the intent of the digital twin is to have a frame -
work that persists throughout the entire lifecycle. That has 
been the intent since the origination of the digital twin concept.  
Requiring the digital twin to only exist once there is a physical  
product is inconsistent with that approach. Having different  
types of digital twin, DTP, DTI, and DTA (Digital Twin 
Aggregate), allows us to have a consistent framework, yet  
differentiate how the digital twin manifests itself at different phases  
of product lifecycle.
3 The digital twin and time
One of the least discussed and perhaps most unappreciated 
aspects of the digital twin is its relationship with time. In the 
physical world, we are stuck in the moment. If we want to 
go forward in time, we can. However, we can only do so at the 
pace of each tick of the clock. If we want to go to the past in  
time, we are simply out of luck.

The interest in the ability to predict future events has existed 
for centuries. The famous mathematician, Laplace, said, “Give  
me the positions and velocities of all the particles in the uni -
verse, and I will predict the future” ( Laplace, 1917 )4. However, 
as will be pointed out in a later section, this would apply only to  
the inanimate universe.
In the virtual world, we are under no such time constraint 
and have made great strides in realizing Laplace’s vision. We 
can go back in time via two mechanisms. We can simply run 
our simulations backward from the current time. Simulations 
do not care whether they run forward or backward. The time  
arrow for simulations runs both forward and backward. The  
second mechanism is to have captured the state changes as 
our product has gone through time. We can simply reverse 
those state changes to our current digital twin to reproduce any  
point in the past time.
To move into the future, we simply run our simulations for -
ward. We can pick our increment of time and duration. We can 
set the time as nanoseconds and duration in seconds as we do 
for nuclear explosion simulations or at a little larger time incre -
ments for vehicle crash tests. We can set the time increment in  
years and the duration in decades for determining the durability  
and potential degradation of an airframe.
There are two critical requirements here. The first is that we 
have an understanding of the physics that take place over time 
and second that we have enough computing power in order to 
simulate those physics in the time increment and duration that 
we require. Both these capabilities continue to increase at an  
ever-rapid pace.
Figure 5  illustrates this with the DTP during the create phase. 
T0 represents the point in time when we have a complete dig -
ital twin that could be made into a physical product with full 
functionality. Tx, represent the current time in the product’s  life. Tx+y represents a time in the future of the product’s life.  
We can move back and forth in time from Tx.
Modeling and simulation incorporate the physics and assump -
tions that will drive these simulations. In the create phase, we 
want to virtually create the product, test the product, manufacture  
the product, and sustain and operate the product. We would like 
to model and simulate short periods of time as in a crash test 
and long periods of time as in determining the durability over  
decades of an airframe.
When we get undesirable results, we can step back in time 
with our simulations to see where the deviation between what 
we thought should occur and what actually did occur. We can 
then modify our design so as to obtain the desired system  
outcomes.
The goal in the create phase is to work out all the issues and 
problems with these complex products and only when we get 
it all right, do we want to go and physically make the product.  
The ability to manipulate time is a capability that will allow  
us to better understand our complex systems.
4 Complex systems
We have been referring to complex products or systems, but 
what do we mean when we use that term? In defining what 
a complex system is the first task is to define what we mean 
when we say a system. An accepted definition of the system is  
(Grieves, 2012 )5:
 A system is two or more components that combine together 
to produce from one or more inputs one or more results that  
could not be obtained from the components individually
A system consists of three components: inputs, operations, 
and outputs. Inputs are what drive the system behavior. The 
three main inputs of systems are materials, forces, and infor -
mation. These inputs are processed by the operations of the  
system. The resulting outputs are then used both internally and  
externally to the system.
A taxonomy of systems lists this hierarchy: simple systems,  
complicated systems, system of systems (SoS), complex systems,  
collaborative SoS, adaptive systems, and complex adaptive  
systems ( Baldwin et al ., 2011 ). For purposes of this article, 
we will consider the systems beyond complicated systems as  
the class of complex systems.
Thus, there are three types of systems: simple, complicated, and 
complex. Simple and complicated systems are closely related 
and differ only in the matter of scope. For simple systems,

the inputs, operations, and outputs are readily discernable. 
There is little or no information needed by a simple system. The  
system states are few. A lever and fulcrum constitute a simple

Complicated systems have those same characteristics. The 
major difference is that there are more components that inter -
act. The inputs, operations, and outputs may not be easily  
discernable, but the system can be broken down into discrete  
subsystems. These subsystems can be readily understood. The 
interface between subsystems are few and simple. There are  
more system states, but again they are easily discernable.
Complicated systems have little if any information as inputs. 
What information is entered into the system is entered by 
physical material changes – flipping a switch, pulling a lever, 
etc. Mechanical systems and complicated systems have a 
high, if not complete, overlap. A watch is an example of a  
complicated system.
There is not very good agreement as to what constitutes  
complexity ( Ledford, 2008 ). While complicated systems are an  
extension of simple systems, complex systems are something 
very different. Complexity seems to come into play when there 
is not a good grasp of the number of states that the system can 
take and/or the number of systems states is too numerable  
to calculate.
Like complicated systems, a key aspect of complex systems 
is that there are numerous components in complex systems.  
However, there are also multiple interface points between  
components and many to many communication channels  
(Mitchell, 2009 ). Complexity is also linked to system behav -
iors with systems having more possible behaviors being more  
complex than systems with less possible behaviors ( Siegenfeld  
& Bar-Yam, 2019 ).
It is also proposed that complexity is all about the informa -
tion processing that differentiates complex systems from  
simple and complicated systems. The information inputs, both 
into the system and within the system drive system complexity  
(Stacey et al ., 2017 ). Sophisticated information processing by  
the system also makes predictions of system states difficult.
Complexity drives the need for the digital twin. While not 
clear cut, at a certain level of possible systems states and infor -
mation exchange and processing, products are considered 
complex. As I have often said, a jet fighter needs a digital  
twin. A paperclip does not.
5 Complex systems and emergent behavior
The complex systems of today are said to have emergent behav -
ior ( Grieves & Vickers, 2017 ). However, that is not entirely 
accurate. Emergent behavior is considered to be behavior that 
the system did not have initially but emerges over time as the 
system operates. The reality is that, in most man-made systems, 
new behavior did not emerge. It has been in the system from  
its inception. What actually has emerged is the understanding  that the system could perform in ways that were not foreseen  
by the product developers.
The requirement that a system have actual emergent behav -
ior occurs under one of two conditions. The first condition is 
common. It involves a human interaction with the system that 
changes something in that system to affect its behavior. How -
ever, if we view the human as external to the system itself, 
then again this is not really emergent behavior. If a human  
interacts with the system in a way that the system design -
ers never anticipated or if the human makes a change to the  
system, then the system would have always acted in that  
manner. The behavior wasn’t so much as emergent as it was a  
deterministic outcome of the human behavior.
Where a system exhibits actual emergent behavior is when deci -
sions are taken, or parameters are adjusted that have not been 
designed into the system. At one end of the spectrum, these  
decisions could be completely random, as in generating random  
numbers to put together different processes. At the other  
end of the spectrum is Artificial Intelligence (“AI”) which in 
effect is to put humanlike intelligence (goal seeking) into the  
system to make better decisions to effect outcomes.
Systems with this kind of randomness will be the actual 
emergent systems. However only systems with AI will have  
emergent behavior that is goal seeking.
6 AI and M&S (Artificial Intelligence and Modeling 
and Simulation)
AI and M&S are two sides of the same digital coin. AI con -
sists of computers replicating only human intelligence. M&S 
consists of computers replicating the rest of the physical uni -
verse, except  for human intelligence. Together AI and M&S 
can provide a form of intelligence for digital twins in a virtual  
world.
Both of these capabilities rely heavily on the advances in  
computing. Since 1970, computing performance which is  
proxied by the number of transistors on a chip has been increas -
ing exponentially as predicted by Moore’s Law ( Moore, 1965 ). 
As shown in the logarithmic graph in Figure 6  computing  
performance has been doubling approximately every 18 
months. In 2020, we have reached 54 billion transistor den -
sity. If we continue to keep at this pace, by 2030 we will reach  
approximately six trillion transistors or an equivalent capability.
It is difficult to comprehend what this level of computing 
capability will mean. However, we can be pretty confident  
that the ideas presented here will not be compute bound.
6.1 AI or Artificial Intelligence
The term Artificial Intelligence or AI was coined in the 1960s 
by John McCarthy of MIT for the initiative of creating “thinking  
computers” ( Moravec, 1988 ). The term AI is in pretty liberal

I define intelligence as goal seeking while minimizing resources. 
Nature does goal seeking in survival of the fittest. However, 
nature does not minimize resource. Nature attempts to produce 
all possible combinations and allows the environment to select 
the fittest, eliminating all the rest. Nature can take eons of time 
and nearly infinite resources to accomplish this. Humans do  
not have that luxury.
6.1.1 The state of AI . The idea that computers might repli -
cate human intelligence has been around since at least the mid -
dle of the last century. The most famous example of this is 
the Turing test, which was proposed by Alan Turing in 1950 
(Turing, 1950 ). Basically, the test proposes that if a human 
observer cannot tell the difference between another human 
being and a computer by asking a series of questions, the Turing  
Test is passed.
The Turing test has been deemed not rigorous enough. It relies 
on an observer who might rate high on the ability to be deceived, 
so other tests have been proposed. The Winograd Schema 
Challenge ( Levesque et al ., 2012 ) is a test that asks ambigu -
ous questions. This relies on human common sense that the  deep-learning systems of today find difficult if not impossible to 
reproduce.
Take for example the simple statement, “the cat sat on the 
mat, and it yawned.” Ask any human of even a very young age,  
and we will immediately get the correct answer. A computer  
currently finds this kind of question very difficult, requiring  
both extensive ontological and deep learning programming.
In addition, there is a question as to whether we really want 
computers to replicate human intelligence. In fact, there is a 
serious question as to whether computers can replicate human 
intelligence. What often is overlooked is that the human  
brain and computers have very different hardware.
Computer hardware is implemented in digital circuitry. The 
human brain is implemented in interconnected neurons, with 
specialized areas for different mental functions ( Seung, 2012 ). 
A cognitive science area theorizes that the way humans think is 
hugely shaped by its embedded connections with human sensory  
and motor processes ( Ramachandran, 2011 ).
The intent here is not to have a comprehensive discussion of 
what does or does not constitute artificial intelligence, but to  
discuss elements that make a digital twin appear to be intelligent.

Intelligence is for our purposes understanding context and

It may be that AI does not replicate human intelligence, but 
implements goal seeking and problem solving in ways that do 
not replace humans but augments them. The evidence for this is

numbers, is difficult for almost all humans. Tasks that are easy 
for humans, captcha recognition and ambiguous sentences like  
the one above, are difficult for computers ( Tegmark, 2017 ).
However, the good news is that the actual requirement of AI 
for the IDT is not to have human like intelligence. We have 
plenty of humans. It is to augment human intelligence by 
processing massive amounts of data and presenting relevant 
information for consideration. As will be discussed next, the  
digital twin has from its inception proposed that augmenting,  
not replacing, human intelligence was a core characteristic.
6.1.2 Cued availability . From the earliest stages of the  
digital twin concept, it was proposed that digital twins under -
stand context and provide proactive assistance to the users of 
the digital twin. The characteristics of the digital twin from 
its inception were: singularity, correspondence, traceability,  
reflectiveness, and cued availability ( Grieves, 2006 ).
As a brief refresher, singularity means that there is a  
single representation of product information and not duplicate,  
inconsistent versions. Correspondence means that products  
are both physical and virtual. Cohesion means that all repre -
sentations or views of facets of the product information must  
adhere to the laws of physics the same way their atom-based 
physical twin does. Traceability means that the digital twin 
tracks the history of the product through the phases of the  
lifecycle. Reflectiveness means that, as a physical twin changes, 
its DTI changes. All of these characteristics are currently  
part of the digital twin.
What has not been an aspect of the digital twin is the last char -
acteristic, cued availability. This is where the IDT becomes  
relevant. While not the most elegant term, cued availability 
was the concept that the digital twin would provide information  
to the user based on the task that they were doing.
Cued availability is simply being able to have the “right infor -
mation and processes when we need it” ( Grieves, 2006 ). The 
digital twin would be aware of the information of what the 
user was doing and provide additional information based on  
that context.
6.2 M&S or modeling and simulation
With apologies to Alan Turing, the critical requirement is to 
have the computer emulate everything in the universe, except  
human intelligence. If, as noted above, we can fully simulate  
the inanimate universe but only obtain assistance from AI, 
we will obtain tremendous value for products throughout  
the product lifecycle.Modeling and simulation is about representing physical  
products and their behaviors in a digital or virtual environ -
ment. In this context, a model is a static representation of the  
physical product. The current technology allows for this  
model to be a three-dimensional replication that has complete 
fidelity in terms of dimensioning. The behavior is modeled in  
mathematical form, describing the forces acting on the physical  
product and the forces the physical product generates and acts  
upon the environment.
Simulation is dynamic. Simulation adds the component of time 
and describes how the product changes as forces act on it, and 
how the forces the product generates act on the environment. 
Simulation shows the changes in geometry as the mathematical  
behavior model of forces transforms material. A simulation 
of a vehicle crash test shows at a user defined time scale the 
deformation of all components of the automobile as it crashes  
into a barrier.
Simulation relies on two things: an increasing knowledge of 
the physics and/or data that determine the physical environ -
ment and the computing power to calculate the physics at the 
required scale and fidelity. This has meant that there have been 
limitations of the products that could be modelled and simulated.  
A few decades ago, only simple product systems could be 
simulated. With the doubling of Moore’s Law over time,  
today even complex product systems can be simulated.
The question is how do we decide how well we are doing with 
M&S? Over a decade and a half ago, I proposed some Tests 
of Virtuality to answer that question. The Tests of Virtuality  
were modeled on the Turing Test ( Grieves, 2006 , pgs. 18–19).
The original formulation of the Grieves Test of Virtuality had 
three distinct tests: a visual test, a performance test, and a reflec -
tion test. The format of the tests was similar. An observer was 
exposed to the physical and the virtual versions. If he or she  
could not tell the difference between the two versions, then the test 
was passed.
In the visual test, the observer looked at the video screens 
of a product placed in a physical room and the digital twin 
version. The observer could ask for any spatial manipula -
tion to be done. The observer could ask to see the product  
from any angle.
The observer could ask that the product be disassembled and 
look at any individual component. In the example of a car, 
the observer could ask that the doors be opened to look inside 
at the interior or that the hood be opened and to look in the 
engine compartment. If the observer cannot tell the difference  
between the physical version and the digital twin version, then 
Grieves Visual Test is said to be passed.
The behavioral test was a little more difficult. The observer had 
the same two views of the product, the physical and the digital  
twin one. The observer could then ask that forces be generated  
and/or applied to both the physical and virtual and observe 
Page 10 of 24Digital Twin 2022, 2:8 Last updated: 13 NOV 2024

==== Page 11 ====
the results. For an airplane, this could mean that the two planes 
jet engine would be turned on, and the planes sent down 
the physical and virtual runways to take off. This would be  
an internal force.
The observer could also ask that once the planes were flying  
that they be put into a steep dive and see the forces that 
acted upon them. That would be an external force test. If the 
observer could not tell the difference between the physical 
and the virtual performance, then it passed the Grieves Test of  
Performance.
The third test is a test of reflectivity. Reflectivity was defined 
as any change to the physical product would be reflected in 
its digital twin. Again, we have the observer and the physi -
cal twin and digital twin versions. In this particular case, the 
test is that the observer can see no differences between the two  
versions. Using an example of an oil rig, if the observer were 
to compare every valve setting, every gauge, every pump 
serial number, there would be no difference between the 
two versions. If that was the case, then the Grieves Test of  
Reflectivity would be passed. 
These tests were meant to be ideal test. They were always 
meant to be tied to use cases that would provide value to the 
user. It was intended that only those things that provided value 
would be the things that were intended to be tested. If there 
was no value in disassembling a product and determining if the  
physical twin and digital twin versions were identical, then 
that would not be part of the test. If a component of the oil 
rig above was serialized, but there was no interest in track -
ing serial numbers, then the physical twin and digital twin  
versions not being identical would be irrelevant.
So where are we a decade and a half later? The short answer is 
that these test of virtuality are easily passed on a daily basis. A 
decade and half ago, we were close to passing the visual test. 
Today there is no question that the visualization of physical 
products and its digital twins have the fidelity and granularity  
that we need for most use cases.
The behavior and reflectivity tests were proposed before  
Internet of Things (IoT) became so prevalent. Back then, the 
issue for these tests was going to be being able to get the appro -
priate sensoring data in order to maintain the digital twin  
version. Today with our smart products, we routinely get the  
sensoring information that we need in order to pass both 
those tests. Again, we need to remember that this is driven by 
use cases. The value to the user needs to be there in order to 
expend the resources necessary to maintaining the digital twin  
for all these tests.
At this time, I would like to propose a new Grieves Test of  
Virtuality. This is Grieves Test of Prediction. This is a slightly 
different test and much harder. In this test version, the observer 
asks that the digital twin version be moved a certain amount 
of time into the future. The observer then waits that amount of  
time. When that time has elapsed, the observer now  compares the two versions. If the states of the physical twin and 
digital twin are effectively identical, then the Grieves Test of  
Prediction is passed.
6.3 Merging AI with M&S
The natural habitat of AI is in the virtual world. It is much  
better suited in dealing with the information of the virtual world 
than with the tangible structures and environment of the physical  
world. In dealing with the physical world, AI is dependent  
on sensors to capture the physical world state changes in  
context and relies on physical actuators to cause changes in  
tangible structures.
To provide a simple example, a pilot of an airplane sitting on a 
taxiway waiting for take-off can, at a glance, assess the situa -
tion they are in: the state of the runway, the position of other 
aircraft, the state of the engines, the weather conditions, the 
trustworthiness of air control, and numerous other factors. The  
sensoring and programming required to have an AI system 
have all that same information and replace the pilot completely  
is a daunting if not insurmountable task.
That is not to say that AI does not have a role in assisting the 
pilot, even on mundane taxiways. It does. With the informa -
tion and assessments AI can do, it could prevent disasters such 
as the 1977 Tenerife 747 accident that was the result of a tragic 
miscalculation by the pilot ( Weick, 1990 ). The AI would have 
alerted the pilot that a crash was unavoidable in time for the  
takeoff to be aborted.
Where AI is the most at home is in the virtual world of M&S. 
In fact, it acts more like nature than like humans. In the  
virtual world, AI is goal seeking but with a much-relaxed 
requirement to minimize resources. As computing capability  
continues its exponential growth, the capability of simulating  
more and more possible combinations and evaluating their 
outcomes becomes faster and less expensive. Crash testing  
a vehicle physically is an expensive proposition. Crash test -
ing a virtual vehicle gets less and less expensive over time 
because of Moore’s Law. This means that more and more testing  
variations can be done for minimal costs.
7 Intelligent Digital Twins
The digital twin has traditionally had a characteristic of  
passiveness. The digital twin has been described as a reposi -
tory of product information. Information is populated in the 
digital twin and consumed from it. The digital twin could be 
interrogated by its users to use information in order to replace  
wasted physical resources.
The Intelligent Digital Twin (IDT) has these characteristics:
•    Active
•    Online
•    Goal seeking
•    Anticipatory

7.1 Active
A key characteristic of the IDT is that it is active. A tacit 
understanding of the current digital twin is that it is passive. 
It is usually referred to as a repository of product informa -
tion. To obtain that information, users inquire or search for  
information that they need. While predictions are part of the 
current digital twin, it is usually in the framework of users  
initiating the request for specific predictions of their choosing.
The IDT actively assists working with and providing informa -
tion as it is needed. As discussed previously, the intent is to  
not replace humans and human intelligence. It is to augment it.
7.2 Online
Referring back to the digital twin model in Figure 1 , one of 
the three elements is the communication links between the  
digital twin and its environment and the physical twin and its  
environment. The communication goes both ways. We feed 
data from the physical side to the virtual side and information  
goes from the virtual side to the physical side.
The frequency of the communications between the physi -
cal and virtual is rarely mentioned. When it has been, it is tied 
to a particular use case. Air bags deploying require instant  
communication. Maintenance updates could be batched and  
could lag for days.
However, even the instant air bag deployment communication  
is passive. It requires that data be sent from the physical  
airbag system to its digital twin in order for some action to 
occur. The communications between the digital twin and its 
physical counterpart is offline until the physical counterpart  
performs some action.
For the digital twin to be active requires that it be online. What 
this means is that the DT continues to scan the PT and its  
environment. The primary initialization moves from the physi -
cal side of the DT model to the active DT side. Depend -
ing on the phase of the lifecycle, the physical side includes 
the environment, the physical twin, and the humans involved. 
The DT evolves from a passive repository to a proactive  
repository and constant agent assistance. 
7.3 Goal seeking
In the current digital twin model, humans provide all the goal 
seeking. With the IDT, goal seeking is shared between it and 
its human users. This means that a key aspect will be the crea -
tion and articulation of goals throughout the phases of the  
product lifecycle.
There will need to be defined goals for the different phases. For 
the creation phase, the goals are related to developing products  
that meet the defined requirements. In the build phase, the 
goals are producing these products using the minimum 
amount of physical resources, i.e., time, energy, and materials.  
In the operate/sustain phase, the goals are the continued  
operations with minimum downtime and performance that meets the requirements set out in the create phase. The disposal  
phase goals are the safe and effective disposal and recycling.
The intent of the IDT is to assist and augment the human in 
reaching the goals that have always been present. It is not to  
change the goals.
7.4 Anticipatory
The IDT is anticipatory. This is something that humans instinc -
tively do, although sometimes not very successfully. As goal 
seeking/resource minimizing agents, humans attempt to look 
ahead in time and adjust their current actions to obtain their 
future goals. A common example of that is driving. Humans  
continually evaluate the surrounding vehicles and adjust their 
direction and velocity to avoid collisions. That humans do not  
do a wonderful job is evidenced by the frequency of accidents.
The IDT could be of major assistance to humans in at least two 
major areas: performing complex calculations to accurately 
model and simulate physical events and preventing human  
biases from tainting decision making.
I have in previous works discussed this type of capability  
and given it the name of Front Running Simulation (FRS) 
(Grieves, 2019 ). FRS attempts to anticipate the future and run 
out “in front” of the physical twin. FRS is a constant running  
simulation using the digital twin of complex products.
We have discussed the first characteristic of FRS at length in 
the section on M&S. A core characteristic of the digital twin 
is the ability to virtually model and simulate physical events. 
It relies on the previously discussed digital twin’s ability to  
manipulate time.
For the anticipatory IDT, what we would like is the IDT to 
be constantly running simulations of the product as the prod -
uct performs its duties. Figure 7  takes Figure 5  and adds the  
aspect of the physical twin. At every new defined T0, the physical 
twin feeds its new, current state information into the IDT.
The IDT would then run a new simulation, utilizing not only 
the history and current state of this physical twin but also infor -
mation from the Digital Twin Aggregate (DTA) of all products  
in this class. The IDT would run this new simulation from

that point in time into the future to determine how the product

This would allow the IDT to predict future adverse events that

With the exponential advances of computing power, the IDT 
could run multiple scenarios and use Bayesian probabilities 
to give probabilities of adverse events, allowing the human  
to assess the risk.
The second characteristic of FRS is to prevent human biases 
from tainting and even preventing good decisions. Humans 
have two biases that often lead to disastrous decision. These 
biases are the confirmation bias and the optimism bias  
(Kahneman, 2011 ).
Confirmation bias is the human tendency to only value infor -
mation that confirms the perception that a person already has.  
Optimism bias is the tendency to view optimistic outcomes as 
having a much higher probability than they actually have. Exam -
ples abound of disasters that have resulted from these biases, the 
Tenerife accident discussed above, the Air France 447 accident  
off of Brazil ( Palmer, 2013 ), the BP Gulf Disaster ( Graham  
et al., 2011 ), and many others ( Perrow, 1984 ).
On that basis, the IDT can provide valuable assistance to 
humans. The IDT can process all the data and information avail -
able to it in an accurate and unbiased manner. It can explore a 
wide variety of scenarios by modifying parameters, calculating  
probabilities, and providing humans with estimates of outcomes 
based on current conditions and actions.
7.5 Intelligent digital twins throughout the lifecycle
The digital twin’s value is always driven by use cases. The  
question always needs to be: can the information from the  
digital twin replace wasted physical resources of time, energy, 
and material? If it can, and the cost of collecting, processing,  
storing, and retrieving the information is less than the cost of 
wasting material resources, then the information should be  
created and used.
Figure 8  shows common digital twin use cases organized by 
product lifecycle phase. This is by no means a comprehen -
sive list, but it shows a scope of the usage of digital twins in  
today’s modern product organization.
Our intent is to move as much work from the physical world 
to the virtual world as we can and as makes economic sense.  
Virtual bits will continue get become cheaper at an exponential  
rate. Physical atoms will become more expensive, if only at  
the rate of inflation.
7.5.1 Create phase . The create phase is obviously the most 
important part of product development. It is in this phase 
that we start the life of a new product. Theoretically there is  
nothing that exists at the beginning of this phase. There is no  
digital twin and certainly no physical twin.The reality is that we rarely start from a blank sheet of paper 
for a revolutionary new product. The vast majority of the 
time we start with some components that are already in exist -
ence and that may be repurposed for the new product. A great 
deal of the time we are developing the next generation of a  
previous product.
If as stated previously, we want to create the product  
virtually, test the product virtually, manufacture the product  
virtually, and support and sustain the product virtually, then 
the create phase is about using as much virtual capability as we  
can. It is in this create phase that the IDT, linking AI and M&S,  
can provide tremendous value.
Our first order of business is to create the requirements that 
we want this product to have. These requirements will drive the 
development of the DTP that will provide the foundation for 
the physical products to come. This DTP is defined as all the  
products that we can make, with all its variants.
Once we have our requirements, which in essence is the new 
product system, this forms the basis for our new DTP. We use 
the tools of the Product Lifecycle Management system (PLM) 
that share the characteristics of our IDT. We want this capabil -
ity to be active, online, goal seeking, and anticipatory. We can  
almost think of this as an uber digital twin.
This is where cued availability comes in the play, with an active 
IDT looking to assist the product developers. It is active. As 
a developer works on a new product, from requirements to 
full product design and manufacturing realization, the IDT  
can assist him or her. The IDT can monitor the work and 
present the designer with material that he or she may not even  
know that they need. This requires constant online access.
The IDT can assess shapes and present exemplars of things 
already designed in order to assist. It can look for designs Figure 8.  Digital twin use cases.

that have been previously designed and present those designs,

designs using the requirements in order to make early deter -

early warnings when that is not the case.
The IDT needs to be anticipatory. The IDT can look for inter -

to eliminate problems of parts not fitting together. It can run 
early simulations of related components to ensure that they are 
interfacing and working the way they are intended. Looking  
at the list of use cases, there are always use cases that would

Goal seeking at this stage is driven by the requirements. Our 
goal is to produce a new product that meets the characteris -

doing so. We test that by M&S capabilities that check to see 
if the requirements are met as we go through the test phase and  
model and simulate operation / sustainment performance.
In this phase, we not only need to create a product that meets 
the design requirements, but we also need to create a produc -
tion process that will produce a physical representation of 
our digital product. We need to do so efficiently and effec -
tively. This means that we will need a digital twin of the factory  
production process in order to determine that this production 
process will result in the physical product we desire. Again, 
M&S of the factory will allow us to determine that our pro -
duction process will produce the physical twins effectively  
and efficiently.
7.5.2 Build phase . In the build phase, the focus is on  
creating a physical twin that as closely as possible replicates a  
variant of the DTP . It is in this phase that we also want to create  
the DTI of the specific physical twin that we manufacture. We would like this manufacturing process to as closely as possible 
duplicate the M&S manufacturing process that we developed  
in the create phase.
As shown in Table 1 , manufacturing facilities exist on a  
spectrum from simple to complex. On the simple end of the 
spectrum, a single machine may be the manufacturing proc -
ess. It may not need a DT, let alone an IDT. On the complex 
end of the spectrum, where we have a large number of machines  
performing different processes, we need DTs of the machines 
organized in a consistent system.
In the latter case, the IDT that we want to employ is the IDT of 
the factory itself. We want that IDT of the factory to be active 
and online. We want the IDT to be constantly monitoring the 
factory floor for issues that will impact either the productivity  
or the quality of the products that we are manufacturing.
The concept of factory replication is that information about 
our factory is always instantaneously and simultaneously avail -
able to any authorized user ( Grieves, 2014 ). Factory replication 
requires that the IDT be online at all times. The goal seeking  
that we want is producing quality parts as efficiently as  
possible. The IDT, through cued availability, would be  
suggesting improvements in our processes as it determined 
how we actually produce the product versus how we thought we  
were going to produce the product.
The anticipatory aspect comes into play with FRS. By con -
stantly simulating the future state of the factory, we can predict 
issues such as bottlenecks before they occur and take remedial 
action to prevent those issues. With the right information, we can 
do that at this macro level for the factory and at the micro level  
for individual equipment. We want to be able to move 
from periodic maintenance based on MTBF to prognostic  
maintenance based on performance indicators. This 
means that we would fix problems before they occur as  
opposed to performing maintenance on a periodic basis.

This is where the digital twin differs from Industry 4.0. Indus -

event to its remediation ( Schuh et al ., 2016 ). If we can collect  
sufficient information of our factory equipment, we should 
be able to predict failures and fix them before they occur

The other anticipatory aspect is to be looking for weak  
signals or anomalies. These are signals that are often ignored by 
humans, but an indication that something is going wrong ( Weick 
& Sutcliffe, 2007 ). The IDT would process this information  
taking into account that looking for weak signals is important,

7.5.3 Operate/sustain . In the operation/sustainment phase, there 
are numerous opportunities for the IDT. The complex products 
of today, modern jet fighters, massive drilling platforms, and 
sophisticated medical devices, are beyond human processing 
capabilities in order to understand exactly what these products 
are doing. The IDT in this phase needs to be active in looking  
for issues of performance, comparing them to the perform -
ance expected, and either adjusting software to compensate 
for the degradation, alerting humans to the issue early on, or 
at least identifying the problem for future generations of the 
product. At a minimum, we want to close the loop between the  
operations/sustainment phase and the creation phase. We do this 
by feeding the data of the actual performance of the product  
to the designers and engineers who create these products.
While the classical digital twin could collect a great deal of this 
information, it was on a periodic basis, with potential long lag 
times between collecting the data and transmitting that infor -
mation to its digital twin. The online aspect means that we  
would be collecting information on a constant basis.
Goal seeking is built in the requirements for the product, so the 
intent of the IDT would be to continually to match the actual 
performance with requirements and make whatever adjust -
ments were required to obtain the required performance. This 
would also mean that the IDT would learn as the product  
matured in life so that it could compensate for degradation. It 
would then feed that information to the Digital Twin Aggregate  
so that it would be propagated to other DTIs.
The emergent aspect of complex systems due to AI being incor -
porated into these products make IDTs critical. The initial 
product designers of these systems are by definition unable to predict all the system responses. The IDT will be needed to  
provide overwatch capability for these emergent systems. As 
the AI changes parameters of the system, these changes need to  
be transmitted to its DTI. At a minimum, the DTI will have a 
record of the changes made to analyze if something goes wrong, 
and the physical twin is damaged or destroyed. In the ideal situ -
ation, the intelligent DTI will predict the adverse condition  
and intervene to prevent it from happening.
8 Conclusions
Digital twins have emerged as a top technological initiative 
in the 21st century. The ability to move work from the physi -
cal world into the virtual world will enable products to be 
faster, better, and cheaper6. The proliferation of products that 
are more complex with emergent capabilities will require the 
information capabilities of digital twins throughout the entire  
product lifecycle. Digital twins exist from the moment there 
is an intended  product since the information about a product

Digital twins will need to evolve in order to meet the challenge

will move from being an information repository to providing

Specifically, in section 3, where the author introduces a quote attributed to Pierre Simon, the 
Marquis de Laplace. I could not find any translation of that actual statement in the 1902 version of 
the essay  included as a link in the reference section. A google search included several instances of 
that quote being attributed to "Pierre Simon Laplace", but none met the standard of academic 
credibility expected for a journal article. My suggestion would be to change the statement as 
follows: "The famous mathematician, Pierre Simon de Laplace, has been popularly paraphrased as 
saying 'Give me the positions...' (Laplace, 1902)". Then include a footnote that states this adage is 
most likely a popularization of the statement regarding "an intelligence sufficiently vast to submit 
these data to analysis" (Laplace, 1902, p. 4) in the first edition English translation of the 6th edition 
of PSL's essays published in French during the 19th Century (circa 1814). At a minimum, I suggest 
a correction of the embedded link for this reference to point to the 2nd Edition English Translation 
(1917) or change the reference to the 1st Edition English translation (1902).   
 
In a couple instances in section 6, the author indicates present tense when referring to activity
