Abstract  4 
Resilience i ndicators are a convenient tool  to assess the resilience of engine ering systems. T hey are often used 5 
in preliminary design s or in the assessment of complex systems. This paper introduces a novel approach to 6 
assess the time -dependent resilience of engineering systems using resilience indicators. A  Bayesian network  7 
(BN)  approach is employed  to handle the relationships among the indicators. BN is known for its capability of 8 
handling causal dependencies between  different variables  in probabilistic t erms . However, the use of BN is 9 
limited to static systems that are in a state of  equilibrium. Being at equilibrium is  often not the case because 10 
most engineering systems are dynamic  in nature  as their performance fluctuates with time, especially after 11 
disturbing events (e.g. natural disasters). Therefore, the temporal dimension  is tackled in this work using the 12 
Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN). DBN extends the classical BN by adding the time dimension. It permits  13 
the interaction among variables at different time steps. It can be used to track the evolution of a system ’s 14 
performance given evidence recorded at a previous time step. This allows  predicting the resilience state of a 15 
system given its initial condition. A mathematical probabil istic framework based on the DBN is developed to 16 
model the resilience of dynamic engineering systems.  Two illustrative examples  are presented  in the paper  to 17 
demonstrate  the applicability of the introduced framework.  One example  evaluates the resilience of  Brazil. The 18 
other one evaluates the resilience of a transportation system.  19

2 1. Introduction  22 
Research on disaster resilience has recently been fostered  due to the noticeable increase in the  number of  natural 23 
and human -caused disasters. Resilience has been  defined differently depending o n the field of study  (Doorn et 24 
al. 2018 ; Hosseini et al. 2016b ; Manyena 2006 ). In engineering, resilience  is the ability to withstand a 25 
disturbance caused by an external agent and recover quickly if damage occurs  (Cimellaro et al. 2016 ). Current 26 
scientific  contribution s are  aimed at understanding disaster resilience and find ing new wa ys to measure it, 27 
quantitatively  or qualitatively . Several solutions exist in the literature  to measure  resilience at the  community 28 
level (Cutter et al. 2014 ; Kammouh and Cimellaro 2018b ; Kammouh et al. 2018c ) and at the  infrastructure level 29 
(Gardoni 20 18; Liu et al. 2017 ; Sharma et al. 2018 ); however, r esearch on  resilience  quantification  is still in the 30 
early stage  of development  and n o universal  method exists so far. Among others,  resilience indicators are an 31 
effective tool to compute the resilience of engineering systems because they allow modeling complex systems  32 
easily and effectively  (Cutter et al. 2008 ). Several authors have proposed  indicator -based methodolo gies to 33 
evaluate the resilie nce of engineering systems ; some are deterministic  (Henry and Ramirez -Marquez 2012 ; 34 
Kammouh et al. 2017b ; Kammouh et al. 2019b ) and the others are probabilistic (Cockburn and Tesfamariam 35 
2012 ; De Iuliis et al. 2019b ; Kammouh et al. 2018b ; Kammouh et al. 2018d ; Shahriar et al. 2012 ). Nevertheless , 36 
developing a  standardized indicator -based methodology  to quantify resilience is still challenging.  The m ain 37 
challenge  is the presence of uncertainty in the resilience model  and its input s. The interdependency and the 38 
weighting factors  distribution among the variables are also other issue s that cannot be handled in a deterministic 39 
way. Therefore, probabilistic methods are usually preferred  over deterministic methods . Probabilistic models 40 
are more powerful to model uncertainties and interdependencies  and they are more ap propriate to represent 41 
reality. In fact,  deterministic models are considered a particular case of probabilistic model s (Pourret et al. 42 
2008 ).  43 
While probabilistic models solve the gap of modeling capability that deterministic models suffer from, not all 44 
probabilistic approaches are suitable to model the behavior of engineering systems, especially in cases where 45 
past data is not  readily  available  (Cimellaro 2016 ). One way to properly model the behavior of a system in a 46 
probabilistic manner  is through the use of Fuzzy analysis and Bayesian Networks (BNs).  Fuzzy theory  47 
represents a method for solving problems that are related to uncertainty and vagueness . It has been implemented 48 
in some  research works to study the res ilience of infrastructure systems (De Iuliis et al. 2019a ; Kammouh et al. 49 
2018e ; Kammouh et al. 2018f ). Fuzzy theory is  usually  implemented in cases with no data , which  is replaced 50 
with expert judgments. The output of fuzzy -based methods is heavily subjective  given that they are based on 51 
opinions rather than data . On the other hand, BN is a Directed Acyclic Graph where the nodes represent 52 
variables of interest and the links between them indicate causal dependencies. BNs are widely used for 53 
knowledge representation and reasoning under uncertainty , especially in the context of partial information . They 54 
are effective  when different types of variables and knowledge from various sources need to be integrated within 55 
a single framework. In addition , BN provides probabilistic relationships among the variables, which allows 56 
mode ling the interdependencies  among them . In the literature, there is a limited number of research 57 
contributions that use the BN in resilience analysis. Johansen and Tien (2018)  proposed a probabilistic 58

3 methodology based on the BN approach to model the interdependencies between critical infrastructure system s. 59 
Their research aims at understanding the effect of interdependencies on the fragility of the overall system.  Cai 60 
et al. (2017)  developed a universal resilience metric  for infrast ructure systems. A  BN approach was employed 61 
to calculate the resilience metric value. The proposed resilience metric can be used to design and /or optimize 62 
different types of engineering systems  against various hazards, such as earthquakes, floods, cyber -attacks 63 
(Kammouh and Cimellaro 2018a ), etc . Moreover, Hosseini and Barke r (2016)  introduced a resilience 64 
quantification methodology  using BN . In their research,  resilience is defined using three parameters: absorptive 65 
capacity, adaptive capacity, and restorative capacities. A case study on an inland waterway port, an essent ial 66 
component in the intermodal transportation network , was used to demonstrate the methodology.  Hosseini et al. 67 
(2016a)  employed a BN  to quantify the supply chain system resilience of sulfuric acid manufacturer s in Iran. 68 
Their work consists of a formulation for supplier selection, that acco unts for operational (e.g., customer demand) 69 
and disruption (e.g., natural disaster) risks and their effect on resilient suppliers . (Hossain et al. 2019b ) applied 70 
Bayesian Network to rank ports infrastructure assets. Their study proposes a formulation for supplier selection, 71 
accounting for operational (e.g., customer demand) and disruption (e.g., natural disaster) risks and their effect 72 
on resilient suppliers . (Hossain et al. 2019b ) introduced a BN approach to rank port infrastructure assets. 73 
(Eldosouky et al. 2017 ) introduced a novel analytical resilience index to measure the effect of each critical 74 
infrastructure ’s physical component  on its probability of failure  using Bayesian Network . The proposed 75 
framework was applied to  a case study of hydropower dams and their interdependence on the power grid. Finally, 76 
(Hossain et al. 2019a ) address ed a range of possible risks to the electrical power system and its interdependent 77 
networks (EIN)  using the BN. 78 
Rather than the static approaches, Tabandeh et al. (2018)  developed a n indicator -based  probabilistic formulation 79 
to model the societal impact and estimate the impact considering the immediate consequences and the recovery 80 
condition.  The methodology uses DBN  to integrate the predictive model of the indicators. BNs have  also been 81 
employed by r esearchers in fields other than disaster resilience  (Cockburn and Tesfamariam 2012 ; Ismail et al. 82 
2011 ; Kabir et al. 2016 ; Kabir et al. 2015 ; Kammouh et al. 2019a ; Siraj et al. 2015 ). For example, Cockburn 83 
and Tesfamariam (2012)  used BNs to estimate the risk of several cities located in Canada , Kabir et al. (2015)  84 
evaluated the risk of water mains failure using a BN model, while Siraj et al. (2015)  employed BNs in the 85 
seismic risk assessmen t of high voltage transformers.  86 
Resilience can be an outcome (static)  or a process (dynamic)  (Cutter et al. 2008 ). While most of the 87 
abovementioned research es focused on  analyzing engineering resilience from a static point of view , there is a 88 
significant gap in assessing  the dynamic nature of resilience  through q uantitative approaches.  In fact,  BN does 89 
not capture the behavior of dynamic system s and the interdependencies among  the system ’s components and 90 
variables. BN is a snapshot of the system, which implies that the restoration process, which is inherently time - 91 
dependent, cannot be modeled.  Moreover , a feedback loop is not allowed in a BN model ; thus, it cannot be used 92 
to model a cyclic relationship. Although  there are some research works that use the DBN as an inference tool 93 
to express resilience in a dynamic manner, the resilience model s adopted in those researches and the transitional 94 
model from  one step to another in the time -space was not clearly defined.   95

4 This paper first proposes a static framework  to model systems of static nature (e.g.,  assessing a system’s 96 
performance at a specific instance of time ). It employs the BN as a tool to quantify the system’s resilience. The 97 
framework is demonstrated using a case study in which the resilience of a country, namely Brazil, is assessed. 98 
In general, BN is a good  tool to assess a physical -causal model; h owever, learning and updating a BN requires 99 
an extensive computational load . Updating a BN is necessary for resilience modeling , especially in monitoring 100 
the possibility of disruptive events.  Thus , this  paper also presents a dynamic framework to quantitatively assess 101 
the resilience of systems of  dynamic nature (i.e., critical infrastructure s, building s, communitie s, etc.)  The 102 
framework can be used to assess the resilience of multiple systems at once and it adopts the DBN as an inference 103 
tool. In fact, a  DBN model can be obtained by expert knowledge, from a database using a comb ination  of 104 
machine -learning techniques, or both. These properties make the DBN formalism very useful  in the disaster 105 
resilience domain  as this domain has an abundance of both expert knowledge  and database records.  Moreover, 106 
a DBN allows perform ing a transient analysis of the system after the occurrence  of disruption until the system 107 
was recovered from its disruptive  states.  The transient analysis can be  rather useful to model the restoration 108 
process of the damaged system. The proposed resilience fr amework is presented in the form of a mathematical  109 
formulation  that integrates the probability distribution of all variables’ states . An illustrative example of a 110 
transportation network is used to demonstrate the proposed methodology. Results show the abil ity of the 111 
framework to dynamically model complex systems even in cases where data are scarce. 112

Section 3 introduces a framework to assess  the resilience  of static systems  where the BN is used as the inference 114 
method . In Section 4, an illustrative example is presented to show the applicability of the static resilience 115 
framework. Section 5 discusses  the general framework for modeling and quantifying  the resilience of dynamic 116

2. Bayesian and Dynamic Bayesian Network s 120 
2.1 Bayesian Network (BN)  121 
The Bayesian Network, also known as Bayesian Belief Network, is a graphical model that allows the design of 122 
stochastic  relationships among a group of variables  (De Iuliis et al. under review ). Application s of BN can be 123 
found in a variety of fields, from social to econo mic and biological disciplines (Ismail et al. 2011 ; Schultz and 124 
Smith 2016 ). BN permits the usage of different types of knowledge, both quantitative and qualitative, and can 125 
cope with missing data considering the uncertainty embedded  in the system (Balbi et al. 2014 ). To construct a 126 
BN, several hypotheses have to be made. E ach hypothesis is de composed into a set of random variables . Each 127 
variable can take values within a finite set of states (also known as beliefs), mutually exclusive and collectively  128 
exhaustive (MECE)  (Grover 2013 ). The dependency of one variable on another is represented in the network 129 
as a directed edge (or lin k). The relation ships between the variables in a BN are expressed in terms of family 130 
relationships. The  link starts from the so -called fathe r node and points at the son node, which is the impacted 131

considered in the model), but also the dependencies between the variables. The foundation  and the inference 134 
process  of BN is set in the Bayes' Theorem . Given  a state b for a variable B and a number k of MECE states aj, 135

2.2 Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN)  154 
BNs are used when the analyzed system is in a static state. This is often not the case in a dynamic, continuously 155 
changing world. This raises the need for a tool that is capable of accounting for system change s, such a s the 156 
Dynamic Bayesian Network . DBN is a Bayesian network extended with additional mechanisms that are capable 157 
of modeling influences over time (Murphy and Russell 2002 ). It extends the classical BN by adding the time 158 
dimension. It  is suitable for describing  dynamic systems where the performance fluctuates (e.g. before and after 159 
a disaster).  Like the BN, the DBN is a directed  acyclic graphical model used for statistical processes.  A DBN 160 
consists of multiple  BNs (often referred to as time -slices or time steps),  each with its own variables. The 161 
variables within a single and/or successive time -slices are connected using links. A DBN can be defined as (B 1, 162 
B→), where B 1 is a BN that specifies  the initial distrib ution of the variable states P(Z1) (Murphy and Russell 163

The DBN is often seen as a generalization of other temporal reasoning developments, such as the hidden Markov 177

Temporal plate and contemporal nodes  181 
The temporal plate is the area within the DBN model that includes temporal information (i.e., information that 182 
changes from a time step to another) . The temporal plate includes the variables that evolve over time . These 183 
variables are  the part of the DBN that can be unrolled. However,  nodes that have a constant value at every time 184 
step are considered a waste of memory and computational power  if copied in each time step . Therefore, i t is 185 
wise to introduce the se nodes outsid e the temporal plates. T he collection of these nodes is called the contemporal 186 
space , and the nodes are called c ontemporal nodes  (Murphy and Russell 2002 ). 187 
Anchor and terminal nodes  188 
One extension of the original DBN formalism was established  in (Hulst 2006 ), where the author  introduced 189 
nodes that are only c onnected to the first and last t ime slices  of the DBN. Such varia bles do not affect the 190 
intermediate time slices like the contemporal nodes.  191 
 Anchor node (A): a node that is outside the temporal plate but has at least one child  node  inside the 192 
temporal plate  in the first time slice of the unrolled DBN.  193 
 Terminal node (T) : a node that is outside the temporal plate and has at least one par ent inside the 194 
temporal plate in  the last time slice of the unrolled DBN.  195

4.1 Model definition: Hyogo Framework for Action  291 
This section illustrates the static resilience framework introduced in Section 3. Given the increase in the number 292 
of natural and man -made disasters, the United Nations (UNISDR) ha s formulated a structured approach to help 293 
communities cope with unexpected disruptions. The conceived framework, firstly presented in the 2005 294 
UNISDR report (ISDR 2005 ; UNISDR 2008 ), is known as the Hyogo Framework for Action (HFA). It is now 295 
considered a global blueprint for minimizing the risk associated with natural hazards through the 296 
implementation of national laws for risk managemen t and control. The HFA was originally conceptualized in 297 
Kobe, Japan with the goal of encouraging countries to implement resilient measures in their respective laws. 298 
The lifespan for the implementation was from 2005 to 2015. After that, each of the particip ating countries was 299 
required to submit a report (a detailed questionnaire) on their own progress. A score was then given by the UN 300 
to each of the submitted reports based on the progress each country had made (Kammouh et al. 2018c ; UNISDR 301 
2011 ). The progress recorded by every country is computed on the basis of a five -point scale for each indicator, 302 
where ‘one point’ indicates weak progress while ‘five points’ implies a great endeavor and commitment in that 303 
specific area. The scores of the 2 2 indicators for 37 countries assessed by the United Nations  are reported in the 304 
Appendix . 305 
The objective of the HFA is the significant reduction in losses after disasters. Following the resilience model 306 
introduced in Section 3.1, the HFA indicators are unfolded under the three resilience pillars:  307 
a) Reduced Vulnerabil ity: includes consideration of disaster risk that is aimed at preventing and mitigating 308 
disaster as well  as reducing vulnerability;  309 
b) Robustness: Strengthening of institutions and mechanisms at all level aiming at increasing resilience 310 
(P2); 311 
c) Adaptive and Recovery capacity: Structural embedding of risk reduction methods for emergency 312 
preparation, response, and recovery (P 3). 313 
To increase the level of detail and to convert the strategic goals into operationalizable activities, the UNISDR 314 
introduces five priorities:  315

Sensitivity analysis can help validate the probability parameters of a Bayesian network. It is done by 365 
investigating the effect of small changes in the values (probabilities)  of the input parameters on the output. 366 
Identifying  highly sensitive parameters  allows for a directed allocation of effort in order to obtain accurate 367 
results of a Bayesian network model.  368 
In this example , an algorithm proposed by (Kjærulff and van der Gaag 2000 ) is implemented. Given a set of 369 
target nodes, a complete set of the derivatives of the posterior probability distributions over the target nodes 370 
over each of the numerical parameters of the Bayesian network  are efficiently calculated . These derivatives 371 
indicate the  importance of precision of network numerical parameters for calculating the posterior probabilities  372 
of the targets. A large  derivative of a parameter  p leads to a large variation  in the posteriors of the targets  given 373 
a variation in the parameter p. If the derivative is small, then even large changes in the parameter make little 374 
difference in the poster iors. 375

analysis shows the most sensitive parameters for a selected state of the target node. Figure 7-9 show the 377

5. Time -dependent resilience analysis using Dynamic Bayesian Networks  395 
In general, the r esilience of a system tends to be  a process rather than  a state; thus, accounting for the 396 
performance variation of a system can be  important. Ordinary Bayesian Networks are unable to account for  the 397 
time dimension in the analysis as they are limited to static systems. In this section , we propose a new 398 
methodology to assess the resilience of engineering systems in a dynamic manner.  399 
5.1 Dynamic resilience model  400 
The resilience model used in the dynamic resilience analysis is based on the resilience definition by Bruneau 401 
and Reinhorn (2007)  who describe the resilience of a system using four components, also called the four R ’s of 402 
resilienc e (4R’s): 403 
 Robustness  (R1): refers to the ability of a system to stand a certain level of stress preserving its 404 
functionality;  405 
 Redundancy  (R2): indicates the alternative resources in the recovery stage when the primary ones are 406 
inadequate;  407 
 Rapidity  (R3): the capacity to contain losses and avoid future disruption. It represents the slope of the 408 
functionality curve during the recovery phase ; 409 
 Resourcefulness  (R4): considers the human factor and the capacity to move needed resources . 410 
This model is more detailed and more suitable to study dynamic events  than the one described in Section 3.1; 411 
therefore , this model will be used  hereafter . As shown in Figure 10, the first two resilience components ( R1 and 412

5.2 Network structure  and elements connectivity  420 
DBN  is a series of Bayesian networks with changing conditions. The elements connectivity within a  single time 421 
step of a  DBN is treated similarly to what introduced before (see Section 3.2 and Figure 4). One main 422 
characteristic  of DBN  is that elements are connected through different time -steps. For example, element At can 423 
be linked to element Bt+1 using a temporal link if element Bt+1 has a dependency on At, where t is the time step. 424 
The connections between elements at different time steps are done using expert knowledge  or from past data. 425 
Figure 11 shows a DBN where the individual networks at different time steps are connected with one an other. 426 
In our methodology, a n element in a BN at time -step t can only affect itself at time -step t+1 (i.e., At affects At+1 427 
and Bt affects Bt+1). 428 
Regarding the four resilience components  (4R’s) , they are incorporated in the network at different time -steps. 429 
In Figure 11, the first step ( t=1) corresponds to the initial state of the system (i.e., before hazard occurrence). 430 
At this stage, none of the 4R’s  is involved as the aim is to assess the initial  performance of the system. The 431 
second step ( t=2) is dedicated to assessing the damage that w ould incur if a hazard of a certain magnitude occurs. 432 
The level of damage, or the drop in the functionality , can be determined by acquiring information about the 433 
hazard  (H) and the system’s characteristics (i.e., R1 and R 2). The combination of the paramete rs H, R1, and R2 434 
can provide valuable information on how a system with a predefined initial state would behave. Thus, t he two 435 
resilience components R 1 and R 2 are connected to the DBN  at the second time -step (t=2).  436 
Once the drop in the serviceability is determined, the recovery needs to be evaluated. Since recovery is not an 437 
instantaneous action, several  DBN time steps  are needed  here. Therefore, the  recovery period is divided into a 438 
finite number of time -steps. Information about the rapidity and the reso urcefulness  (R3 and R 4) of the system is 439 
integrated at all recovery time-steps as they will define how the variables (i.e., the indicators) will evolve from 440 
one step to another. Therefore, the same Bayesian network is copied from time -step t=3 until time s tep t=T. 441 


==== Page 21 ====
20 The result of each BN is a performance point. The collection of the performance points creates a resilience 442 
function that shows the changes in the system’s performance , starting  from a stable state (the first uniform  part 443 
of the function  in Figure 11) and ending with a stable state, when the system is fully recovered (the second  444 
uniform  part of the function ). Once obtained, t he resilience function can be  used to obtain a resilience index . 445 
One method uses the area above the resilience curve and links it to the notion “loss of Resilience” (Bruneau and 446 
Reinhorn 2007 ; Cimellaro et al. 2010 ) while o ther methods consider  other metrics to quantify  the resilience 447 
(Sharma et al. 2018 ). 448

The use of DBN to model and analyze engineering systems has been very limited in the literature. DBN adds to 637 
the conventional BN the ability to consider time as a  parameter  in the analysis . All engineering systems are 638 
becoming strongly interdependent and t his results in increased dynamic behavior of the systems. The DBN 639 
solves the challenge of dynamic modeling of engineering systems while it preserves  all features of BN. While 640 
in this study we employ DBN to model and analyze the resilience of transportation  infrastructure, the proposed 641 
model can be used to study the resilience of other infrastructure types as well as other engineering  (and non - 642 
engineering)  phenomenon  that requires consideration of time . To give some examples of future applications, 643 
the propo sed DBN formulation can be used to model, analyze, and improve the intervention activities of 644 
infrastructure in terms of scheduling and cost.  This type of analysis cannot be performed with a static tool like 645 
BN. This, however, entails a detailed model of i nfrastructure intervention strategy. Another example can be  646 
structural degradation modeling and analysis. Degradation is a time -dependent process that cannot be studied  647 
at an instance of time , and this provides a good medium for  DBN to be utilized.  It shou ld be noted that the use 648 
of DBN in such applications should be limited to cases where more complex tools cannot be used either due to 649 
the modeling complexity of data availability.  650 
8. Conclusion s 651 
Unlike the static resilience analysis which assumes a constant s tate of a system and measures the resilience by 652 
a static quantity, the dynamic resilience analysis additionally models the evolvement of the system with time . 653 
This paper introduced  a probabilistic resilience assessment and prediction framew ork using the  Bayesian  and 654 
Dynamic Bayesian N etworks  (BN and DBNs) . The framework employed  resilience indicators for its  655 
implementation to make it more usable by  decision -makers in the industry. The methodology  can handle  both 656 


==== Page 32 ====
31 static and dynamic engineering systems  using quantitative and /or qualitative  data.  The uncertainty in the input s 657 
and in the variables  relationships is accounted for and  propagated  through out the model; h ence, the output is 658 
probabilistic in nature.  Two illustrative examples  were  presented in the paper. T he first is  a static system that 659 
uses the indicators of the Hyogo Framework for Action (HFA) to assess  the resilience of a country, while the 660 
second is a transportation network  modeled  as a dynamic system. The examples  demonstrate  the applicability 661 
of the framework  for both  static and dynamic systems.  662 
In the static analysis, the indicators are the main determinant of the resilience output. A highly uncertain state 663 
of the indicator (i.e., uniform probability distribution among the indicator’s states) would result in a high 664 
standard deviation in the probability distribution of the  resilience states.  For the dynamic analysis , results show 665 
a nonlinear behavior  of resilience as a function of time. T he recovery variables play a  significant role in the 666 
resilience assessment, where the resilience function shows an increasing trend  whose slope depends  on the 667 
recovery capacity of the system. The damage variables also contribute to the overall resilience output as they 668 
are the primar y determinant of the system’s functionality drop following the disaster event. A large functionality 669 
drop would result in a longer re covery time under the same  recovery characteristics  of a system . In both static 670 
and dynamic analys es, the uncertainty is in troduced in the indicators ’ initial conditions. This is rather useful 671 
when determini stic numbers are not available to initiate the analysis.  672 
The quantitative resilience analysis tools that can be readily available to  system designers to model and quantify 673 
engineering resilience are still underdeveloped. This paper aims at motivat ing the resilience community to agree 674 
on the proposed universal resilience framework . The presented framework  provides a tool for decision -makers 675 
to systematically learn about the state of their system s given a specific event. It allows them to improve the 676 
system s’ performance  using the backward analysis feature of BN. This is done by setting a desirable state of the 677 
resilience and getting the variables inputs that lead to the predefined  resilience state.  678 
There is a number of limitations in the proposed BN and DBN approaches. F irst, the need to include subjectivity 679 
during  the different phases of model development and analysis. This is unavoidable because the main feature 680 
of BN is to substitute missing data with expert judgment , which is subjective. This can be partially addresse d 681 
by using multiple experts. Another limitation is the increased complexity of the model as the analyzed system 682 
increases in size and detail. When the system is complex, the mission of classifying the variables and connecting 683 
them to one another becomes so phisticated and involves more subjectivity.  In addition, BN is a directed acyclic 684 
graph, which means that if a variable depends on another variable the reverse is not true. This limits the 685 
possibility of modeling some real -life situations where two variabl es can be dependent on each other. This can 686 
be artificially solved by introducing the same variable twice in the network, one time as a dependent variable 687 
and another time as a leading variable. However, this can also cause some consequences on the final o utput and 688 
on the dynamic s of the system. Finally, DBN presents an additional limitation, which is the complexity in 689 
connecting variables at different temporal states. It can be challenging to identify which variables can affect 690 
other variables at another t ime step.  691 

==== Page 33 ====
32 Future work will be oriented towards  building detailed networks for  the damage and recovery variables as this 692 
would allow expressing the system in more detail. In addition, a procedure to evaluate  the interdependency 693 
among the variables , as well as their weighting factors,  will be further addressed.  Particularly, weighting the 694 
indicators can be crucial in determining the conditional probabilities of the father nodes. Future studies can 695 
address this aspect using the Analytic hierarchy process (AHP) , which has been extensively used in similar 696 
problems for organizing and analyzing complex decisions.  697
