 ABSTR ACT 1 
In a medium -term, the number  of semi -autonomous vehicles is expected to rise significantly . These 2 
change s in the vehicle capabilities makes it necessary to analyze their interaction with road infrastructure, 3 
which was developed for human -driven vehicles.  Current systems use artificial vision,  record ing the 4 
oncoming road and using the center and edgeline road markings to automatically facilitate keeping the 5 
vehicle within  the lane . In addition to alignment , as well as to road markings, lane width emerges as one 6 
of the geometric parameters that might cause disengagements and has to be assessed.  7 
The objective of this research is to study the impact of lane width on a semi -autonomous vehicle  8 
performance . The automatic lateral control of this type of vehicle was tested using one of them along 81 9 
lanes of an urban arterial , with diverse widths.  Results  showed that t he semi -autonomous system tend ed 10 
to fail at narrow lanes. There was a maximum width below which human control  was always required – 11 
named human lane width  – measuring  2.5 m. There was also found a minimum width above which 12 
automatic control  was always possible – named  automatic lane width  – and established in 2.75 m. Finally, 13 
a lane width of 2.72 m has  the same probability of automatic and human lateral control  – named critical 14 
lane width . 15 
Following a similar methodology, these parameters can be determined for other vehicles , enhancing the 16 
interaction between AVs and road infrastructure and thus supporting a fast deployment of autonomous 17 
technology without compromising safety.  18

 INTRODUCTION  1 
The number of semi -autonomous vehicles is on the rise. With increasing technical capabilities 2 
and efforts by the Original Equipment Manufa cturers ( OEMs ) and public administrations, these vehicles 3 
are expected to dominate the market in five years , despite cyberattack and safety concerns  (1). The 4 
European Commission has recently included a new policy action with a strong focus on a comprehensive 5 
framework f or connected (2) and automated driving (3), requiring all new marketed vehicles by 2022 to 6 
be semi -autonomous. All these actions help fostering the deployment of full -autonomous vehicles, 7 
although this scenario is still unforeseeable in an accurate way (4). 8 
The Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) scaled automated driving systems from 0 to 5 (5) as 9 
a function of how driving negotiation is controlled by the system (longitudinal and/or lateral ), fallback 10 
response, and Operational Design Domain (ODD).  An ODD is defined as the circumstances under an 11 
Autonomous Driving System is designed to operate.  12 
In automation level 2, the vehicle can control both longitudinal and lateral  negotiation . However, 13 
level 2 systems are not perfect and occasionally fail at perceiving/interpreting the road  path, giving  the 14 
control to the driver in what is called a disengagement. This is why the human must always be  monitoring 15 
the system . Automation level 3 goes a step further, only requesting take -over by the driver for fallb ack 16 
response.  A level 4 semi -autonomous driving system should be able to take control of the vehicle under a 17 
given Operational Design Domain (ODD), continuously and uninterruptedly.  Current marketed vehicles 18 
may reach level 2 and, under certain limited operational domains, level 3.  19 
Since these systems require continuous human presence and monitoring, a Human -Machine 20 
Interface (HMI) is needed. This  interface alerts the driver when a disengagement occurs  (level 2) or their 21 
intervention  is about to be required (Take -Over Request, TOR) (level 3)  (6). Unfortunately, existing semi - 22 
autonomous systems are in a n early development stage and disengagements are relatively frequent . This 23 
may end into the drivers not willing to activate the system, or eventual surprises caused by unexpected 24 
disengagements  – which might be  risky, since it increases drivers’ response times ( 7,8). The effect of 25 
these failures is higher  as the system is more intervening on the driving task.  In addition, the HMI 26 
warnings are often quite subtle, given their relative frequency.  27 
Semi -autonomous driving automation systems  use center and edgeline road markings  to 28 
automatically facilitate keepi ng the vehicle within the lane.  There are different technologies to assist 29 
drivers in negotiation: Lane Departure Warning (LDW, it warns the driver if the vehicle is to leave the 30 
lane), Lane Keep Assist (LKA, in warns the driver and performs slight steering movements to avoid 31 
leaving the lane), and Lane Centering Assist (LCA, the system completely negotiates the vehicle direction  32 
following the lane path ). In addition, there are many different technologies behind these Advanced Driv er 33 
Assistance Syste ms (ADAS), with different performance  outcomes . 34 
Despite the technological variability  across systems, most of them are based on digital image 35 
processing , given the higher cost of other systems with good results ( 9). This system normally uses video 36 
cameras to record the oncoming road path.  Artificial vision systems must process a large amount of 37 
information per second, in real time and with high reliability. For that purpose, the information is 38 
generally  first cropped to a certain Region of Interest (RoI). Every photogram will be further decomposed 39 
into matrices, being the HSI system the most general one ( 10). Each one of these matrices is then 40 
processed to detect object boundaries and textures. Classic edge detection algorithms compare adjacent 41 
pixel s, while ridge -type algorithms use more pixels and give better results ( 11). 42 
This is a critical process, since road markings might not be in good state, thus leading to unclear 43 
zones, or shady areas that are difficult to be interpret ed as a road marking. C ácere et al. proposed a 44 
methodology to overcome this limitation, based on an RGB filtering followed by an intensity clustering 45 
(12). 46 
With this information, another algorithm depicts a polyline centered in each road marking, and 47 
inverse conic restitution is  later applied ( 9), taking the visualized road markings and project ing them into 48 
a X-Y reference . Besides the correct detection of these lines in every photogram, it is necessary their 49 
temporal comparison, checking that the geometry from consecutive photograms is  consistent ( 9). In most 50 
cases, stochastic methods are used.  51

 Some methodologies may be applied in this step, to increase the reliability  of the result  and/or 1 
alleviate the processing requirements . These kind s of methodologies help the system when encountering  2 
confusing zones.  Some of them compare the layout of the boundary lines that conform a lane (which 3 
should be parallel in nearly all situations). Du and Tan applied a ridge detection methodology for this 4 
purpose ( 11), after enhancing the quality of the photograms  with several filtering processes.  5 
The relative position of the vehicle is also very important. Its position is normally determined 6 
based on inverse conical restitution, but more advanced systems such as stere oscopic vision and a helping 7 
camera are currently under development ( 9). 8 
Many disengagements and take over requests are caused by an impossibility of clearly tracking 9 
road markings or a misinterpretation of road geometry. This may partially be explained si nce the 10 
remarkable advances in vehicle automation are not being developed in line with road infrastructure and 11 
regulations ( 13, 14, 15 ). 12 
Harmonization and update of regulations to consider both human and autonomous vehicles would 13 
help at minimizing some  disengagements.  This would require more information about what aspect 14 
triggered the disengagement  but given the variety of systems  and the limited information available, it is 15 
sometimes very difficult to know  it. This is why the authors have focused on developing methodologies to 16 
test how well a  certain  driving automation system adapts to road infrastructure, regardless its underlying 17 
technology. This information would help to a) establish a clear benchmark  for OEMs to develop their 18 
systems, and b) shorten the path towards SAE level 4 systems and adequately defined ODDs.  19 
In recent studies, t he authors explored the geometric -related limitations of a driving automation 20 
system, showing a clear relationship betw een speed , geometry  and disengagements  (14). They also 21 
studied how the same driving automation system  responds  to existing edgeline road marking  gaps (such 22 
as those present at exit, entrance ramps, lane merging and so on)  (13). It was observed that near 25% of 23 
entrance and exit ramps  caused the system to fail, as well as diverging, merging sections, and similar  ones 24 
(Figure 1 ). 25

OBJECTIVES  AND HYPOTHESIS  30 
The literature review allowed  to establish the scope for this research, not having found 31 
experimental studies about  the impact of the lane width in the performance of automatic lateral control.  32 
The objective of this research is to study the impact of lane width and road marking layout on a certain 33 
Lane Keeping Assist (LKA)  system . The main hypothesis is th at a wider  lane increases the reliabi lity of 34 
this autonomous driving system . To check  that, a single semi -autonomous vehicle was tested along some 35 
tangent sections of urban streets, with different lane widths and road marking  patterns.  36

RESULTS   7 
Figure 5 depicts the automatic and human lateral control rate for every lane, as a function of the 8 
lane width. The main hypothesis is confirmed , since the automatic lateral control rate increases with the 9 
lane width. On the contrary , the human lateral control rate decrease s.  10 
The human lateral control was totally prevailing under 2.5 m wide . This new concept  – maximum 11 
width for which steering control is 100% human – is defined as human lane width . 12 
In addition, the automatic lateral control was 100% effective above 2.75 m wi de. The automatic 13 
lane width  is therefore defined as the minimum width for which the steering control can be 100% 14 
fulfilled by this driving  automat ion system . 15

 Human and automatic lane widths reveal as crucial factors for automotive industry and 1 
infrastructure design.  Conversely to other design factors such as horizontal and vertical geometry, the 2 
narrow interval between both thresholds reveal the stro ng and undesirable  effects  that subtle design 3 
actions  might have on autonomous driving.  4 
Figure 6 summarizes the human and automated lateral control rates in 5 cm  bins. The 5 
abovementioned performance is observed, and the critical lane width  can be calculated as the 6 
intersection of both lines, being the width that presents the same probability for human and automatic 7 
lateral control , determined  at 2.72 m.  8 
Thus, a vehicle w ith the same dimensions and this driving automation system would perform as a 9 
function of the lane width as follows:  10 
• Lanes narrower than the human lane width: the system cannot perform  in autonomous mode.  11 
• Lanes between the human lane width and the critical lane width: the system generally 12 
disengages  but might sometimes track the road path if all other conditions are favorable.  13 
• Lanes between the critical lane width and the automatic lane width: the system general ly 14 
tracks road geometry, but might eventually disengage ( not perfect road marking, presence of 15 
dust/shadows on the road, etc.).  16 
• Lanes wider than the automatic lane width: the system is capable of track the road in an 17 
autonomous way (as long as other factor s are also compatible with the system).  18

without considering the vehicle te chnology . Thus, the technolog y-related causes triggering 5

are provided  therefore : 7 
• The visual sub system cannot perceive /process  narrower lanes in an accurate way . In this case, 8 
other vehicles with the same driving automation system would present the same limitation 9 
regardless their dimensions (including heavy vehicles).  10

To summarize , these findings clearly define 2.50 m to 2.75 m as a n Operational Design Domain 20 
threshold  for this driving automation technology.  Other systems could be tested following a similar 21 
methodo logy, hence determining the lane width they require to fully perform autonomously (again, as 22 
long as other limiting parameters are met).  HD GPS maps should therefore include information about the 23 
lane width, allowing any driving automation system to compare it to its limitations.  24 
For autonomous heavy vehicles, their automatic lane width might define another ODD threshol d 25 
(around 3.40 -3.50 m) . From a design perspective, dedicated lanes for automated heavy vehicles could be 26 
proposed , increasing road capacity . At this point, it would be necessary to force harmonization of the 27 
driving automation system capabilities  to these widths.  Dedicated lanes for light vehicles could be 28 
designed following the same principle, as well.  29 
Finally, it is necessary to highlight  that all tests were performed at daytime and under good 30 
weather conditions , but other aspects that might affect the system performance were undetermined 31 
variable s. These factors were related to the road marking pattern and discontinuity , color (white or 32 
yellow), visibility of road markings  and some dust; some shadows over the ca rriageway; and sun glar ing 33 
(E/W orientation).  Some of these parameters are static and some others are dynamic, all requiring further 34

CONCLUSIONS  5 
As well as other road des ign features, lane width has been found to impact the disengagement 6 
probability of driving autonom ation  systems.  The authors have explored how this factor affects the 7 
reliability of a level 2 vehicle  throughout 81 lanes located at tangent  sections  of an urban arterial. Results 8 
show that lane width thresholds for human and automated lateral control can be clearly def ined.  9 
Three new lane width concepts are therefore proposed : human lane width  – maximum width for 10 
which human is always requested  for the steering control ; automatic lane width  – minimum width for 11 
which the steering control can always be performed autonomously ; and the critical lane width , which 12 
presents  the same probability of human and automatic  lateral control.  13 
For the tested system, h uman lane width was determined as 2.50 m, automated lane width was 14 
2.75 m, while the critical lane width was established at 2.72 m. Note  the very narrow range that shifts 15 
from complete manual to complete automated lateral control  (0.25 m) . 16 
It is important to highlight that these thresholds were determined for a single LKA system . Other 17 
LKA/LCA systems are based on a variety of technologies and will present different limitations. The 18 
methodology proposed in this paper establishes a framework to test the capabil ities of the different 19 
systems, without the need to know the underlying technology.  Hence, this information could be used as a 20 
factor to define clear Operating Design Domains  and therefore rapidly determine if any width -related 21 
disengagement is expected al ong a certain corridor . 22 
In order to get some insight about the limitations of current marketed systems, a further research 23 
will include more observations with other makes and models, including heavy vehicles . These tests will 24 
be carried out in different Operating Design Domains  and different geometries (such as horizontal and 25 
crest vertical curves), and different speeds. Adverse weather, night driving and sun glaring conditions 26 
should also be tested, to check how robust is a certain driving auto mation system technology to different 27 
ODD variations.  28 
New parameters should also be considered in the future, such as the lateral position of the vehicle, 29 
to determine if it has some influence on the disengagement event. If so, it is expected a slight redu ction of 30 
these thresholds, as well as a possible reduction of the range between manual and automatic lane widths.  31 
Every vehicle should be measured with and without mirrors, considering the outer boundary of the 32 
vehicle.  33
