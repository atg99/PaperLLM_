4.1 Model definition: Hyogo Framework for Action  291 
This section illustrates the static resilience framework introduced in Section 3. Given the increase in the number 292 
of natural and man -made disasters, the United Nations (UNISDR) ha s formulated a structured approach to help 293 
communities cope with unexpected disruptions. The conceived framework, firstly presented in the 2005 294 
UNISDR report (ISDR 2005 ; UNISDR 2008 ), is known as the Hyogo Framework for Action (HFA). It is now 295 
considered a global blueprint for minimizing the risk associated with natural hazards through the 296 
implementation of national laws for risk managemen t and control. The HFA was originally conceptualized in 297 
Kobe, Japan with the goal of encouraging countries to implement resilient measures in their respective laws. 298 
The lifespan for the implementation was from 2005 to 2015. After that, each of the particip ating countries was 299 
required to submit a report (a detailed questionnaire) on their own progress. A score was then given by the UN 300 
to each of the submitted reports based on the progress each country had made (Kammouh et al. 2018c ; UNISDR 301 
2011 ). The progress recorded by every country is computed on the basis of a five -point scale for each indicator, 302 
where ‘one point’ indicates weak progress while ‘five points’ implies a great endeavor and commitment in that 303 
specific area. The scores of the 2 2 indicators for 37 countries assessed by the United Nations  are reported in the 304 
Appendix . 305 
The objective of the HFA is the significant reduction in losses after disasters. Following the resilience model 306 
introduced in Section 3.1, the HFA indicators are unfolded under the three resilience pillars:  307 
a) Reduced Vulnerabil ity: includes consideration of disaster risk that is aimed at preventing and mitigating 308 
disaster as well  as reducing vulnerability;  309 
b) Robustness: Strengthening of institutions and mechanisms at all level aiming at increasing resilience 310 
(P2); 311 
c) Adaptive and Recovery capacity: Structural embedding of risk reduction methods for emergency 312 
preparation, response, and recovery (P 3). 313
