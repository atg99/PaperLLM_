The paper presents a method called Pretext-Invariant Representation Learning (PIRL), which aims to create image representations that remain invariant to various transformations. The core of PIRL is a loss function designed to minimize the difference between the representations of an image and its transformed counterpart, thus promoting invariance. This is achieved through a contrastive loss that evaluates the similarity between image representations, encouraging the network to produce consistent outputs despite transformations.

The authors contrast their approach with previous methods that learn representations that are covariant to transformations. These earlier methods retain information about the transformations that may not be semantically relevant, whereas PIRL focuses on achieving invariance.

PIRL employs a noise contrastive estimator (NCE) to maximize the similarity between a pair of matched images while minimizing the similarity to a set of negative samples from different images. A significant aspect of the methodology is the use of a memory bank that stores representations of images, allowing the model to leverage a large number of negative samples without requiring a large batch size during training. This memory bank consists of exponential moving averages of representations computed in prior training epochs.

The final loss function combines two NCE losses: one that compares the transformed image representation with its memory counterpart and another that encourages the original image representation to align with its memory representation. This dual focus helps stabilize the learning process and enhances the quality of the learned representations.

The paper emphasizes the flexibility of PIRL, noting that it can be integrated with various pretext tasks involving image transformations, but the authors specifically focus on the Jigsaw pretext task for their experiments. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of PIRL in learning invariant representations that are robust to transformations.
