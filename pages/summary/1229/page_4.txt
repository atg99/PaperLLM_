The paper critiques the common reliance on statistical significance in research, highlighting the neglect of effect sizes which limits the understanding of result magnitudes and their practical relevance. It points out that in nonlinear models, such as logit and probit, interaction effects are often misinterpreted due to their conditional nature on other independent variables. This complicates the interpretation of interaction coefficients, as their significance is context-dependent and cannot be assessed using simple t-tests as in linear regression.

The paper also discusses the challenges of using nonlinear models in the presence of fixed effects, emphasizing that logit models may yield biased estimates when fixed effects are included, as they often drop observations without outcome variation. It argues that linear regression generally performs better in these contexts, except under specific conditions where alternative specifications like the conditional logit estimator may be applicable but come with interpretative difficulties.

Furthermore, the paper outlines the Neyman-Rubin causal model to explain the average treatment effect (ATE) in experimental designs, asserting that when treatment and outcomes are independent, the ATE remains unbiased and consistent, regardless of whether the outcome is binary. It concludes that the binary nature of outcomes does not affect the average causal effect, as differences in means correspond to differences in probabilities.
