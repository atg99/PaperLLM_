Liu et al. argue that traditional approaches to machine learning fairness often overlook the dynamic nature of populations and decision-making processes over time. They emphasize the need for models that consider the long-term effects of seemingly fair rules, particularly in contexts like loan decisions where such rules can inadvertently harm disadvantaged groups.

Further discussions in the paper address the challenges of resource allocation in situations of scarcity, highlighting a proposed learning algorithm that achieves fair allocation without prior knowledge of group frequencies. This is particularly relevant in scenarios such as loan approvals or crime reporting, where the true characteristics of unselected individuals remain unknown. The authors present experimental results demonstrating the algorithm's effectiveness in predictive policing, despite potential feedback loops caused by reliance on historical data.

The concept of feedback loops in predictive policing is explored, revealing how repeated allocation of resources to certain areas can distort crime rate perceptions. The authors propose mathematical models to understand and mitigate the impact of these loops, suggesting that citizen-reported incidents can improve the accuracy of predictive systems.

Lastly, the paper discusses strategic manipulation models, which illustrate how individuals from different social groups may exert varying levels of influence over decision-making algorithms. The findings indicate that such disparities in manipulation costs can lead to unfair outcomes, where members of advantaged groups might receive favorable decisions while disadvantaged individuals face adverse ones. The authors conclude that the increasing reliance on algorithmic decision-making necessitates careful consideration of social dynamics to prevent reinforcing inequalities.
