This paper discusses the concepts of interpretability and explainability in machine learning, emphasizing their distinct yet interconnected roles. Interpretability refers to the broader understanding of a model's internal logic, while explainability focuses on the specific reasoning behind a model’s decisions based on its inputs and outputs. The authors highlight that interpretability does not guarantee explainability, nor vice versa, arguing that both are crucial for a comprehensive understanding of machine learning systems.

The paper outlines evaluation methods for interpretability, as proposed by Doshi-Velez and Kim, which include application-grounded, human-grounded, and functionally-grounded evaluations. Application-grounded evaluation assesses how interpretations affect specific tasks, while human-grounded evaluation focuses on the quality of interpretations as perceived by general users rather than domain experts. Functionally-grounded evaluation relies on mathematical definitions to assess interpretability without human involvement, useful in situations where human testing is impractical.

The authors also reference related work that attempts to define interpretability and explainability, including a taxonomy proposed by Gilpin et al., which categorizes interpretability methods for neural networks into three groups: methods emulating data processing, methods explaining internal representations, and transparent networks that inherently explain their functionality. The paper acknowledges the advancements made in explaining deep neural networks but also points out the ongoing challenges in establishing clear measurement criteria and metrics for interpretability evaluation.
