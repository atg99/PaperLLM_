In this paper, Floridi and Chiriatti explore the concepts of reversibility and identity in relation to mathematical logic and philosophy, particularly focusing on the implications for distinguishing between human and artificial intelligence. They illustrate the difference between reversible and irreversible processes using examples from logic gates, where the NOT gate is reversible (invertible), while the XOR gate is irreversible. This leads to a discussion of Leibniz's law, which posits that if two entities possess the same properties, they are indistinguishable.

The authors introduce the distinction between different types of questions—particularly semantic questions—which can reveal the nature of their source. They argue that while some answers to mathematical or factual questions are irreversible and do not indicate whether the source is human or artificial, semantic questions can yield reversible answers that may help identify the source. This sets the stage for the Turing Test, which aims to evaluate a machine's ability to exhibit intelligent behavior indistinguishable from that of a human.

Turing had predicted that by the year 2000, computers would be able to perform well in the imitation game, making it challenging for an interrogator to distinguish between human and machine responses. The paper concludes by reflecting on the historical context of Turing's beliefs in artificial intelligence, drawing parallels with the pursuits of other intellectual figures like Hobbes and Newton, highlighting the ongoing quest for understanding true AI.
