This paper discusses the effects of various data augmentation techniques on the performance of image generation models, focusing on the concept of "leaking" behavior during training. The authors analyze three specific augmentations: isotropic image scaling, random rotations, and color transformations, demonstrating how the probability of applying these augmentations affects the quality of generated images.

In their analysis, isotropic scaling is identified as a safe augmentation that does not lead to leaking, regardless of its application probability. In contrast, random rotations can cause issues if applied too frequently, as the generator may struggle to predict the correct orientation of images. The authors find that maintaining a probability below approximately 0.85 helps ensure correct orientation, while higher probabilities introduce randomness that can degrade image quality.

The augmentation pipeline proposed in the paper consists of 18 transformations categorized into pixel blitting, geometric transformations, color transforms, image-space filtering, additive noise, and cutout. These augmentations are applied during the training of the generator, with their strength controlled by a scalar value that determines the probability of application. The authors highlight the importance of differentiable implementations of these augmentations to ensure compatibility with training dynamics.

Furthermore, the paper presents experiments demonstrating the effectiveness of stochastic discriminator augmentation, revealing that the optimal strength of augmentations varies with dataset size. For smaller datasets, pixel blitting and geometric transformations yield significant benefits, whereas color transformations show moderate improvements, and other methods like noise and cutout are less effective. The study concludes that careful management of augmentation probabilities is crucial to minimize leaking while maximizing the diversity of training data.
