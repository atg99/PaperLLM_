The paper discusses various definitions and measures of proximity in random forests, highlighting their applications and limitations. The original definition of random forest proximity, as described by Breiman, calculates the proportion of trees in which two observations share the same terminal node, regardless of whether they are in-bag or out-of-bag. This definition has shortcomings, particularly in overestimating class separation due to not considering bootstrap status, which can lead to misleading results in applications like outlier detection and data imputation.

To address these issues, a revised measure known as OOB proximity is introduced. This measure only considers trees where both observations are out-of-bag, but it also has limitations, particularly in failing to incorporate in-bag observations that are crucial for understanding random forest predictions.

The paper also explores alternative proximity measures, including a proximity-based kernel that accounts for the number of branches between observations in decision trees, which showed some improvement over the original definition but is computationally expensive. Other approaches, such as random partitioning kernels and dissimilarity measures, aim to enhance the understanding of proximity in random forests but lack public source code for implementation.

Overall, the paper underscores the need for more comprehensive and practical measures of proximity that accurately reflect the behavior of random forests while being computationally feasible.
