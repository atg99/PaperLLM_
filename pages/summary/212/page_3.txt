The paper discusses the challenges of building large-scale Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) that achieve high accuracy, despite various learning approaches being proposed. It focuses on converting trained deep residual networks (ResNets), which are a type of convolutional neural network (CNN), into their spiking counterparts, Spiking ResNets.

The authors explain that ResNets address the degradation problem in deep networks by introducing identity mapping layers, allowing deeper networks to perform at least as well as shallower ones. This is based on the premise that residual mappings are easier to optimize. The conversion process involves transforming an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) into a biologically plausible SNN, which is inherently more complex due to differences in how neurons process information. In ANNs, neurons receive real-valued inputs and use activation functions, while SNNs operate on spike trains and integrate incoming spikes to generate output spikes.

The conversion process is predicated on the assumption that the activation in ANNs can be approximated by the firing rate of spiking neurons. The authors detail the specific changes made during the conversion, such as replacing ReLU activation functions with integrate-and-fire neurons, and adapting convolution, pooling, and fully connected layers to their spiking equivalents. They outline how weights and biases from the ANN are mapped to the SNN, including adjustments for batch normalization and normalization of activations to ensure compatibility between ANN and SNN firing rates.

Overall, the paper provides a comprehensive overview of the methodology for constructing Spiking ResNets from trained ResNets, highlighting the theoretical foundations and practical considerations involved in this conversion process.
