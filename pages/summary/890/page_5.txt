The paper discusses a method for interpolating artistic styles, specifically between Paul Cezanne and Vincent van Gogh, demonstrating that the approach effectively mimics not only colors but also contours and textures unique to each style. The authors conduct experiments to assess the effectiveness of their stylization technique through expert and non-expert deception rates. 

In the expert deception rate experiment, art historians are shown stylized image patches alongside real artworks to determine how often they misidentify the stylized images as authentic. Similarly, non-experts are tested to compare their ability to discern stylized images from real ones. The deception rate is also measured using a pretrained network for artist classification to evaluate how accurately the network can identify the artist behind the stylization.

Experiments focused on patches instead of full images to avoid the influence of contemporary objects that could easily reveal the images as not being authentic paintings. Results indicate that the proposed model outperforms the state-of-the-art AST model across ten different artists, with the expert deception rate being higher among art historians compared to non-experts, who may be better at identifying artifacts typical of generative models.

The paper also introduces a fixpoint disentanglement loss to separate style from content. This is validated through experiments measuring style variation in real artworks, confirming that the model can maintain intricate style details regardless of content changes. Overall, the findings suggest significant advancements in the ability to create convincing stylized images that resonate with historical artistic styles.
