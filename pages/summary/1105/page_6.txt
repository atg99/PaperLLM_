The paper discusses the concept of equivalence classes in Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) and their relationship to probability distributions in Bayesian Networks (BNs). While a factorization of the probability distribution of a variable X is uniquely determined by a DAG, the reverse is not true; multiple DAGs can represent the same distribution without altering the conditional independence relationships. This leads to the formation of equivalence classes characterized by an underlying undirected graph and v-structures, where some edges are compelled to have a specific direction to avoid introducing new v-structures or cycles.

The paper outlines the most common probability distributions used in BNs, namely discrete, ordinal, Gaussian, and conditional linear Gaussian distributions. These distributions must meet several criteria, including efficient structure learning from data, flexibility in distributional assumptions, and ease of querying for inference.

In discrete BNs, local distributions are represented by conditional probability tables, which are particularly useful for psychological data typically scored on Likert scales. However, the treatment of ordinal data as categorical can lead to information loss, prompting some researchers to treat Likert data as continuous.

Gaussian BNs operate under a multivariate normal distribution and utilize linear regression models for local distributions. They have limitations, such as the rarity of real-world data conforming to a multivariate Gaussian distribution and challenges in computing partial correlations with large variable sets due to singularities.

Conditional linear Gaussian BNs accommodate both discrete and continuous nodes, using conditional probability tables for discrete nodes and linear regression models for continuous nodes. However, they restrict continuous nodes from being parents of discrete nodes, limiting the modeling capabilities of mixed data.

Finally, structure learning algorithms for BNs can be categorized into constraint-based, score-based, and hybrid approaches, which utilize statistical tests, goodness-of-fit criteria, and expert knowledge to deduce the network structure from data.
