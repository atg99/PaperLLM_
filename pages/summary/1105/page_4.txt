Bayesian Networks (BNs) are defined as a combination of a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) and a probability distribution. The DAG serves as a qualitative framework for understanding the relationships between symptoms, while the probability distributions quantify these relationships in terms of magnitude and direction.

The DAG comprises nodes and directed edges, where each edge indicates a parent-child relationship between nodes. The absence of cycles in a DAG ensures that it can effectively express conditional independence among variables, meaning that some variables do not influence each other.

Markov blankets are critical in assessing the independence of nodes within a BN. They consist of a node's parents, children, and co-parents (spouses), allowing for the investigation of a target node while ignoring other nodes in the network. The Markov property asserts that nodes not directly connected by edges are either independent or conditionally independent when conditioned on other nodes.

The local Markov property applies to individual nodes, while the global Markov property extends this to sets of variables. Graphical separation in the DAG corresponds to probabilistic independence, making the network an effective representation of conditional independence relationships.

The Markov property enables the factorization of joint probability distributions into simpler models for each variable conditioned on its parents. This factorization is facilitated by the absence of cycles in the graph.
