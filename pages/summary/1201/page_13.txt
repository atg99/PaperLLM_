The paper presents algorithms for the AI Physicist framework, focusing on proposing and adding theories to a theory hub. Algorithm 5 outlines the process for proposing theories based on their performance against a new dataset. The hub retrieves all available theories, evaluates them based on their accuracy, and proposes the top M0 theories with the best performance. 

Algorithm 6 describes how theories are added to the hub after training. Each theory calculates its description length and, if it falls below a specified threshold, is added along with its training data to serve as a reference for future evaluations. This approach is grounded in the principle of Occam's razor, aiming for efficient representation.

The time complexity of the algorithms is discussed, highlighting that the differentiable divide-and-conquer algorithm (DDAC) has the highest computational cost, scaling with the number of parameters, data points, and discovered domains. Other algorithms, such as those for lifelong learning and Occam's razor, also exhibit polynomial time complexity, but their costs are comparatively lower.

The paper concludes with a theorem that establishes conditions under which predictions made by the theories can be compared based on their loss function, providing a foundation for the framework's reliability in making accurate predictions.
