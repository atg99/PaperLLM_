The paper discusses the challenges of scalability in reinforcement learning (RL), particularly in relation to real-world applications. Two primary limitations are identified: training time and inefficient state representation. Current RL methods require millions of samples for training, which is impractical in real-world scenarios due to the wear and tear on physical devices. To address this, the paper suggests either reducing the sim-to-real gap or enhancing the sample efficiency of exploration.

Another significant limitation is the capability for efficient state representation, which is essential for memorizing states and actions in large domains. The paper compares current RL methods, such as Go-Explore, with the human brain's ability to quickly recognize novel scenes using a network of neurons. This biological process allows for efficient representation that current RL techniques have yet to match.

The exploration-exploitation dilemma is also highlighted as a key issue in RL research. While many exploration strategies incorporate solutions to this dilemma, they often rely on designer-defined thresholds. The paper proposes training a set of skills during exploration that can be combined into more complex, goal-oriented policies, akin to human problem-solving.

Additionally, intrinsic reward mechanisms are discussed, focusing on improving the agent's ability to self-reward and balancing long-term versus short-term novelty. Current methods often involve expert-defined reward formulations, which are challenging to design. The paper suggests that an agent should learn to self-reward, reflecting a more natural evolutionary development of this mechanism. Achieving a balance between revisiting states and exploring new ones remains a challenge, as it typically requires time-consuming parameter tuning by designers.
