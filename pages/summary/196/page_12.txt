The paper discusses self-supervised exploration strategies in reinforcement learning, focusing on intrinsic rewards derived from model disagreement and state visitation counts. The disagreement method generates intrinsic rewards based on the variance between different models, allowing for effective exploration without the challenges of prediction speed and state recognition. This approach leverages the agent's uncertainty to foster exploration while seamlessly transitioning between exploration and exploitation.

Prediction error strategies address the "couch-potato" problem by incentivizing the agent to explore states that are sufficiently distant from previously observed states stored in a memory buffer. This prevents repetitive visits to similar states by limiting rewards based on temporal distance.

Count-based methods assign intrinsic rewards based on the visitation counts of states. A state with a low visitation count receives a higher reward, promoting further exploration. The effectiveness of this method is supported by the finding that using the reward computation of 1/N(s) leads to faster convergence compared to the conventional approach of 1/pN(s).

In environments with large state spaces, tracking visitation counts can be challenging due to memory constraints. To mitigate this, states can be represented in a reduced form, allowing for efficient storage of visitation counts in a compact table format. Techniques such as static hashing and SimHash are employed in these count on state representation methods to facilitate memory-efficient exploration.
