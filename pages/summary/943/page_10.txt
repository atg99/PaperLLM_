The paper discusses the evolution and application of neural network architectures in bioinformatics, particularly focusing on multilayer perceptrons and convolutional neural networks (CNNs). Multilayer perceptrons, with 10 layers of neurons, were among the first networks applied in biological modeling due to their simplicity and rapid training capabilities. However, as the field has advanced, newer architectures have emerged, although fully connected layers remain common in many of these models.

CNNs are highlighted for their effectiveness in processing image-like data, leveraging local structures in the input. They consist of convolutional layers that apply filters to small patches of input data, enabling the detection of specific patterns such as objects in images. CNNs maintain the same filter across all pixels, which facilitates the learning of local structures. The paper also introduces the concept of residual networks (ResNets), which incorporate skip connections to enhance training efficiency.

Different configurations of CNNs exist, including 1D, 2D, and 3D CNNs, tailored for various spatial data types, such as text, images, and volumetric data like MRI scans. The paper underscores the success of CNNs in biological applications, notably in protein structure prediction, where they analyze co-evolution of residue pairs for accurate 3D structure modeling. Additionally, CNNs have been applied in areas like genetic variant identification, 3D genome folding, DNA-protein interactions, cryogenic electron microscopy image analysis, and medical image classification, often achieving performance comparable to that of expert human analysts.
