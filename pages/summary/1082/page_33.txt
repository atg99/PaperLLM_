This paper discusses the mechanisms by which Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks address the vanishing gradient problem, particularly in the context of training over long sequences. It introduces the terms related to the partial derivatives of the state signal and the objective function, emphasizing the need to maintain certain conditions to prevent error gradients from diminishing excessively.

The authors derive a key equation (180) that extrapolates the error gradient from an earlier step to a later step in the sequence, highlighting the importance of preserving information across many time steps. They assert that if the partial derivatives remain below one at each step, the overall error gradient can be effectively managed, preventing it from approaching zero. Three scenarios are outlined: a trivial state where the network remains at rest, a complex interplay of signals that is unlikely in practice, and a situation where the spectral radius of a specific matrix is less than one, allowing gradients to decay but not vanish entirely.

The paper further details conditions under which LSTM can maintain manageable gradients, suggesting various configurations of weight norms and signal saturation. The authors conclude that when trained correctly, LSTM networks can effectively manage long-range dependencies in data, allowing for the gradients to decay at a controlled rate. They emphasize that while certain configurations can lead to favorable outcomes, careful orchestration of signals is critical for practical applications.
