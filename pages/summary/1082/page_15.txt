The paper discusses several critical remarks regarding the application and limitations of recurrent neural networks (RNNs), particularly in the context of unrolling techniques and their relationship with signal processing. 

Remark 1 highlights that the current propositions and proofs do not encompass advanced RNN architectures like Gated Recurrent Units (GRU) or Attention Networks, which are becoming increasingly relevant in the field.

Remark 2 addresses the independence of segment-level state signal subsequences, noting that while these subsequences are independent, input signal subsequences across different segments do not require independence. It emphasizes that dependencies in input signals can be managed through appropriately trained hidden layer weights.

Remark 3 points out that there is no universally optimal method for converting Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) systems to Finite Impulse Response (FIR) systems. The effectiveness of any approximation technique is contingent on how well the FIR system can adapt to specific data distributions and meet application quality metrics. It explains that unrolling RNNs for a finite number of steps can lead to artificial discontinuities in output sequences. The paper asserts that an unfolded RNN model can be trained effectively when the sequence is composed of independent segments, drawing a parallel to the Sampling Theorem in Discrete-Time Signal Processing.

Remark 4 discusses the potential challenges of applying Proposition 1 in practical scenarios, where output subsequences may exhibit inter-dependence or where the initial state of one subsequence could be influenced by another. It suggests that through empirical experimentation and statistical analysis, one may identify a suitable way to partition output sequences into independent subsequences while considering the generation process of input samples.

Overall, the paper underscores the complexity of RNN modeling in practice, particularly regarding independence assumptions and the implications of truncation during unrolling, which can significantly affect model performance and the quality of predictions.
