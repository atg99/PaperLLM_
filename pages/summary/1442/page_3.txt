The paper discusses a text-to-speech (TTS) system that employs three convolutional layers with 512 filters and a bidirectional long short-term memory (LSTM) layer of 256 units for each direction. The system uses a location-sensitive attention mechanism that incorporates attention history to compute a normalized weight vector for aggregating text encodings. The autoregressive decoder network processes the aggregated text encoding, conditioned on a speaker-specific 256-dimensional d-vector, similar to Tacotron 2D. To convert mel spectrograms to time-domain waveforms, a WaveRNN is separately trained.

Training TTS on non-studio data, particularly Isolated-Sentences, presents challenges. To enhance TTS fidelity, the authors implement two key modifications: they adopt a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)-based attention mechanism and augment the model with a hierarchical variational autoencoder (VAE). This hierarchical VAE consists of a local encoder encoding two-second chunks with overlap and a global encoder for the entire utterance. The combined style vectors improve stability and fidelity in the synthesized output, achieving a lower word error rate (WER) in recognition tasks.

The automatic speech recognition (ASR) experiments utilize a listen-attend-spell (LAS) model with an additive attention mechanism. Input speech is processed using 80-mel features through convolutional and bidirectional LSTM layers. The model is trained on a vocabulary of 16k graphemic word pieces, with specific hyperparameters tuned for performance on the LIBRISPEECH dataset.

The paper also addresses issues with end-to-end speech synthesis, particularly end-of-sequence problems that may lead to incomplete or nonsensical outputs. It discusses a method for filtering out poorly synthesized utterances based on WER before training, which is not applied to Isolated-Sentences due to fewer endpointing issues.

Lastly, the authors explore acoustic diversity through data augmentation in LIBRISPEECH by synthesizing duplicate training utterances using TTS. They describe different approaches to generating d-vectors for speaker representation, including using original d-vectors, randomly selected d-vectors, and generating random d-vectors to enhance speaker variability in synthesized outputs.
