The paper discusses various pretraining techniques for language models, particularly focusing on the advancements made in models like BERT and RoBERTa. It explains the traditional n-gram models that predict the next token based on preceding tokens and introduces the concept of masked language models (MLMs), where random tokens are replaced with a special mask token for prediction. The authors highlight the training process involving a masking rate that can vary over epochs for stability and mention the inclusion of Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) despite debates on its utility.

The paper also explores advanced pretraining techniques, notably whole-word masking (WWM), which enhances the model's ability to understand contextual dependencies by ensuring that entire words are masked during training. Additionally, the authors investigate adversarial pretraining, which incorporates perturbations in the input embedding layer to fortify the model against adversarial attacks.

Focusing on biomedical applications, the paper contrasts domain-specific pretraining with mixed-domain pretraining. It argues that while the common practice has been to incorporate general-domain text alongside biomedical data, purely biomedical pretraining could yield superior results for domain-specific tasks. The paper critiques existing models like BioBERT and BlueBERT for their reliance on general-domain vocabularies, ultimately advocating for a fresh approach to biomedical pretraining that does not dilute the focus with out-domain text. SciBERT is noted as a partial exception, but it still employs mixed-domain techniques, which the authors suggest may not be optimal for biomedical applications.
