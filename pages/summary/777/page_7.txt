Dai et al. segmented the brain into 351 Regions of Interest (ROIs) using a template and calculated functional connectivity through Pearson's correlation. Bohland et al. utilized the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas to segment the brain into 116 ROIs, computing functional connectivity with three correlation methods: Pearson's correlation, sparse regularized inverse covariance, and Patel's Kappa. Eloyan et al. focused on five ROIs from the motor network, employing Pearson's correlation for functional connectivity and subsequent classification. Cheng et al. also used Pearson's and partial correlation for 90 brain regions from the AAL template, incorporating multiple measures like Regional Homogeneity and fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation for classification.

Despite the widespread use of correlation-based approaches in these studies, they fail to adequately characterize the network structure of brain regions, specifically whether they belong to the same functional cluster. Additionally, the dense networks produced by correlation methods may hinder classifier performance. The studies collectively underscore the absence of a consensus on a common brain atlas in fMRI analysis.

In terms of dimensionality reduction, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) is frequently applied to fMRI data. Garcia et al. introduced an ICA-based functional-anatomical discriminative region model for ADHD classification, using ICA to extract brain functional connectivity networks. Tabas et al. presented a variant of ICA to differentiate between healthy controls and ADHD patients, employing 20 independent components and integrating ICA with a spatial variant of Fisher's linear discriminant. ICA methods are regarded as effective for analyzing brain connectivity.
