Graph-based methods have been utilized to model brain networks, particularly in the context of classifying ADHD. Dey et al. introduced a graph-based classification approach where brain connectivity is represented as a graph, with nodes characterized by a set of attributes termed node signatures. They constructed functional networks using correlation and applied a threshold to define the network, although the threshold was chosen arbitrarily and varied across imaging datasets. Similarly, Siqueira et al. explored various graph-based measures for ADHD classification.

Clustering methods are also prominent in evaluating functional connectivity, positing that regions within the same cluster are functionally connected. Research indicates that clustering methods provide a more nuanced analysis compared to correlation-based methods, yielding sparser networks. Recent studies have applied clustering techniques to the ADHD-200 dataset, with Zhang et al. employing k-means clustering for functional connectivity analysis. However, k-means faces challenges due to its reliance on random cluster initialization and the need for prior knowledge about the number of clusters, which is often unknown in fMRI studies. Hierarchical clustering is another option, but it similarly struggles with pre-determined thresholds and cluster counts. Alternative methods, such as affinity propagation (AP) clustering, have been used for brain disorder classification. AP clustering does not require a predefined number of clusters, making it suitable for fMRI data, yet it relies on a similarity measure that can influence the algorithm's outcomes, highlighting limitations in distance measure selection.
