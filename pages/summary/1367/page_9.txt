The paper examines anthropomorphizing behavior in human-robot interaction, distinguishing between two levels of interpretation: proximal and distal scenes. The proximal scene involves semiotic processes where the robot's actions are interpreted as meaningful, such as a robot waving to greet someone. In contrast, the distal scene involves a more fictional interpretation, where the robot is perceived as a social actor, evoking character-like qualities, such as a friend or companion.

The authors argue that while the proximal scene relies on the encoded meaning of the robot's actions, the distal scene requires imagination and a degree of pretending from the observer. This distinction is crucial for analyzing how design features of robots can influence perceptions. For instance, a robot's voice may index a gender, but any associated stereotypes are part of the distal scene, not encoded in the robot's design.

The paper uses the analogy of Mickey Mouse at Disneyland to illustrate these concepts. Just as children can orient their interactions based on the performer in the costume, the depiction of the character, or the character itself, similar dynamics occur in human-robot interactions. The authors propose a depiction model that allows for multiple perspectives—base, proximal, and distal—coexisting and influencing how people engage with robots.

Ultimately, the paper aims to define the anatomy of a 'social cue' in robotics, where design choices and behaviors are intended to evoke specific human-like interactions and characters, thereby enhancing the understanding of human-robot relationships.
