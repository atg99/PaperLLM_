This paper discusses the depiction model in the context of human-robot interactions, illustrating how robots' behavior can be understood on different levels: base, proximal, and distal. The base scene refers to the mechanical actions of robots as artifacts, such as their programmed movements. The proximal scene recognizes these actions as purposeful, attributing anthropomorphic qualities to the robots based on their design or behavior. The distal scene contextualizes these behaviors within fictional narratives, allowing the robot to be perceived as a character with intentions.

Examples provided in the paper demonstrate these concepts. For instance, Nakata et al. (1998) describe a fish-shaped robot that lifts its 'head' in response to touch, which can be categorized as a rebellious act when analyzed through the depiction model. Tielmann et al. (2014) examine a robot designed to express emotions in response to a child's emotional state, adjusting features like speech volume and pitch to match the child's arousal and emotional valence.

The paper emphasizes the importance of using qualitative methods to analyze how people's behaviors are oriented towards these three levels of robot depiction, enhancing our understanding of human-robot interaction dynamics.
