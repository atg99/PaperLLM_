The paper discusses anthropomorphism as a psychological phenomenon where individuals attribute human-like traits to robots. It defines anthropomorphism as an automatic cognitive process that evokes various emotional, behavioral, and cognitive responses towards non-human entities. The author outlines three types of effects identified by Nass and Moon: the overuse of human social categories, the application of learned social behaviors to robots, and premature trust in robots labeled as specialists.

The concept is further explored by distinguishing between three aspects: anthropomorphism as a socio-cognitive phenomenon, anthropomorphic design as the human-like features integrated into robots, and anthropomorphizing behavior, which describes how people respond to robots similarly to how they would respond to humans. Various methods like brain imaging, reaction time studies, questionnaires, and linguistic analyses have been employed to study these aspects. Results indicate that brain regions activate similarly for human and robotic partners based on anthropomorphic design, and people often use human-like descriptors for robots.

However, interactions with robots are not identical to those with humans. Studies show that children engage less with robots than with human experimenters. Additionally, individual differences exist in the tendency to anthropomorphize, with lonely individuals often showing a higher propensity. The paper also suggests that trait attribution may evolve over time, with unique human traits being harder to attribute to robots. The findings highlight the complexity of human-robot interactions and the influence of design and individual differences on anthropomorphism.
