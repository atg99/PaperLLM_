The paper discusses how humans interact with robots by analyzing their responses based on different levels of orientation towards the interaction. It identifies three types of scene orientations: the base scene, where the robot is treated as a mechanical tool; the proximal scene, where the robot's behavior is seen as intentional and meaningful; and the distal scene, where the robot is perceived as human-like. 

For example, when a robot greets a user, the user might respond in various ways that indicate their level of engagement. A response like "I'm fine, thanks" reflects a distal orientation, treating the robot as a conversational partner. In contrast, responding with "drive forward" signals a base scene orientation, viewing the robot merely as a tool. Acknowledging the robot's greeting with a simple "fine" indicates a proximal scene understanding, recognizing the robot's social function without fully engaging in a pretend conversation.

The paper emphasizes that responses to robots can reveal anthropomorphizing behavior, where users attribute human-like qualities to robots. This behavior can manifest through verbal responses, timing of replies, and gaze direction, paralleling human-to-human interactions. The proposed method focuses on observing these behaviors to understand how people anthropomorphize robots, aligning with previous research on human-like responses to technology, but without making assumptions about individuals' subjective experiences.
