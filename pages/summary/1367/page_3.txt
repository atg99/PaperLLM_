The paper discusses the dynamics of anthropomorphizing behavior in human-robot interactions, highlighting a common trend where individuals initially attribute human-like qualities to robots but later perceive them as more machine-like. It notes that human-robot interactions can also include behaviors atypical of human interactions, such as flaming or swearing. The extent of anthropomorphism is influenced by analysis methods and cognitive load during interactions. Despite existing evidence of psychological responses to anthropomorphic design, the paper emphasizes a gap in understanding the temporal dynamics of anthropomorphism during interactions, as most studies have focused on isolated measures or post hoc analyses.

The author proposes a new method to track and describe the progression of anthropomorphizing behavior over time, which could integrate findings on interpersonal variations and anti-social behaviors. The paper also reviews existing methods for studying anthropomorphism, including questionnaires, experimental behavioral elicitation, brain imaging, and reaction time studies. Questionnaires, in particular, have been widely used to assess the impact of robot design on anthropomorphism, examining attributes like aggression, intelligence, and social characteristics. The author aims to contribute to the understanding of how anthropomorphizing behavior evolves in human-robot interactions and its implications for explanatory models of anthropomorphism.
