The paper investigates the variability in how participants anthropomorphize robots during human-robot interactions. The study involved 11 participants who engaged with a robot while their attention to different scenes (base, proximal, and distal) was tracked. Results indicated significant intra- and interpersonal variation in responses, with no two participants reacting similarly. Notably, none of the participants treated the robot as a social actor throughout the interaction, as evidenced by the lack of exclusive attention to the distal scene. Some participants did not anthropomorphize the robot at all.

The findings align with prior research on interpersonal variation in treating robots as social actors. A comparison with previous questionnaire data revealed that participants rated the robot's human-like characteristics moderately, with scores ranging from 2.91 to 5.88 on a 7-point Likert scale. The only significant difference between conditions was related to the robot's perceived friendliness. The paper emphasizes that while questionnaire results provide post-interaction impressions, the detailed analysis of participants' real-time responses offers deeper insights into anthropomorphizing behaviors and the impact of design choices, such as social framing utterances.
