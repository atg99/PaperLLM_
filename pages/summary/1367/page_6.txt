The paper discusses the prevalence of anthropomorphism in participants' verbal behaviors during interactions with robots, emphasizing the importance of ethnographic analyses. It highlights research by Forlizzi, which documents social attributions made to robots, and a study by Chun and Knight that explores employees' interactions with robots, focusing on their perceptions of robots' gender and personality. The paper also reviews behavioral studies that measure reaction times when participants attribute social characteristics to robots versus humans, noting phenomena such as the Social Simon Effect, which demonstrates motor interference based on perceived robot agency. Klapper et al.'s research further explores how anthropomorphic design influences participants' motor responses, revealing that beliefs about a robot's intelligence can affect behavior. The conclusion asserts that while behavioral measures can provide insights into anthropomorphism, they often capture only isolated behaviors, lacking the ability to track the dynamic progression of anthropomorphism over time.
