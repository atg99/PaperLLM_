The paper discusses various methodologies for studying anthropomorphism in robots, particularly focusing on the use of questionnaires and behavioral measures. It highlights notable questionnaires, such as the Godspeed Questionnaire and the Robotic Social Attributes Scale, which are designed to assess the features people attribute to robots based on their design and presentation. Additionally, the paper references studies that utilize questionnaires to explore social categorization, where participants rated robots differently based on whether they were presented as in-group or out-group members.

The paper also examines how questionnaires can measure the effects of robot behaviors and framing on anthropomorphism, illustrating that the way a robot is introduced can significantly influence user perception and interaction experience. Furthermore, it notes that questionnaires can assess participants' assumptions about a robot's knowledge based on its origin and interaction language.

In contrast, the paper emphasizes the value of behavioral measures, which capture anthropomorphism in real-time during human-robot interactions. This approach allows researchers to observe immediate responses to social cues produced by robots, mirroring human interaction patterns. The paper argues for a comprehensive understanding of anthropomorphism by integrating both questionnaire data and behavioral observations.
