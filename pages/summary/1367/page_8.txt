The paper discusses the classification of human behavior in relation to anthropomorphism, proposing a minimalistic versus maximalist approach. A minimalistic classification simply categorizes behaviors as either anthropomorphizing or not, while a maximalist version considers the type and extent of anthropomorphism. The feasibility of such classifications depends on observable behaviors and suggests maintaining a limited number of distinctions for clarity.

The author advocates using a model of depiction, as proposed by Clark, to analyze human responses to robots without assuming the underlying causes of these behaviors. Previous research presents varied perspectives on anthropomorphism: some view it as a mindless response to technology, others as a predisposition, and some as social practice. To avoid circular reasoning, the paper employs a theory-neutral model, focusing on Clark’s three levels of depiction: the base scene, proximal scene, and distal scene. 

In the context of human-robot interaction, the base scene refers to the robot's mechanical properties, while the proximal scene involves the encoded meaning of the robot's behavior, which can be analyzed through signaling relationships. The paper explains how behaviors can be interpreted through indexical, iconic, and symbolic signs, ultimately highlighting the complexity of human-robot interactions and the importance of understanding the meanings attributed to robotic behaviors.
