The paper discusses the dynamics of anthropomorphizing behavior in human-robot interaction (HRI), highlighting that such behaviors are not solely triggered by specific robot actions or appearances. The authors argue that anthropomorphism occurs in varying degrees, as illustrated by participant responses that fluctuate between attributing human-like intentions to robots and recognizing their mechanical nature. The findings challenge existing psychological and social constructivist theories that suggest a consistent disposition towards anthropomorphism, as they reveal significant intrapersonal variation over time.

The study introduces a method to analyze these behaviors and their implications for robot design, suggesting that understanding the subtle effects of social cues can inform real-time adaptations in robot behavior. This method adds to existing research on anthropomorphism in HRI, demonstrating that individuals can easily shift between anthropomorphizing and non-anthropomorphizing perspectives based on contextual triggers. Future research is needed to explore the relationship between these behaviors and users' perceptions of robots.
