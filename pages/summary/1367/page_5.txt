The paper explores the concept of anthropomorphizing behavior in human-robot interactions, drawing on foundational research that highlights how people often apply social rules to technology. It emphasizes observable human behaviors, such as preferences for spatial distance to robots, as indicators of the degree of anthropomorphism. For instance, studies by Ham et al. and Kahn et al. illustrate how cognitive load and robot design affect human interactions, revealing tendencies like compliance with robot requests or willingness to deceive for the robot's sake.

The research also examines single measurable responses, such as physical distance or verbal behavior, to identify conditions that foster anthropomorphism. Notably, some studies analyze participants' verbal responses to social cues from robots, indicating that people often use similar language to describe human and robot behavior, suggesting a level of anthropomorphism. Methods include observing interactions and analyzing speech patterns, focusing on politeness and relevance, which reflect how individuals perceive and relate to robots.

Ultimately, the paper argues that understanding these anthropomorphizing tendencies is crucial for designing effective human-robot interactions, highlighting the need for further investigation into the dynamics of anthropomorphism over time.
