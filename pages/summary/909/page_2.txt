The paper introduces FastAP, a metric learning method that utilizes a single neural network and demonstrates significant performance improvements over ensemble metric learning methods, which are typically expensive to train and evaluate. It begins by discussing the landscape of metric learning, particularly focusing on the parameterization of distance functions, with a strong emphasis on Mahalanobis distances and the growing interest in deep neural networks for nonlinear metric learning.

The authors highlight the common use of pair-based and triplet-based approaches that optimize loss functions to learn distance metrics. These methods often face challenges in scalability due to the infeasibility of enumerating all possible pairs or triplets in large datasets, leading to the development of various sampling heuristics to identify "hard" examples. Despite these efforts, existing approaches tend to rely on fixed thresholds that lack flexibility.

The paper proposes a novel perspective by revisiting the learning to rank framework within deep metric learning and optimizing Average Precision (AP) over entire ranked lists. This listwise approach emphasizes the ordering in the metric space and prioritizes performance at the top of the ranking. However, optimizing AP poses challenges due to its non-decomposable nature and the difficulties associated with differentiating through sorting operations.

To address these challenges, the authors introduce a new approximation of Average Precision that scales linearly with the list size through distance quantization and a differentiable relaxation of histogram binning. Unlike previous methods, their approach directly targets the Euclidean metric in a general metric learning setup, allowing for large-batch training beyond GPU memory constraints.

The paper concludes by outlining the learning to rank framework with Average Precision in a standard information retrieval context, where a neural network is trained to embed inputs into a Euclidean space optimized for AP. The authors derive the precision-recall curve and the formulation for AP, providing a foundation for further exploration of their method.
