This paper investigates the effects of various clipping methods and learning rate scaling strategies on INT8 training, focusing on their implications for model accuracy and computational efficiency. An ablation study reveals that gradient clipping without a clipping method results in degraded performance, with accuracy reaching only 93.02%. The study emphasizes that exponential scaling outperforms linear and quadratic forms, which struggle to maintain optimization within convergence boundaries. The sensitivity of the hyperparameters α and β is examined, showing stability in accuracy across different settings.

The authors propose a periodic update for clipping values, which reduces computational overhead without significantly impacting accuracy. Results indicate that combining gradient clipping with learning rate scaling enhances performance, achieving an accuracy of 93.66% with a period of one.

In the domain of image classification, the paper evaluates several architectures including AlexNet, ResNet, MobileNetV2, and InceptionV3 on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet datasets. The proposed method shows comparable accuracy to FP8 training while consuming less memory and computation. Notably, it achieves a 5.84% improvement over DoReFa-Net for AlexNet and outperforms WAGE in the 2-bit weight and 8-bit activation/gradient scenario.

The authors highlight their method's efficiency, requiring no specialized hardware, making it suitable for mainstream devices. They focus on quantizing convolutional layers, which dominate training time, and report significant speedups while maintaining accuracy comparable to full precision training. The paper claims to be the first to successfully apply INT8 training to the gradients of MobileNetV2 and InceptionV3, achieving minimal accuracy loss.

Finally, the versatility of their approach is demonstrated through experiments on object detection networks like Faster-RCNN, RFCN, and RetinaNet across PASCAL VOC and COCO datasets, indicating the method's broad applicability beyond image classification.
