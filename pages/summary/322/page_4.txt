The paper investigates the behavior of attention heads in the BERT model, particularly focusing on their attention towards the special token [SEP]. It presents findings that challenge the hypothesis that attention heads should broadly attend to the entire segment to form representations. Instead, over 90% of the attention is directed towards [SEP] and itself, suggesting that this attention may serve as a “no-op” when the attention head's intended function is not applicable. Gradient-based measures show that in layer 5, where attention to [SEP] increases, the gradients become small, indicating minimal impact on outputs when varying attention to [SEP].

The research also distinguishes between focused and broad attention among heads, measuring the entropy of attention distributions. Lower-layer heads exhibit broad attention, with high entropy, while the last layer's [CLS] token shows a high entropy of 3.89 nats, aligning with its role in aggregating input representations for tasks like next sentence prediction.

The study probes individual attention heads to determine their learned language aspects, particularly in dependency parsing tasks. It converts token-level attention maps to word-level, preserving the sum of attention weights. Evaluating attention heads on the Wall Street Journal portion of the Penn Treebank reveals no single head performs well overall in syntax prediction, with the best achieving a UAS of 34.5, only slightly better than a simple baseline. However, some heads specialize in specific dependency relations, indicating a nuanced understanding of syntax within the model.
