The paper discusses the architecture and functioning of CapsNet, as proposed by Sabour et al. in 2017. The CapsNet architecture is divided into two main components. The first half focuses on class prediction through three processing layers: two convolutional layers and a fully connected layer. The initial convolutional layer transforms a 28x28 image into a 6x6x256 volume using a 9x9 kernel, followed by a convolution capsule layer called PrimaryCaps that generates a 6x6x256 volume, which is then segmented into 32 layers of 8-dimensional capsules, resulting in 6x6x32 capsules. The final layer, DigitCaps, consists of 10 capsules, each with 16 dimensions, representing different digit classes. Dynamic routing occurs between the PrimaryCaps and DigitCaps layers, and the output prediction is based on the capsule with the highest magnitude.

The second half of the model, known as the decoder, serves the purpose of reconstruction as a form of regularization. It concatenates the 16-dimensional embeddings from each class while masking the components of the non-winning class, resulting in a total embedding length of 160. This embedding is processed through three fully connected layers, ultimately producing a 784-dimensional output reshaped into a 28x28 reconstruction of the original image.

To train the network, a two-part loss function is utilized. The first component penalizes incorrect predictions based on the DigitCaps embeddings, while the second component addresses reconstruction error by measuring the difference between the original image and its reconstructed version. The paper emphasizes the differentiability of dynamic routing, allowing for backpropagation to minimize the loss.

In conclusion, the authors intend to further explore the design decisions behind CapsNets' implementation on the MNIST dataset, setting the stage for deeper analysis and discussion in subsequent sections.
