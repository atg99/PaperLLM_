In the paper "Characterizing and Overcoming the Greedy Nature of Learning in Multi-Modal Deep Neural Networks," the authors explore the challenges posed by the tendency of multi-modal deep neural networks to favor one modality over others during training, a behavior they term as "greedy learning." 

To address this, the authors introduce a training strategy that alternates between regular training steps and re-balancing steps based on the imbalance tolerance parameter, α. When the speed of learning exceeds this threshold, the model switches to a re-balancing mode for a specified number of steps, Q, before returning to regular training. This method aims to mitigate the bias towards a single modality.

The experiments utilize three datasets: Colored-and-gray-MNIST, ModelNet40, and NVGesture. Each dataset presents unique challenges and demonstrates the greedy learner hypothesis, where models tend to rely heavily on one modality. The Colored-and-gray-MNIST dataset serves as a controlled example, showing that uni-modal networks perform significantly differently on correlated versus uncorrelated modalities. ModelNet40 and NVGesture further illustrate this phenomenon, with results indicating a consistent tendency for models to exploit one modality while neglecting others.

The findings reveal that in multi-modal setups with identical inputs, the distribution of utility differences is symmetric around zero, suggesting a lack of preference. In contrast, distinct modalities show an asymmetric distribution, confirming the greedy nature of the learning process. The authors conjecture that there exists a threshold ε such that the expected utility difference exceeds ε when modalities are distinct, supporting their hypothesis.

Overall, the paper emphasizes the importance of addressing the greedy nature of learning in multi-modal networks to enhance their performance across various tasks while proposing a practical approach to re-balance the learning process.
