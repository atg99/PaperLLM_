The background section provides an overview of the history and development of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems, along with the datasets and evaluation techniques used in the field. 

The journey of ASR began in the early 1950s with pioneering work by researchers aiming to create machines capable of understanding and reproducing human speech. The first ASR system, named Audrey, developed at Bell Laboratories, could recognize digits spoken by a single user. In 1959, a system at MIT Lincoln Laboratories could identify ten phonemes for one speaker. Significant advancements occurred in the 1970s, including a Russian-developed system for word distinction and the introduction of dynamic programming and pattern recognition algorithms. The early 1980s saw the emergence of the hidden Markov model (HMM), which, despite its simplicity, replaced previous techniques like dynamic time warping. The n-gram model followed in the late 1980s, and by the early 2000s, HMMs were combined with feed-forward artificial neural networks. Currently, long-short term memory networks, a type of recurrent neural network, are utilized alongside various deep learning approaches for ASR.

Evaluation techniques are crucial for assessing ASR performance, focusing on output accuracy and processing speed. The section also references various datasets used for training and testing ASR systems, underscoring the importance of these metrics in ASR research.
