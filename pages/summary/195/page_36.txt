The paper discusses the role of rewards in reinforcement learning (RL) and their function as evaluative feedback for decision-making by RL agents. It highlights the challenges posed by sparse rewards, such as those found in computer Go, where feedback is only received at the end of a game. To address these challenges, methods like hindsight experience replay and unsupervised auxiliary learning are introduced.

Reward shaping is presented as a technique to modify reward functions to enhance learning while preserving the optimal policy. The paper cites Ng et al. (2000), who demonstrate that potential-based reward shaping can maintain policy optimality. While reward shaping is often a manual process, Jaderberg et al. (2018) propose an end-to-end learning approach.

In scenarios where reward functions are unavailable, imitation learning becomes relevant, allowing agents to learn tasks from expert demonstrations without direct reinforcement signals. The two primary imitation learning methods discussed are behavioral cloning and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL). Behavioral cloning maps expert state-action pairs to a policy without learning the reward function. The challenges of distributional shift in pure imitation learning and the exploration difficulties in pure RL are addressed, suggesting a hybrid approach that combines both methods.

IRL is defined as the process of inferring reward functions based on observations of optimal behavior. Various approaches to IRL are explored, including maximum entropy methods to manage uncertainty in noisy demonstrations. The paper also highlights potential issues with reward function design, such as unintended consequences arising from misaligned objectives, illustrated through examples like King Midas and a vacuum cleaner optimizing for dust collection.

The paper concludes by outlining fundamental issues in reward systems, including distinctions between primary and secondary rewards, intrinsic and extrinsic motivations, and providing a computational framework for optimal rewards. It references various tutorials and surveys related to imitation learning and IRL, setting the stage for further discussion on RL methods and complex reward functions.
