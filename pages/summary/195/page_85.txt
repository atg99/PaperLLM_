The paper argues that current advancements in science, engineering, and technology, including artificial intelligence, are insufficient for the safe road testing of fully autonomous vehicles. A critical concern highlighted is the issue of adversarial examples, which poses challenges to the reliability of autonomous systems. Statistics from the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety indicate that human drivers in the US experience approximately one fatality per 100 million vehicle miles. This raises important questions regarding the decision-making capabilities and accuracy required from autonomous systems, particularly in real-time scenarios.

The authors suggest that existing simulators do not adequately reflect real-world conditions, and the data from road tests conducted thus far are not statistically significant enough to support claims of safety. They emphasize that road tests function as experiments involving human oversight. To enhance safety, the paper advocates for a model where a driver remains in the vehicle, attentive and ready to take control if necessary. Furthermore, it proposes that fully self-driving vehicles undergo testing in controlled environments, using robots as pedestrians, until AI achieves a level of intelligence that includes scene understanding and common sense.
