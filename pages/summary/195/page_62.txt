Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) combines multi-agent systems with reinforcement learning, operating at the intersection of game theory and AI. It faces challenges such as sparse rewards, sample efficiency, multiple equilibria, and the fundamental question of whether convergence to an equilibrium is a suitable goal. MARL requires a clear understanding of the problems to be solved and coherent research agendas.

In centralized approaches, where global state and joint action information are available, estimating joint Q action value functions can mitigate nonstationarity issues. However, this approach suffers from the curse of dimensionality as the number of agents increases and can complicate the extraction of decentralized policies. 

Early work in MARL includes Littman's stochastic games framework, which proposed minimax-Q learning for zero-sum games, and Hu and Wellman's Nash Q-learning for general-sum games, both showing convergence under specific conditions. Tan's independent Q-learning (IQL) allows agents to learn independently, but stability is often compromised due to the dynamic nature of multi-agent environments. 

Recent advancements in MARL have followed the paradigm of centralized training for decentralized execution, yielding promising results in various applications, including complex games like Quake III Arena and StarCraft II, as well as in robotics and resource management. Notable contributions include learning with opponent-learning awareness and dynamic safe interruptibility, which enhance the adaptability of agents in competitive environments. 

MARL's applications span diverse fields, including gaming, robotics, energy management, transportation, and computing systems, highlighting its relevance and potential impact in multi-agent interactions.
