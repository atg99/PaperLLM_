The paper discusses advancements in hierarchical reinforcement learning (RL) through various architectures and methodologies. 

Feudal Networks (FuNs), proposed by Vezhnevets et al. in 2017, introduce a hierarchical framework where a Manager module sets abstract sub-goals at a long time scale while a Worker module selects actions to achieve those sub-goals. This separation allows for effective credit assignment and the development of sub-policies, with sub-goals represented as directions in latent space. The Manager employs a dilated LSTM for efficient backpropagation across time steps, demonstrating effectiveness in environments like water mazes and Atari games.

The Option-Critic architecture, introduced by Bacon et al. in 2017, learns both intra-option policies and termination conditions simultaneously, integrating options discovery with policy learning. This architecture is versatile, handling various state and action spaces without requiring rewards or sub-goals. Subsequent work by Harutyunyan et al. in 2018 focuses on balancing the efficiency of long options with the flexibility of short ones, promoting multi-step off-policy learning for options.

Machado et al. (2017) explore option discovery using proto-value functions (PVFs), defining intrinsic reward functions (eigen-purposes) that guide agents through learned state representations. Their findings indicate that while some discovered options enhance planning efficiency, they may not aid exploration. However, these options, operating at various time scales, can facilitate better exploration strategies.

Lastly, Vezhnevets et al. (2016) present the Strategic Attentive Writer (STRAW), a deep recurrent neural network that learns high-level macro-actions based on environmental observations. STRAW autonomously discovers macro-actions and constructs multi-step action plans, validated through applications in text prediction, maze navigation, and Atari games.

Overall, these contributions highlight the evolution of hierarchical RL and the importance of structuring decision-making processes for improved learning efficiency and exploration.
