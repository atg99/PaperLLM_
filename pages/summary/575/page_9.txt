The paper discusses the application of deep transfer learning (DTL) techniques in smart industry fault diagnosis, achieving a high accuracy of 99.82%. This performance surpasses traditional methods such as deep belief networks, sparse filters, deep learning, and support vector machines. The authors note that while tailored DTL approaches may not be easily generalizable across different tasks or datasets, they pave the way for innovative techniques in the field.

The paper reviews significant experimental studies on DTL, particularly focusing on a recent investigation into the mechanisms behind successful transfer learning. This study compares different network scenarios: pre-trained, randomly initialized, fine-tuned on the target domain after pretraining, and trained from random initialization. The analysis uses IMAGENET as the source domain and various target domains with visual similarities, such as DOMAINNET and CHEXPERT.

Key findings indicate that feature reuse is crucial for DTL success, especially when the target domain shares visual characteristics with the source domain. Pre-trained models show substantial performance improvements compared to randomly initialized models. However, the study also reveals that feature reuse is not the sole factor contributing to DTL effectiveness, as performance boosts are observed even with distant target domains.

The analysis of common errors between pre-trained and randomly initialized models highlights that pre-trained models possess a stronger prior, making them less adaptable to target domains. The paper concludes that the initialization point significantly influences feature similarities and that pre-trained models tend to converge faster than their randomly initialized counterparts.

Additionally, the study explores performance barriers in the loss landscape, finding that fine-tuning a pre-trained model keeps it within the same solution basin, while randomly initialized models can end up in different basins even with the same random values. The analysis also touches on module criticality within deep learning models, suggesting that different layers or components may have varying levels of importance in the learning process.
