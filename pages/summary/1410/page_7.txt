The paper discusses two main types of object detectors: two-stage and single-stage detectors, highlighting their architectures and performance characteristics. Two-stage detectors first generate region proposals and then classify and localize objects, resulting in longer processing times and more complex structures. In contrast, single-stage detectors perform classification and localization in one step using dense sampling, which enhances real-time performance and simplifies design.

The paper also reviews pioneering work in object detection, beginning with the Viola-Jones detector, designed for face detection in 2001. This detector employs Haar-like features, integral images, Adaboost, and cascading classifiers, making it efficient and fast, suitable for small devices.

Next, the Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) detector, introduced by Dalal and Triggs in 2005, improves feature extraction by analyzing edge gradients and orientations. It divides images into grids to create histograms for each cell, primarily used for pedestrian detection but adaptable for various classes.

Finally, the Deformable Parts Model (DPM), presented by Felzenszwalb et al. in 2009, detects objects by identifying individual parts and their probable arrangements, achieving higher accuracy than HOG. DPM models were notably successful before the advent of deep learning. 

Overall, the paper emphasizes the evolution and effectiveness of these traditional object detection methods within the broader context of modern deep learning techniques.
