The paper discusses the concept of swarm behavior, emphasizing that a collection of individuals qualifies as a swarm if it demonstrates specific characteristics such as adaptability, robustness, and scalability. It highlights two essential features that are critical for true swarm systems: critical mass and super-linear scaling of system performance.

Critical mass refers to the necessity of reaching a minimum number of agents for effective swarm performance. It is illustrated by natural examples, such as honeybees and cockroaches, where the collective decision-making process is significantly influenced by the density of participants. Below a certain threshold, swarm systems fail to operate collectively, akin to the formation of a sand dune, where a few grains cannot create a stable structure despite following the same physical laws as a larger number.

Super-linear performance scaling indicates that a larger group of agents should perform more effectively than a smaller group within the same timeframe. In true swarms, the interactions among members should yield a combined effect that exceeds the sum of individual contributions. This concept is supported by examples from natural swarms, robotic swarms, and multi-processor systems, illustrating that cooperative synergies enhance overall productivity.

The paper concludes that while critical mass and super-linear scaling are recognized as integral to swarm behavior, the precise critical threshold for the minimum number of swarm members remains unclear.
