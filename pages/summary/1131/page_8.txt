The paper discusses the importance of considering locality of interaction and overcrowding when building models for swarm intelligence (SI) systems, particularly in the context of physically embodied agents like robots and honeybees. It emphasizes that simple models often fail in real-world applications due to issues such as traffic jams and collision prevention, which can hinder the effectiveness of the swarm's algorithm. The paper cites an example where an ODE-based model of ant pheromone trails did not translate effectively to a robotic system.

To be classified as swarm-intelligent, an algorithm must demonstrate improved performance with increasing swarm size, within practical limits. The authors propose a test consisting of two questions to evaluate swarm intelligence: whether the system operates effectively with a single agent and whether performance per agent declines as swarm size increases. Only systems that answer "No" to both questions qualify as truly swarm-intelligent.

The paper also explores the sources of inspiration for SI algorithms, noting that while they are generally seen as bio-inspired, many recent algorithms draw from a variety of processes beyond natural systems. Examples include the Fireworks algorithm and Swarm Chemistry Particle algorithms, which exhibit SI properties without direct biological inspiration. Conversely, some bio-inspired approaches, such as evolutionary computation and self-organizing neural networks, are not classified as SI despite their collective behavior capabilities. The distinction arises from the historical focus of SI on algorithms derived from animal group behaviors.
