The foundational definition of a swarm as a group of animals moving together has evolved to encompass complex collective behaviors observed in nature, such as foraging and nest-building, where individuals follow simple rules and locally interact to achieve global goals.

Key characteristics of SI are derived from natural systems, where individual members are often unable to solve problems alone but can collectively find effective solutions. The paper emphasizes that SI is not merely about defining the concept but also about exploring its properties and implications. It cites Bonabeau and Meyer’s definition, highlighting self-organization and efficiency in social insects' interactions leading to swarm intelligence. 

The authors argue that successful SI algorithms, such as Particle Swarm Optimization and Ant Colony Optimization, are inspired by natural behaviors and are designed to replicate properties such as adaptability, robustness, and scalability. These properties enable swarms to function effectively in dynamic environments, withstand disturbances, and maintain performance despite changes in membership size. The study of swarm robotics extends these principles to embodied agents, focusing on collective task accomplishment beyond individual capabilities. Overall, the paper aims to deepen the understanding of the conditions and interactions that facilitate swarm behaviors, with implications for both natural and artificial systems.
