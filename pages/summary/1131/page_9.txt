The paper discusses the broad spectrum of sources of inspiration for swarm intelligence (SI) algorithms, highlighting that not all biological systems exhibit swarm-like behaviors. It emphasizes that various nature-inspired algorithms draw from physical or chemical laws, rather than exclusively biological ones. Examples include simulated annealing, spiral optimization, water cycle algorithms, and galaxy-based algorithms.

The text argues that categorizing these diverse approaches is challenging due to the blurred lines between them. Instead of relying on strict definitions based on specific biological systems, the authors suggest characterizing SI algorithms by their collective properties and functionalities. This involves understanding the underlying mechanisms of complex systems, which may consist of interacting components that are not necessarily living organisms.

Key collective capabilities identified as sources of inspiration for SI include:

1. **Collective Decision Making**: This involves systems that can make collective choices, such as ants using pheromones to find the shortest paths or bees selecting optimal foraging areas. The focus is on understanding the microscopic mechanisms that lead to these collective behaviors.

2. **Collective Regulation and Homeostasis**: This feature refers to systems that maintain specific variables within efficient limits, often seen in social animals. Identifying the negative feedback loops that facilitate regulation is crucial for understanding how homeostasis is achieved at the population level.

3. **Collective Periodic Patterns**: Some complex systems exhibit dynamic state changes over time, producing spatial patterns like circular or spiral waves. These patterns can be utilized for collective computation and signaling, influenced by communication delays or internal state changes among agents.

In conclusion, the paper asserts that the definition of SI algorithms is increasingly based on their operational performance rather than their sources of inspiration. Inspiration can be drawn from any complex system with interacting components that demonstrate useful collective properties, extending beyond natural systems.
