The extractive approach to text processing relies on syntactic structures and phrases from the original text, manipulating only the input content. During the 2000s, manual annotation methods were used, but they yielded poor quality results due to the limitations of extractive techniques. For instance, a model created in 2005 for annotating corporate meeting minutes performed significantly worse than human processing. Various algorithms were attempted to improve performance, including those based on rhetorical information, TextRank, naive Bayesian classifiers, and TF-IDF metrics, but none fully utilized deep learning.

The development of recurrent neural networks, effective for word processing, required large datasets and extensive training, but still struggled with long text sequences and overfitting. The introduction of the transformer architecture in 2017 shifted the focus to feedforward networks with attention mechanisms, leading to significant advancements in natural language processing. The BERT model exemplified this shift, enhancing text annotation capabilities through variable-length annotations and vector representations, although challenges like the difficulty in isolating indicative sentences remain.

Evaluating text annotation models is complicated by the absence of standardized metrics, necessitating manual quality assessments. Despite BERT's improvements in perceived annotation quality, the research area of annotating long texts and developing effective evaluation metrics continues to be promising.

Additionally, the paper discusses the challenge of assessing text generation quality, particularly in machine translation. Traditional metrics like BLEU focus on superficial n-gram similarities, often missing the semantic diversity of language. BERT’s representation facilitates a more nuanced measurement of sentence similarity, relying on the distance between vectorized representations. Effective metrics should closely align with human judgment, which has led to classifications of existing metrics into n-gram match, edit distance, attachment comparison, and trained functions. While higher n-grams capture word order and similarity better, they also become limited by specific formulations, exemplified by BLEU and MeTEOR metrics.
