This study investigates the performance of a convolutional neural network (CNN) model for detecting CT abnormalities in COVID-19 patients, utilizing a federated learning approach. The model was trained on a substantial dataset of 4146 CT slices, outperforming two other models trained on smaller datasets across three external testing sites. The results suggest that larger and more diverse training datasets can enhance model performance and generalizability, highlighting the importance of collaboration among clinical centers, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Despite the recruitment of patients from seven clinical centers, there was an imbalance in the number of cases from each site, with some centers experiencing higher patient volumes. This disparity reflects the real-world challenges in training AI models due to the scarcity of COVID-19 patients at individual sites. The study emphasizes the need for multicenter studies and collaborative data efforts to address such challenges.

The model showed variable performance across different cohorts, with reduced generalizability observed in the German cohort, potentially due to demographic differences and variations in lesion annotation procedures, leading to concept shifts in the data. The study excluded certain cases to improve model performance, achieving an area under the curve (AUC) of 88.15% with a limited number of cases.

The authors envision the AI tool complementing expert radiologists' assessments, providing quantitative support during clinical decision-making while maintaining a human oversight to prevent erroneous predictions. Future studies employing federated learning with standardized procedures across sites could mitigate issues related to concept shifts.

In conclusion, the federated learning-based CNN model demonstrates effectiveness in detecting CT abnormalities in COVID-19 patients, with promising generalizability across diverse datasets. This approach offers potential for scalable AI tools in clinical disease management, despite the challenges faced in multicenter studies. Ethical approvals for the study were obtained from the relevant committees of participating hospitals in Hong Kong, and the federated learning process enabled privacy protection while leveraging data from multiple centers.
