The paper discusses various methodologies for uncertainty quantification (UQ) in deep learning (DL) applications, particularly in materials science. Techniques such as Monte-Carlo dropout and deep ensembles are highlighted as effective ways to generate statistical distributions of model outputs, thus providing mean and variance estimates for predictions. Monte-Carlo dropout involves randomizing the network during training and prediction to capture uncertainty, while deep ensembles leverage multiple models to enhance predictability and evaluate aleatory uncertainty.

Quantile regression is presented as another method to estimate prediction intervals, allowing for the prediction of specific quantiles to derive confidence intervals, although it typically requires multiple model runs. The use of Gaussian processes is also discussed, emphasizing their ability to provide UQ information without additional computational costs. However, challenges remain, such as the computational demands of Bayesian methods and the limitations of standard kernels.

The paper identifies several limitations and challenges in applying DL to materials design, including issues with data reliability, the inadequacy of chemical formula-based representations, and the need for predictive input features. It highlights the difficulties in working with noisy experimental data and the need for improved interpretability in DL models to gain trust within the materials community.

Furthermore, the paper points out the importance of developing best practices for data handling in regression models specific to materials science and emphasizes the technological challenge of creating closed-loop autonomous materials design and synthesis processes. The authors conclude by indicating that while significant progress has been made, further advancements are necessary to fully realize the potential of DL in materials science. Data availability is also addressed, stating that new figures can be requested from the corresponding author.
