The paper discusses the ethical implications of defining legal personhood for robots and highlights the necessity of a virtue ethics approach in addressing the complexities of human-robot interactions. It posits that ethics should precede technological definitions, emphasizing the relational aspects of virtue ethics rather than individualistic solutions. The author critiques the philosophical and legal frameworks that prioritize abstract individualism, suggesting that this framework has led to a distorted understanding of legal personhood.

The concept of electronic personhood is explored through a pragmatic lens, arguing that legal personhood is a functional construct rather than inherently linked to human characteristics. The paper asserts that the notion of legal person is a simplification that facilitates legal processes and interactions, allowing for the allocation of rights and duties without needing to reference individual stakeholders continuously.

Moreover, the paper discusses the implications of treating robots as legal persons, drawing parallels with corporate personhood while cautioning against oversimplification. It highlights potential issues, such as the risk of evading accountability among programmers and engineers, and questions whether the corporate model adequately addresses the ethical and legal challenges posed by artificial intelligence. Ultimately, the paper advocates for a nuanced understanding of legal personhood that recognizes the complexities involved in attributing rights and obligations to non-human entities.
