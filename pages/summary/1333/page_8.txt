The paper explores the implications of legal personhood in relation to anthropocentrism and the individualistic model that defines both natural and legal persons. It questions whether legal personhood can effectively combat anthropocentrism or if it merely reinforces outdated frameworks centered on subjective rights and ownership. The author critiques the notion of an abstract legal subject that detaches individuals from their socio-economic and environmental contexts, advocating for a reconceptualization of personhood that acknowledges these connections.

Historical cases, such as People’s Pleasure Park Co. v. Rohleder and Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad, illustrate how legal personhood has been applied in ways that challenge or uphold social injustices. The paper highlights the Supreme Court's controversial ruling in Hobby Lobby, which prioritized the religious beliefs of corporate shareholders over the rights of female employees, raising concerns about the balance between corporate personhood and individual rights.

The author emphasizes the need to differentiate between natural persons and legal entities, arguing against the conflation of the two. The discussion extends to the potential recognition of electronic personhood for robots, suggesting that while metaphors in legal language are powerful, their implications must be critically examined, particularly in jurisdictions like Brazil, where legal personhood remains incomplete.

In conclusion, the paper asserts that while the notion of robots as legal persons is gaining traction, it is essential to confront ongoing human rights issues and the complexities of legal personhood before extending rights to artificial entities. The author calls for a careful reconsideration of the metaphors used in legal discourse to ensure that the distinctions between natural and legal persons are maintained and that the rights of actual human beings are prioritized.
