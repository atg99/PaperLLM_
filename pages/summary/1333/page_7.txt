The paper discusses the challenges and implications of conferring legal personhood to companies and, more recently, to artificial intelligence (AI) entities. Galgano (2010) highlights the significant drawbacks in the legal treatment of companies, emphasizing that the term "legal person" has been misused, leading to a unitary approach that overlooks the diversity of entities involved. This has resulted in a distortion of legal functions and a misunderstanding of shareholder liability, where not all creditors can negotiate their risks adequately.

Ferro-Luzzi (2001) argues that the concept of activity, crucial for understanding business, has been wrongly conflated with abstract subjectivity, complicating legal regulation. The paper critiques the emerging notion of electronic personhood, suggesting that it may replicate existing issues by treating diverse operational contexts of robots under a single legal framework. Such an approach fails to recognize the specific ethical and legal challenges posed by different applications of robotics, from medical use to military operations.

The authors assert that accountability based on this new form of personhood may obscure the true sources of responsibility, particularly those of programmers and developers. They argue that existing legal doctrines, like piercing the corporate veil, are inadequate for addressing these complexities. Instead, they advocate for differentiated liability mechanisms that are sensitive to the varied uses of robotic technologies.

The paper also references the European Commission's 2021 Proposal for a Regulation on Artificial Intelligence, which adopts a risk-based approach rather than creating electronic legal personalities. This proposal is part of a broader discussion on recognizing new legal actors, including non-human entities, as seen in recent legal developments in New Zealand and India concerning the personhood of rivers.

Overall, the paper calls for a reevaluation of legal frameworks to better accommodate the nuances of emerging technologies and their implications for accountability and responsibility.
