The paper examines the concept of autonomy in robotic applications, distinguishing between strong and weak forms of autonomy. Bertolini identifies three meanings of autonomy: consciousness or self-consciousness, independent interaction in an operational environment, and the capacity to learn. Strong autonomy implies moral agency, which is not currently applicable to robots, while weak autonomy refers to the ability to perform tasks without human supervision. The paper critiques the anthropocentric view of autonomy, suggesting that no existing robotic systems fulfill the criteria for strong autonomy.

The discussion also includes the concept of emergent behavior in complex adaptive systems, where actions arise from individual interactions rather than being explicitly programmed. This leads to an illusion of central coordination, complicating the predictability of robotic behavior. The paper emphasizes that unexpected behaviors can still be influenced by the robot's input, highlighting the importance of understanding machine learning, particularly deep learning, in relation to robotic autonomy. 

Machine learning enables robots to acquire knowledge and improve their performance over time, but it raises questions about whether this constitutes real learning. The paper critiques the anthropomorphic language surrounding AI and robotics, noting that such rhetoric can obscure the functional capabilities of these technologies. Overall, the paper calls for a clearer understanding of autonomy in the context of robotics and AI, moving beyond simplistic comparisons to human agency.
