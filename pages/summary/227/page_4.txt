The paper investigates various methods for analyzing the generalization capabilities of deep neural networks, emphasizing the significance of scale normalization. It introduces a definition of sharpness and connects it to the PAC-Bayes theory, demonstrating how these theoretical frameworks can explain experimental observations.

Lang et al. focus on capacity measures from a geometrical invariance perspective, advocating for the use of Fisher-Rao norm to assess deep neural network capacity. Their findings reveal the norm's invariance properties and establish norm-comparison inequalities, positioning the Fisher-Rao norm as a comprehensive framework for existing complexity measures, supported by experimental data.

Novak et al. conduct comparative studies on the generalization abilities of neural networks, highlighting the critical roles of the input-output Jacobian norm and linear region counting. Their results indicate that the generalization bounds are influenced by the proximity of the output hypothesis to the data manifold.

Additionally, works by Bartlett et al. and Neyshabur et al. provide upper bounds for the generalization error in chain-like deep neural networks, with Bartlett proposing a spectral-normalized margin-based bound and Neyshabur offering a similar result within the PAC-Bayesian framework. The current study is motivated by these analyses.

In the preliminary section, the paper outlines essential concepts for expressing an algorithm's generalization capability and presents a margin-based generalization bound for multi-class classification algorithms. It defines generalization error as the difference between expected risk and empirical risk, illustrating the inverse relationship between an algorithm's generalization ability and the complexity of its hypothesis space, as suggested by Occam's razor.
