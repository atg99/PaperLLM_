The paper presents an analysis of various pruning methods for neural networks, specifically focusing on magnitude pruning, SNIP, GraSP, and SynFlow. The authors investigate the performance of these methods when applied to subnetworks found at initialization, particularly on ResNet-50, ResNet-20, ResNet-18, and VGG-16.

Magnitude pruning relies on the initialization distributions of each layer, which are determined using He initialization. The study finds that when these distributions are appropriately utilized, magnitude pruning can achieve performance comparable to SNIP, GraSP, and SynFlow. However, when all layers are initialized uniformly, magnitude pruning's effectiveness diminishes, resembling random pruning. In contrast, SNIP, GraSP, and SynFlow maintain accuracy even under less informative initialization conditions.

A key observation regarding SynFlow is the phenomenon termed "neuron collapse," where entire neurons, or convolutional channels, are pruned at a higher rate than with other methods. This was evidenced by the percentage of pruned neurons across different networks, with SynFlow showing significantly higher pruning rates. The authors explain that SynFlow's mechanism involves summing the contributions of weights across all paths in the network, leading to a feedback loop where pruning one weight increases the likelihood of further pruning within the same neuron. This effect is exacerbated in skip connections, which are pruned heavily due to their limited participation in network paths.

The paper also explores the sensitivity of the pruning methods to the initial weight values, questioning whether performance remains stable when weights are reinitialized. While SNIP's performance can be influenced by initialization, the study highlights that the other methods, particularly SynFlow, exhibit inherent weaknesses leading to neuron collapse under certain conditions. Overall, the findings contribute to understanding the dynamics of pruning methods and their dependency on initialization strategies.
