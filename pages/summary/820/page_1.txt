In the paper "Pruning Neural Networks at Initialization: Why Are We Missing the Mark?" the authors Jonathan Frankle, Gintare Karolina Dziugaite, Daniel M. Roy, and Michael Carbin investigate the effectiveness of various pruning methods applied at the initialization phase of neural networks. They analyze techniques such as SNIP, GraSP, SynFlow, and magnitude pruning, highlighting that while these methods outperform random pruning, they do not achieve the accuracy levels of magnitude pruning after training.

The authors demonstrate that shuffling weights within layers or sampling new initial values can preserve or even enhance accuracy, suggesting that the per-weight decisions made by these methods could be replaced by a simpler per-layer approach to determine the fraction of weights to prune. This finding points to potential shortcomings in the heuristics of the pruning methods themselves or in the concept of pruning at initialization.

The paper contextualizes the need for early pruning within the growing costs of training neural networks and references the lottery ticket hypothesis, which posits that certain subnetworks can achieve full accuracy when trained in isolation. The authors critically assess the state of current pruning literature and propose that a more effective strategy may involve exploring broader challenges in pruning at initialization rather than relying solely on existing methods.
