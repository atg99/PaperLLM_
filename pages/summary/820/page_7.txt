The paper examines the effectiveness of early pruning techniques in neural networks, specifically focusing on methods such as SNIP, GraSP, SynFlow, and magnitude pruning. It highlights that while traditional pruning methods are sensitive to weight reinitialization, early pruning techniques maintain accuracy regardless of whether the network is initialized from the original weights or newly sampled weights. This insensitivity raises concerns about the potential limitations of these methods in achieving optimal performance.

The authors conduct an ablation study to evaluate the scoring functions used in these pruning methods, assessing whether they accurately rank weights based on their importance. The results indicate that while magnitude pruning, SNIP, and SynFlow show decreased accuracy when the most important weights are pruned, GraSP behaves differently, with accuracy remaining unchanged. This suggests that GraSP's heuristic may not effectively capture the importance of weights as intended.

The findings imply that the success of early pruning methods may rely more on the overall layerwise proportions of weights rather than the specific weights themselves. This contrasts with the broader pruning literature, where reinitialization and shuffling typically lead to lower accuracy. The authors question whether the high accuracies achieved by SNIP, GraSP, and SynFlow can be attributed to the hypotheses proposed in their respective papers, given that random shuffling of pruned weights does not affect performance.

Overall, the study suggests a need for further investigation into the underlying reasons for the effectiveness of early pruning methods and whether they can achieve higher performance beyond the observed results.
