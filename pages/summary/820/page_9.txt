The paper discusses the challenges of pruning neural networks at initialization compared to pruning after training. It highlights that methods for pruning at initialization tend to achieve lower accuracy than magnitude pruning applied post-training, despite using diverse signals such as magnitudes, gradients, and Hessians. The authors note that these methods show similar behaviors under various ablations, raising questions about their inherent limitations in accuracy when pruning at initialization.

The paper identifies a gap in understanding why methods struggle with pruning specific weights at initialization, suggesting that this difficulty may stem from properties of optimization or the multi-phase nature of training. The authors emphasize the importance of further research into this challenge and its implications for pruning strategies.

Looking ahead, the authors advocate for early pruning in training for both scientific and practical reasons, but they caution that current methods require a tradeoff between reducing training costs and maintaining accuracy. They propose that future research should focus on developing effective pruning techniques that do not compromise accuracy.

The paper also discusses the gradual improvement of specific pruning methods after initialization and suggests that new approaches may be necessary for effective early pruning. It raises questions about the efficacy of signals like magnitudes and gradients during the initial training phase compared to later stages. Finally, the authors propose that future evaluations of pruning methods should consider tradeoffs in accuracy and sparsity across different stages of training, potentially incorporating metrics that capture overall training costs.
