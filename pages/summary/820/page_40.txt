This appendix evaluates the hypothesis that early pruning methods may leave some weights unpruned but disconnected, leading to a higher effective sparsity compared to actual sparsity. The study explores whether random shuffling of network weights can reconnect some of these weights, potentially resulting in better performance due to a lower effective sparsity.

To investigate this, the authors employ a method to identify disconnected weights: they set unpruned weights to 1 and pruned weights to 0, then forward-propagate a single example, compute the logits, and determine gradients. Weights with a gradient of 0 are identified as disconnected and subsequently pruned.

The evaluation compares effective sparsities of shuffled networks against unmodified pruned networks across various pruning methods and networks (ResNet-20, VGG-16, ResNet-18, and ResNet-50). The results indicate that at the highest actual sparsity, the effective sparsity of shuffled networks is only slightly larger than that of unmodified networks, with the maximum increase being 3.5%. Most cases show negligible differences, often rounding to similar values.

The conclusion drawn is that while random shuffling can restore some disconnected parameters, the extent of this restoration is minimal, particularly at the sparsities of interest, suggesting that the effective sparsities closely align with actual sparsities in practical scenarios.
