This paper surveys various approaches to Neural Architecture Search (NAS), focusing on segment-based architectures. Tan et al. (2018) propose a method that decomposes neural networks into distinct segments, each characterized by a specific pattern of operations and filters that are repeated a set number of times. Notably, certain architectures deemed ineffective or computationally expensive are excluded from their search space, such as those featuring pooling as the initial operation or dense layers applied to high-resolution feature maps.

In parallel, Zoph and Le (2017) introduce a relaxed chain-structured search space that allows for arbitrary skip connections among ordered nodes. This approach enhances design variety, although it restricts operations to convolutions with varying hyperparameters. Their framework maintains a recursive definition while including skip connections, where a node's parent set must include its immediate predecessor.

Xie and Yuille (2017) further expand this concept by using summation instead of concatenation for merging operations. Their architecture deviates from a strictly sequential format, allowing for greater flexibility in parent node selection. They implement a template that organizes architectures into connected segments, with each segment comprising convolutional nodes. This template standardizes the number of convolution operations and filter sizes, exemplified by a configuration for the CIFAR-10 dataset, which specifies a fixed number of convolutions and filter sizes across three segments.
