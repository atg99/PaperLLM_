The paper discusses advancements in Neural Architecture Search (NAS) and highlights several key findings. It emphasizes the potential of using graph generators to create random graph structures that outperform architectures identified by complex optimization methods, even without deep learning-specific prior knowledge. The authors critique the common practice of comparing human-designed architectures with those discovered through NAS, arguing that state-of-the-art models are often not evaluated under equivalent conditions. They present results for CIFAR-10 and ImageNet datasets, showing that the performance gap between human-designed and discovered architectures is narrower than frequently claimed.

The paper also addresses the concept of early termination in training processes. It suggests that for NAS, it is unnecessary to achieve full validation accuracy to assess the quality of an architecture. Instead, the learning curve—mapping training iterations to validation accuracy—can help determine if a candidate architecture is likely to surpass the best known solution. By analyzing this curve, researchers can terminate unpromising training processes early, thereby reallocating resources to more promising candidates. The authors note that while many innovative architecture search methods have emerged, the underlying reasons for performance improvements remain unclear, marking an ongoing challenge in the field.
