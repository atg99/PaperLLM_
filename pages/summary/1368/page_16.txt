Optimization-based control is utilized in scenarios where system behavior can be reliably modeled or when validating new approaches. Key factors include the availability of quantifiable metrics such as task completion time and error rates. In multi-agent systems, the exponential increase in required information and potential interactions poses challenges, prompting research into computationally efficient decision-making techniques. Applications include robots influencing human teams, navigating among obstacles, predicting human behavior, and task allocation among robots.

As the number of robots increases, human operators may struggle to maintain awareness of system states, leading to the need for decision support systems (DSS). These systems assist in managing multiple robots, as seen in various studies focusing on operator allocation and human supervision of robot fleets.

Predefined and rule-based control methods allow for robot control without computational models, using techniques like Wizard of Oz and expert knowledge-based rules. This approach simplifies decision-making and enables researchers to focus on human-robot interaction outcomes. In cases where optimization is not feasible, actions may be determined through subjective interpretations based on expert knowledge.

There is potential for combining optimization-based and rule-based methods, as demonstrated in hybrid controllers that integrate behavioral rules with machine learning. Additionally, predefined control can facilitate studies on specific robot behaviors' effects on human users, resulting in unidirectional interactions from robots to humans.

The Wizard of Oz approach involves a hidden operator controlling robot actions, which can enhance robot capabilities or allow for a mixed-initiative control where either humans or robots can take charge.

Robot control can be based on individual agent parameters or the group as a whole. For example, trust can be assessed at the individual robot level or for the entire robot team, allowing for a more nuanced exploration of human-robot interactions in multi-agent settings. This individual modeling aids in extending dyadic research into more complex multi-agent environments.
