Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) are a type of linear model that predicts a variable by aggregating unknown smooth functions for predictor variables. The main goal of GAMs is to identify these smooth functions to approximate the predicted variable, allowing for clear interpretation of each variable's impact on the output. GAMs are commonly used in fields such as finance, environmental studies, healthcare, and biology due to their understandable nature, which fosters user confidence. They often incorporate visualization methods to enhance interpretability and are considered simulatable and decomposable, depending on modifications like link functions or interactions between predictors. While GAMs may not always outperform more complex models in accuracy, their primary advantage lies in their ability to elucidate underlying relationships within data.

Bayesian models, represented as probabilistic directed acyclic graphical models, illustrate conditional dependencies among variables, such as the relationship between diseases and symptoms. These models similarly provide a clear depiction of feature-target relationships through their connections. Like GAMs, Bayesian models can be categorized as simulatable and decomposable, although complexity can hinder these properties. They have been effectively applied in various domains including cognitive modeling, fishing, gaming, climate studies, and econometrics, and can also be used to explain other models like averaging tree ensembles.

When machine learning models lack transparency, post-hoc explainability techniques are employed to clarify their decision-making processes. These techniques aim to provide understandable insights into how a model generates predictions based on specific inputs.
