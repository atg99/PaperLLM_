While machine learning techniques have demonstrated potential in spatial interpolation, especially with the abundance of remote sensing data, they often overlook the spatial dependence of observations. 

Kriging, a traditional geostatistical method, serves as a Best Linear Unbiased Predictor under certain conditions but has limitations, such as computational demands and difficulties in modeling non-normally distributed data. Alternative methods like indicator kriging and the Generalized Linear Geostatistical Model address some of these issues but still face challenges with data characterized by many zeros or extreme values, as seen in precipitation data.

Recent advancements in machine learning (ML) have shown promise in enhancing spatial interpolation accuracy across various fields. Techniques such as random forests and quantile regression forests have outperformed traditional kriging in specific applications, capturing spatial autocorrelation through auxiliary variables. However, many existing ML frameworks for spatial interpolation fail to fully utilize the spatial correlation inherent in geo-referenced observations.

To improve ML models, researchers have begun integrating geographic context, including coordinates and distances to relevant geographical features, as covariates. This approach has shown improvements in prediction accuracy, suggesting that a more robust incorporation of spatial information can enhance the performance of machine learning methods in spatial data analysis.
