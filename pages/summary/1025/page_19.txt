The discussion section of the paper traces the evolution of pattern recognition control schemes from their inception in the late 1960s to contemporary advancements, emphasizing significant milestones in accuracy and real-time application. By the 1980s, feature extraction techniques improved accuracy to nearly 86%, but real-time application remained a challenge. The introduction of artificial neural networks in the 1990s enhanced accuracy to above 92%, prompting a focus on real-time analysis and natural control in myoelectric prostheses.

Despite advancements, the implementation of real-time prosthesis control faces several challenges. Key issues include the need for retraining pattern recognition classifiers, which involves complex restructuring of training matrices and variable estimation, raising concerns about processing speed in embedded systems. Additionally, the interaction among various components of the EMG-pattern recognition algorithm and the requirement for compact, efficient embedded systems are critical challenges that remain underexplored.

The paper notes that many studies have been conducted under ideal clinical conditions, often using able-bodied subjects, which do not accurately reflect the complexities faced by amputees in real-life situations. Factors such as varying loads and environmental conditions can lead to increased classification errors. Moreover, the practical application of pattern recognition systems is hindered by limitations including poor user interfaces, uncertainties in classification accuracy, and pattern variations over time.

While previous research has achieved notable control of upper limb prostheses, issues such as inaccurate classifications and slow response times continue to impede user experience. The paper highlights that no current pattern recognition system has achieved 100% accuracy, and the frustration stemming from unintended prosthesis movements can lead users to reject the technology altogether. Therefore, addressing these shortcomings is crucial for enhancing the viability of myoelectric pattern recognition control for amputees.
