The paper discusses various filtering mechanisms employed in data augmentation methods, emphasizing their importance when generative methods do not perfectly preserve labels. Liu et al. introduce a basic filtering approach by removing generated instances based on unigram word overlap. Other metrics like Levenshtein distance and Jaccard similarity can also be utilized. Wan et al. employ a similarity discriminator to assess the similarity of sentences, while Anaby-Tavor et al., Abonizio and Junior, and Claveau et al. filter instances using classifiers trained on class data, which may limit sample diversity.

Bayer et al. enhance this process by using embeddings to evaluate instance quality and involving human experts to set thresholds. In contrast, Yang et al. propose a sophisticated filtering mechanism that does not require human intervention, focusing on increasing dataset size for question-answering tasks. Their approach includes measuring validation loss changes using influence functions and selecting diverse examples to maximize unique unigrams.

The paper outlines a research agenda for textual data augmentation, stressing the need to explore the merits of augmentation methods in the context of large pre-trained language models. It highlights the challenges faced due to the dominance of these models, which may render some augmentation techniques less effective. Current research should consider integrating advancements from various neural network architectures and conducting experiments with larger models like BERT to better understand the efficacy of different augmentation strategies.
