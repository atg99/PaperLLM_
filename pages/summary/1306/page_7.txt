Feng et al. conducted a study where they randomly deleted, swapped, and inserted characters in text prompts used for fine-tuning text generators. They focused on ignoring the first and last characters of words and measured the effectiveness of their method based on diversity, fluency, semantic context preservation, and sentiment consistency, outperforming the baseline in all aspects. Their techniques are also applicable in text classification.

Ebrahimi et al. generated adversarial examples using an existing model trained on the initial dataset, modifying input data to flip letters that increased the model's loss. Retraining the model with this augmented data improved error rates and reduced the effectiveness of adversarial attacks. Their approach yielded a 0.62% accuracy improvement on the AG News dataset using CharCNN-LSTM, surpassing Miyato et al.'s method by 0.38% and contrasting with Belinkov and Bisk's method, which decreased accuracy.

Coulombe introduced weak textual sounds through letter changes, deletions, and additions, achieving a 2.5% accuracy increase compared to the best baseline, although the evaluation was limited to basic architectures without embeddings. Belinkov and Bisk explored natural noise, which involves common spelling mistakes, and found that while it generally degraded translation models, it made other models more robust against similar attacks. Coulombe's inclusion of spelling errors also improved classifier performance by 1.5%.

Coulombe also implemented rule-based transformations using regular expressions, acknowledging the complexity of establishing valid rules due to the need for deeper grammatical changes. His transformations included converting verbal short forms to long forms, resulting in a 0.5% accuracy gain in the best baseline model.

Noise induction was discussed as another technique, where Xie et al. proposed "unigram noising," which replaces words in input data with other words at a specified probability, highlighting the potential for word-level data augmentation.
