The introduction of the paper discusses the dominant approach to inference in social sciences, which typically relies on random sampling to induce uncertainty about population parameters. This method is effective when samples represent a small fraction of the population, as seen in studies using individual-level data from sources like the U.S. Current Population Survey. However, the authors argue that this perspective is less suitable in contexts where larger populations or entire populations are sampled, such as when states or counties are treated as units of observation.

The paper proposes an alternative framework for interpreting uncertainty in regression analysis that accommodates both sampling-based uncertainty and design-based uncertainty. Design-based uncertainty is relevant when the parameter of interest pertains to unobserved outcomes resulting from specific interventions, a concept commonly recognized in randomized experiments but often overlooked in regression analyses and observational studies.

To illustrate the differences between sampling-based and design-based inference, the authors present examples involving finite populations characterized by pairs of variables. In these examples, uncertainty arises from observing only a sample of the population, with sampling indicators used to assess variability across different samples. The paper sets the stage for a deeper exploration of these concepts, aiming to enhance the understanding of uncertainty in statistical analyses beyond traditional sampling assumptions.
