The paper discusses the implications of sampling and design-based uncertainty in estimating causal effects, particularly focusing on the variance associated with different estimands. It highlights that when estimating the causal effect, the overall population size becomes irrelevant if the estimand is based solely on the sample. The variance between total and sampled data suggests that ignoring a significant portion of the population can lead to overstatements of variance.

The distinction between internal and external validity is emphasized. Internal validity pertains to whether an estimator accurately reflects the causal effect within the sampled population, relying on random treatment assignment. External validity, on the other hand, concerns whether the expected causal effect from the sample can be generalized to the entire population, which hinges on random sampling. For a robust estimator of the causal effect, both internal and external validity must be satisfied.

The paper also presents a specific example involving a single binary regressor, where the EHW variance estimator is derived. This estimator is adjusted for degrees of freedom and aligns with Neyman's variance estimator, demonstrating that in an infinite population context, design-based uncertainty becomes negligible. The findings underscore the importance of both random assignment and random sampling in achieving valid causal inference.
