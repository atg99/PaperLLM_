The paper discusses self-supervised learning (SSL), categorizing it into three main types: Generative, Contrastive, and Generative-Contrastive (Adversarial). It highlights the motivation behind SSL, emphasizing the data-hungry nature of deep learning algorithms and their tendency to overfit when supervised data is scarce. The paper notes that while many efforts focus on new neural network architectures, SSL offers a simple solution by utilizing vast amounts of unlabeled data available today.

SSL leverages inherent co-occurrence relationships within the data for self-supervision, illustrated by the example of a language model predicting missing words in a sentence. The three categories of self-supervision include:

1. **Generative**: This involves training an encoder to represent input data and a decoder to reconstruct it, focusing on tasks like the cloze test and graph generation.
2. **Contrastive**: This method trains an encoder to measure similarity between inputs, utilizing techniques like mutual information maximization.
3. **Generative-Contrastive (Adversarial)**: This combines generative and contrastive approaches, training a generator to create samples and a discriminator to differentiate between real and fake samples, as seen in Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs).

The paper contrasts the architecture and objectives of these methods, noting that generative methods do not use a discriminator, while both GANs and contrastive methods do, albeit with different parameter complexities. It concludes that effectively designed training objectives can transform models into powerful pre-trained feature extractors, particularly in visual classification tasks, by modeling class invariance through contrastive learning. The essence of self-supervised learning, therefore, lies in defining appropriate objectives for unlabeled data.
