The paper explores methods for explaining predictions made by recurrent neural networks (RNNs) in natural language processing (NLP). The authors evaluate and compare various explanation techniques using two experimental setups, adapting a simple toy task to serve as a testbed. They focus on how word relevances can be leveraged to build sentence-level representations and demonstrate the utility of relevance visualization for understanding misclassifications related to semantic composition.

The authors establish a framework for explaining RNN predictions, denoting a trained model's scalar-valued prediction function for each class. They define input data as sequences of word embeddings and aim to assign scalar relevance values to each input variable, quantifying their importance in relation to a specific class prediction.

Two main explanation methods are discussed: gradient-based and occlusion-based explanations. The gradient-based approach utilizes partial derivatives of the prediction function to derive relevance values, either directly or through a variation that incorporates the input variable's value. This technique is computationally efficient, requiring only one forward and backward pass through the network.

In contrast, the occlusion-based method assigns relevances by occluding input variables and measuring the change in predictions. This can be done by replacing word vectors with zeros, and it is computationally more intensive, necessitating multiple forward passes to compute relevance for each word.

The paper highlights the strengths and weaknesses of each method, contributing to a better understanding of how RNNs make predictions and how to interpret their behavior in NLP tasks.
