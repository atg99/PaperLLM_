The paper "Is the Policy Gradient a Gradient?" examines common misunderstandings in the literature surrounding policy gradient methods in reinforcement learning. The authors identify three papers with explicit erroneous claims and highlight two others that made misleading assertions regarding the relationship between discounted policy gradients and their corresponding algorithms. 

The paper discusses the Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C) method, which achieved state-of-the-art performance in Atari games, and clarifies that the claim asserting ∇θπ(at|st;θ)Rt as an unbiased estimate of ∇θE[Rt] is incorrect. It also critiques the Actor Critic using Kronecker-Factored Trust Region (ACKTR) method for erroneously defining the policy gradient without acknowledging its approximative nature. The Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) method is also discussed, particularly its misconceptions regarding the optimization of the average reward objective.

In the discussion, the authors note that many papers misinterpret ∇J?(θ) as a gradient, leading to confusion between the gradients of discounted objectives and the gradients followed by algorithms. They argue that while ∇J?(θ) is not a true gradient and can lead to poor performance, it remains popular due to its practical effectiveness. The paper calls attention to the need for greater precision in the representation of policy gradients in the literature, as widespread misunderstandings persist. The authors conclude by emphasizing the open question regarding the reasons for the effectiveness of these estimators, particularly in episodic contexts.
