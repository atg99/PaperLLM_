The paper discusses a general framework for graph-based semi-supervised learning methods, which typically involves three steps: graph creation, graph weighting, and inference. In the graph creation phase, nodes representing data points are connected based on a similarity measure. This results in a graph with nodes and an n×n weight matrix indicating edge weights, where a weight of zero signifies no connection. The terms node and data point are used interchangeably throughout this context.

The inference step aims to predict labels for unlabelled data points. The objective function for transductive graph-based methods consists of two components: one penalizes discrepancies between predicted and true labels, while the other penalizes differences in label predictions among connected data points. This is expressed in a mathematical formulation that incorporates a supervised loss function for labelled data and an unsupervised loss function for both labelled and unlabelled pairs. The parameter λ balances the importance of the supervised term, and some methods include a unary regularization term for unlabelled predictions.

Graph construction typically relies on local similarities among data points, adhering to the smoothness and manifold assumptions. This is compared to supervised nearest-neighbour methods, which predict labels based on the labels of similar labelled data points. Graph-based methods extend this concept by considering similarities among unlabelled data points, thus enabling label propagation through a network of points.

The paper notes that while early research focused on inference methods, there has been increasing attention to graph construction due to its significant impact on classifier performance. This shift reflects a growing recognition of the importance of the graph construction phase in the overall efficacy of graph-based semi-supervised learning methods.
