The paper discusses the self-training paradigm in machine learning, which involves various design choices such as the selection of data for pseudo-labelling, the re-use of pseudo-labelled data across iterations, and the criteria for stopping the training process. The selection of data to be pseudo-labelled is crucial, as it directly affects the training set for the classifier. Typically, data selection is based on prediction confidence, making the quality of these estimates vital for the algorithm's performance.

If well-calibrated probabilistic predictions are available, they can be used directly, allowing for an iterative self-training approach similar to expectation-maximization. This method has been effectively studied in the context of naïve Bayes classifiers, particularly for tasks like detecting fake product reviews.

For algorithms that do not inherently provide reliable probabilistic predictions, adaptations are necessary. For instance, decision trees usually produce biased probability estimates due to their design, which prioritizes minimizing impurity. To address this, Tanha et al. proposed enhancing prediction probability estimates through methods like grafting and Laplace correction, as well as introducing a local distance-based measure to determine confidence rankings.

Additionally, Leistner et al. improved random forests by independently pseudo-labelling unlabelled data points for each tree based on estimated posterior distributions and suggested a stopping criterion based on out-of-bag error.

The paper also highlights that base learners in self-training must be retrained in each iteration. However, when classifiers can be trained incrementally, a similar iterative pseudo-labelling approach can be applied without complete retraining. Lee introduced the pseudo-label approach in neural networks, where the weight of pseudo-labelled data is increased over time, allowing for fine-tuning rather than retraining after each pseudo-labelling step. This method maintains clear similarities to self-training while deviating from the wrapper method paradigm.
