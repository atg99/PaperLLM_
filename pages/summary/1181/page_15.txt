The paper discusses the theoretical properties of self-training algorithms, noting that limited research exists in this area. Notable studies by Haffari and Sarkar (2007) and Culp and Michailidis (2008) explored connections between self-training and graph-based methods, particularly focusing on convergence properties with multiple base learners.

The concept of co-training is introduced as an extension of self-training that involves multiple supervised classifiers. In co-training, classifiers iteratively train on labeled data and share their most confident predictions with each other. The effectiveness of co-training relies on a diversity criterion, which emphasizes that base learners should not be strongly correlated in their predictions to maximize the exchange of useful information.

Co-training methods traditionally depend on distinct views of the data, which correspond to different feature subsets. For example, video data can be separated into audio and visual components. This approach falls under multi-view learning, which has been comprehensively surveyed by Xu et al. (2013). However, in scenarios lacking predefined data views, single-view co-training methods are utilized, either by automatically generating different views or by enhancing diversity among learning algorithms. 

The foundational co-training method proposed by Blum and Mitchell (1998) is highlighted, wherein two classifiers are trained on different feature subsets. The method's success hinges on two assumptions: that each feature subset can independently yield good predictions and that these subsets are conditionally independent given the class label. This co-training framework has been effectively applied in various fields, especially in natural language processing.
