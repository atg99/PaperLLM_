The paper discusses the limitations of traditional generative models, particularly mixture models, which rely on the critical assumption that the model accurately represents the true data distribution. When this assumption is violated, as is often the case in real-world applications, the inclusion of unlabelled data can negatively impact model performance. To address these challenges, the focus has shifted towards more flexible generative models, notably generative adversarial networks (GANs). 

GANs, introduced by Goodfellow et al. in 2014, operate on a dual-learning paradigm, involving a generator and a discriminator. The generator creates data points that resemble real data, while the discriminator attempts to differentiate between real and generated data. The optimization process for GANs is framed as a minimax game, where the discriminator aims to minimize the probability of misclassifying data, and the generator seeks to maximize the likelihood of its generated data being classified as real.

The training of GANs is unsupervised, leveraging unlabelled data. However, adaptations such as semi-supervised GANs have been proposed, allowing the discriminator to also classify data into distinct categories. An example of this is CatGAN, which integrates a cross-entropy cost term to penalize the discriminator for misclassifying real data, thus enhancing the model's ability to learn from both labelled and unlabelled data. This evolution in generative modeling highlights the potential of GANs to address the limitations of earlier approaches by enabling more robust data generation and classification.
