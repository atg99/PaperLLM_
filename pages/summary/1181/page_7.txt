The paper discusses the role of similarity in clustering and semi-supervised learning. It emphasizes that the choice of similarity concept significantly influences the definition of clusters. The smoothness, low-density, and manifold assumptions present different definitions of similarity: smoothness suggests that close points are similar, low-density indicates that points in high-density areas are similar, and manifold states that points on the same low-dimensional manifold are similar. The authors argue that the cluster assumption is essential for semi-supervised learning, implying that if data points cannot be meaningfully clustered, semi-supervised methods cannot outperform supervised ones.

The paper also addresses the challenges of effectively using unlabelled data in semi-supervised learning. It points out that unlabelled data is only beneficial if it provides information not available from labelled data. However, identifying when this is the case is complex. The authors note that while graph-based methods depend on local similarity measures, high-dimensional data, such as images, complicates this task. Many semi-supervised learning approaches for images rely on a weak version of the smoothness assumption, requiring predictions to remain stable under minor input perturbations. Similarly, semi-supervised adaptations of supervised algorithms often rely on the same assumptions as their supervised counterparts, such as the low-density assumption used by support vector machines.
