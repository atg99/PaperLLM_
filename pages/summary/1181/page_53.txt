The paper discusses various methods for constructing edge weights in graphs used in machine learning, particularly focusing on Gaussian kernels and local neighborhood approaches. It starts with the isotropic Gaussian kernel, defined as Wij = exp(-||xi - xj||² / (2σ²)), where σ² represents the variance. A local variant introduced by Hein and Maier uses a maximum distance to the nearest neighbors to define weights, resulting in Wij = exp(-||xi - xj||² / (max{hi, hj})²). Other variations include adjusting feature importance based on information gain and employing binary weights.

The authors highlight that traditional methods calculate edge weights based solely on pairwise similarities. In contrast, Wang and Zhang's linear neighborhood propagation (LNP) algorithm considers the entire neighborhood to approximate each data point as a linear combination of its neighbors. This leads to an optimization problem that minimizes the reconstruction error while ensuring non-negative weights that sum to one for each node.

The paper also notes that LNP is akin to locally linear embedding but includes additional constraints. Moreover, weight symmetry is not enforced in this approach, making the weights independent of other connections. Lastly, Karasuyama and Mamitsuka propose a method combining locally linear embedding with a local similarity measure, using a Gaussian kernel with a diagonal covariance matrix to minimize local reconstruction error.
