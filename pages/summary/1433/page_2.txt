The paper provides a comprehensive review of automatic speech recognition (ASR), focusing on the evolution and comparison of different models, particularly the hidden Markov model (HMM) and end-to-end models. The primary goal of ASR is to accurately calculate the posterior distribution p(L|X). HMM-based models have been the standard in large vocabulary continuous speech recognition (LVCSR), often combined with deep learning techniques. Despite their success, the emergence of end-to-end models, which simplify the process by mapping audio directly to characters or words without extensive domain expertise, has gained significant traction in recent research.

The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews the history of ASR, detailing the development of various models including HMM-Gaussian mixed models, HMM-deep neural networks, and end-to-end models, while comparing their strengths and weaknesses. Sections 3 to 5 analyze specific end-to-end technologies such as connectionist temporal classification (CTC)-based models, recurrent neural network (RNN)-transducers, and attention-based models, emphasizing their principles and advancements. Section 6 offers a theoretical and experimental comparison of these models, discussing their respective advantages and disadvantages. Finally, Section 7 outlines potential future directions for end-to-end models.

The background section highlights key milestones in ASR history, beginning with Dreyfus Graf's early work and progressing through significant developments at Bell Labs and various Japanese laboratories, which laid the foundation for modern speech recognition technologies. The initial systems were limited to recognizing single words or vowels, but advancements in the 1960s introduced techniques for recognizing phonemes and continuous speech, setting the stage for contemporary ASR systems.
