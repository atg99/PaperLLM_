The paper discusses the evolution of speech recognition technology, beginning with the introduction of linear prediction technology by Itakura, Saito, and Atal in the 1970s. This was followed by the application of dynamic programming in speech recognition by Vintsyuk et al. and the independent proposal of Linear Predictive Coding (LPC) cepstrum distance by Atal and Itakura. These advancements facilitated progress in recognizing isolated words and small vocabulary tasks.

As speech recognition technology progressed, researchers sought to extend capabilities to large vocabulary continuous speech recognition (LVCSR) for non-specific speakers. However, challenges arose due to variations in speaker characteristics, context correlations in continuous speech, and vocabulary size. HMM-based (Hidden Markov Model) technology and deep learning emerged as crucial components for overcoming these challenges.

In the mid-1980s, HMM technology gained prominence, particularly with the development of the SPHINX system by Kai-Fu Lee, which utilized HMM for speech state modeling and GMM (Gaussian Mixture Model) for observation probability. This marked a significant milestone in LVCSR, and HMM-GMM remained dominant until the advent of deep learning techniques.

Despite extensive research on HMM-GMM in the 1990s and early 2000s, performance stagnated, especially in daily conversation scenarios. The introduction of deep learning led to significant advancements in various fields, including speech recognition. In 2011, a combination of HMM with context-dependent deep neural networks (CD-DNN-HMM) was proposed by researchers from Microsoft, resulting in notable performance improvements over traditional systems.

The paper categorizes LVCSR models into HMM-based and end-to-end models, noting that HMM-based models have historically been the most effective. These models consist of three independent components: the acoustic model, which maps speech input to feature sequences; the pronunciation model, which connects phonemes to graphemes; and the language model, which translates character sequences into fluent transcriptions. In HMM-based models, the acoustic model predominantly employs HMM, where sound serves as the observation and features represent hidden states.
