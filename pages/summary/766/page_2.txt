The paper discusses the challenges associated with phase retrieval in digital holographic microscopy (DHM), particularly the issues of phase wrapping and aberrations caused by optical components. Traditional unwrapping algorithms, while effective, tend to be slow and sensitive. Alternatives, such as additional hardware or algorithms based on strong assumptions, have drawbacks in terms of cost and complexity.

The authors highlight the need for extended focused imaging (EFI) and depth map (DM) reconstruction in microscopy, where depth information is crucial. They note that digital holography is advantageous for capturing 3-D information in a single hologram. Current computational methods often involve complex algorithms or additional hardware, both of which can be inefficient.

Recent advancements in deep learning have shown promise in addressing some of these challenges. The paper references existing studies where deep learning techniques have been applied for phase aberration compensation and artifact elimination in DHM. However, these methods still rely on conventional reconstruction algorithms and have limitations in handling defocused images.

The authors propose a novel end-to-end deep learning framework called the holographic reconstruction network (HRNet) to streamline the reconstruction process. This framework allows raw holograms to be directly input into the network, which learns to perform necessary processing steps autonomously. This approach aims to eliminate the need for prior knowledge of physical parameters or additional processing.

The HRNet architecture is designed to balance depth and computational efficiency. It consists of three functional blocks: input, feature extraction, and reconstruction. The network is trained on separate datasets for different types of objects, enabling it to reconstruct amplitude and phase objects as well as to extend depth of field.

The paper concludes by quantitatively comparing the proposed method with traditional approaches, demonstrating its superior performance in reconstructing various modalities, including noise-free amplitude and quantitative phase images, as well as achieving significant improvements in depth of field.
