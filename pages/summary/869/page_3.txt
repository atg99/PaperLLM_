The paper discusses the implementation and evaluation of Self-Selective Attention Networks (SSANs) for natural language processing (NLP) tasks. SSANs utilize a selector network to identify significant input elements before applying self-attention, illustrated through the example of the word "talk" focusing on the words "Bush," "held," and "Sharon." The selector network faces challenges due to the lack of ground-truth labels and the non-differentiable nature of discrete variables. To address this, the authors adopt Gumbel-Softmax techniques, specifically a Gumbel-Sigmoid function, which allows for differentiable sampling during training while maintaining the ability to select the action with the highest probability during inference.

The effectiveness of SSANs is evaluated across three NLP benchmarks: language inference, semantic role labeling, and machine translation, which encompass classification, sequence labeling, and sequence generation tasks. For language inference, experiments are conducted using the Stanford Natural Language Inference (SNLI) dataset, where the model classifies the relationship between premise and hypothesis sentences. Semantic role labeling experiments are performed on the CoNLL2012 dataset, focusing on identifying predicate-argument structures in sentences. The results indicate that SSANs improve performance by leveraging structural information and enhancing the model's ability to learn from input sequences.
