The paper discusses the evolving perception of students as customers in the context of higher education, highlighting the moral obligation for institutions to consider student feedback due to their role as fee payers. It emphasizes the importance of student satisfaction as a critical factor in retention, particularly in the first year of college, where dropout rates are high. 

Student satisfaction is defined as a short-term attitude influenced by educational experiences, where satisfaction arises when actual performance meets or exceeds expectations. It is multi-dimensional and shaped by clarity of student goals and trust in educational practitioners. Trust can be fostered through consistent and equitable treatment, addressing student expectations and complaints effectively.

The current study aims to investigate the factors influencing students' preferences to recommend their institution, OCEM, to their peers and family. It posits that students' reluctance to recommend may reflect their reasons for considering leaving the institution. The research examines how personal variables such as gender, family income, and college location affect student satisfaction and preferences. It also seeks to understand the motives behind students' recommendations and whether satisfaction levels differ based on their experiences with various academic and infrastructural factors, controlling for the aforementioned personal variables.
