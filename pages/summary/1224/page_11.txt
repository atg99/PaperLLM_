The paper discusses the negative impacts of maternal international migration (MIM) on left-behind girls (LBGs) compared to the effects of paternal international migration. Adhikari (2018) and Tong et al. (2015) found that MIM had more severe consequences for LBGs, as mothers are culturally and naturally more adept at nurturing and motivating their daughters than fathers. The separation from mothers often leads to feelings of loneliness, guilt, and vulnerability among girls, with studies showing that 60 out of 400 mother-migrant households reported mental and physical health issues for LBGs due to the absence of maternal care.

LBGs were found to take on additional household responsibilities, negatively impacting their educational performance. The studies highlighted that mothers play a crucial spiritual role in family dynamics, which is less pronounced in the case of fathers, who typically serve as breadwinners. Furthermore, boys in South Asian countries tend to engage less in household chores compared to girls.

Research indicates that MIM is perceived as a more significant disruption in a child's life than paternal migration, with serious implications for educational achievement. There are alarming reports, such as those by Mahata (2018), of sexual abuse occurring in the wake of MIM. Studies in China and the Philippines corroborate that MIM leads to educational setbacks and a shift in LBGs’ time towards household duties, further deteriorating their educational outcomes.

In conclusion, the absence of mothers is shown to be more problematic than the absence of fathers in migrant households, with LBGs expressing a strong preference for maternal support during critical developmental years. The findings underscore the rigid gender roles in many Asian cultures, where the mother’s role is pivotal in child development, making MIM particularly detrimental.
