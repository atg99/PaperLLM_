The research highlights that many graduate students from reputable business schools lack integrative thinking skills compared to undergraduates from other fields. Consequently, companies have initiated basic business training programs for new hires, with studies indicating that a significant portion of workplace learning occurs through on-the-job experiences and mentoring.

Business schools face pressing questions regarding their ability to meet the expectations of global companies while ensuring students achieve career success. Challenges such as high costs, inadequate faculty, and a status-based system hinder their progress. Critics argue that business schools are often disconnected from practical applications, leading to curricula that focus more on theoretical functions than on essential managerial skills. This gap results in insufficient evidence for the skills needed in real business environments.

Additionally, traditional assessment methods, like the GMAT, are seen as misaligned with the qualities necessary for entrepreneurial success. Many business programs are criticized for prioritizing profit over relevance to career development, with the MBA often viewed as a financial asset for universities rather than a meaningful educational experience.

To address these issues, business schools must innovate and embed practical experiences into their culture, focusing on quality enhancement and attracting high-performing faculty. The development of business leaders is a complex process requiring a reevaluation of learning practices. Schools must consider how their approaches to teaching and learning align with the evolving demands of the business landscape. This involves addressing perceptions of learning, the relevance of grading, and the overall effectiveness of their educational methodologies.
