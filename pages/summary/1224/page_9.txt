The study highlights the significant impact of maternal migration on the education, health, and psychosocial development of left-behind girls (LBGs) under the age of 16. It notes that in families where mothers migrate, older sisters often assume caregiving roles for younger siblings, which can lead to increased responsibilities and stress, particularly in Nepalese societies where cultural expectations position them as secondary caregivers. 

Sibling dynamics also play a critical role, as the presence of brothers can increase boys' involvement in household chores, while conflicts among siblings may arise over leadership roles, exacerbating issues of rivalry and jealousy. These sibling interactions can negatively affect the psychosocial development of LBGs, who may experience heightened stress due to their nurturing roles and the aggressive nature of sibling conflicts.

The literature review indicates that there is a lack of nuanced understanding regarding gender differences in migration studies, with many existing frameworks failing to adequately address the unique experiences of female migrants. This gap in research is significant as it may lead to misconceptions about the effects of migration on both genders. The study posits that maternal migration (MIM) disrupts the traditional mother-child relationship, which can have far-reaching consequences on the educational and health outcomes of LBGs.

In summary, the study aims to explore the effects of maternal migration on LBGs, focusing on their educational, health, and psychosocial outcomes compared to those in non-migrant households, while also emphasizing the importance of considering gender in migration research.
