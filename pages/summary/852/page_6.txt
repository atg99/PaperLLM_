This paper discusses Neural Architecture Search (NAS) utilizing reinforcement learning (RL) to identify high-performance neural network architectures. Early NAS methods treated network design as an agent's action, where the designed architecture, or child model, is trained and evaluated on validation data to provide a reward signal for the agent. Zoph et al. pioneered the use of policy gradient methods in NAS, employing a recurrent neural network (RNN) to generate architectures. Their initial macro-search space allows for the design of an entire convolutional neural network (CNN) in one go, incorporating various parameters like the number of layers and operations. However, this approach is computationally expensive for large datasets.

To address this cost, Zoph et al. introduced a micro-search space, focusing on smaller modules known as normal and reduction cells. This structure promotes efficiency by connecting these cells to form a complete network while ensuring good generalization across tasks. The NASNet method further evolved into a multi-objective optimization variant, balancing classification performance and computational cost.

Q-learning, a popular RL method, has been effectively applied in NAS. Baker et al. utilized an epsilon-greedy Q-learning strategy to select layer types and hyperparameters sequentially. Zhong et al. expanded this concept with a block-wise network approach, where optimal blocks are designed and repeated to form the entire architecture. Techniques such as distributed asynchronous strategies and early stopping have been implemented to speed up the search process.

Efficient NAS (ENAS) introduced parameter sharing, allowing different architectures to share weights from a common super-net, which enhances the search efficiency. The paper also mentions a multi-task architecture search (MAS) approach based on ENAS for discovering effective cell structures.
