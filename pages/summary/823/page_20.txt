The paper discusses advancements in network pruning techniques, particularly focusing on structured and unstructured sparsity in deep learning models. Liu et al. (2019b) challenge the conventional approach of training overparameterized models followed by pruning, demonstrating that smaller models can be trained from scratch with random weights to achieve competitive accuracy, especially for neuron pruning, though they note limitations with weight pruning on large datasets like ImageNet.

SNIP (Lee et al., 2019) introduces a data-driven method for identifying unstructured sparsity before training, utilizing a weight importance metric based on loss sensitivity. However, Wang et al. (2020b) highlight issues with SNIP, noting that high sparsity can hinder gradient flow and lead to "layer collapse," where entire layers become untrainable. They propose Gradient Signal Preservation (GraSP), which focuses on preserving gradient norms during pruning, thus improving performance in sparse regimes.

Verdenius et al. (2020) critique GraSP's complexity and propose simpler iterative pruning methods, SNIP-it and SNAP-it, which reassess weight importance after each removal to prevent bottlenecks. They demonstrate that their approach can significantly reduce training time while maintaining performance quality. Overall, the paper illustrates the evolving landscape of network pruning, emphasizing the balance between sparsity, model performance, and complexity.
