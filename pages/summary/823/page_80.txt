The paper by T. Hoefler et al. discusses the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, which posits that dense, randomly-initialized, feed-forward networks contain subnetworks, referred to as "winning tickets," that can achieve comparable test accuracy to the original network when trained in isolation. The authors demonstrate that for shallow vision networks, winning tickets can be identified through magnitude pruning, and retraining these subnetworks starting from the original weights leads to similar or improved accuracy within the same number of iterations. They also highlight that random initialization alone is insufficient for achieving optimal performance.

Further evidence from Zhou et al. suggests that while exact initial weights may not be necessary, the signs of the weights can suffice for training winning tickets. The concept of pruning as a standalone method is emphasized, with studies indicating that initial subnetworks can perform well without weight training. For instance, training to discover a "supermask" can yield high accuracy on datasets like MNIST and CIFAR-10 while maintaining static random weights. Malach et al. argue that any network can be approximated with high accuracy by pruning a larger network, supporting the notion that weight modification may not be necessary.

The paper also explores the challenges of applying the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis to larger networks. Liu et al. found that the original initialization did not enhance accuracy over random initialization at optimal learning rates. Frankle et al. propose a variant of the hypothesis that incorporates "rewinding" the weights after initial optimization, suggesting that a small fraction of iterations could be sufficient for achieving high sparsity in networks. However, the methodology for determining the optimal iteration for rewinding remains unclear, and the authors investigate how the order of batches affects training outcomes. Their findings indicate that the iteration at which a working lottery ticket can be derived correlates with the rewinding process, emphasizing the importance of early training iterations in large networks.
