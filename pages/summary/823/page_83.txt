This paper discusses the concept of sparsity in deep learning, emphasizing the idea that much of the optimization occurs within a limited subspace of the parameter space, termed the "intrinsic dimension." Li et al. (2018) propose a "dense plus sparse" method, which efficiently stores differences from randomly initialized parameters, allowing for substantial pruning.

Bartoldson et al. (2020) introduce the notion of modeling pruning as "noise injection," linking it to improved generalization capabilities. They observe that the initial drop in test accuracy post-pruning correlates with better generalization of the final model, suggesting that effective pruning schedules can enhance model performance by "flattening" the loss landscape.

The paper addresses the challenges of interpreting pruning results due to varied experimental setups and methodologies, as noted by Blalock et al. (2020). They advocate for standardized benchmarks, such as MLPerf and Deep500, to facilitate comparisons across different pruning techniques. Specific datasets and architectures, like ResNet-50 on ImageNet and BERT on GLUE, are recommended as de-facto benchmarks for evaluating model sparsity and performance.

The authors observe a strong correlation between achieved sparsity at high accuracies and the attention certain models receive in literature, indicating that well-tuned models like ResNet-50 set the state of the art, complicating the assessment of "prunability" for other architectures without comprehensive experimental validation.

For simpler tasks, the paper suggests using the MNIST dataset with LeNet architectures as a calibration point, noting that while these models achieve high accuracy with minimal parameters, they may not reflect the performance of methods on more complex tasks. Therefore, the authors recommend reporting results on larger convolutional networks for tasks like CIFAR-100 and ImageNet to track progress in pruning methodologies. They conclude that global magnitude pruning serves as a solid baseline across various scenarios, emphasizing the importance of estimating a model's amenability to sparsification when developing or utilizing sparse training models.
