The paper discusses various methods for pruning neural networks, highlighting the prevalence of magnitude-based pruning for weights and sensitivity-based pruning for neurons. It presents statistics on how different selection methods are combined to prune candidate elements during training and inference, emphasizing that magnitude pruning remains dominant.

The authors introduce the concept of dynamic pruning, where a network not only removes elements during training but also re-adds them to maintain the model's size. This process resembles architecture search, as it explores potential model configurations. While adding new neurons can be straightforward, re-adding weights is more complex due to their zero values when pruned. The paper suggests that additional information, like gradient or Hessian magnitude, may assist in selecting weights to re-add, although this could increase memory and computational overhead.

One scheme for weight regrowth is random or uniform regrowth, where weights are activated randomly during training. This method has been shown to produce power-law distributions in weight connections, drawing analogies to biological processes in the brain, where weight removal and addition mimic natural selection and mutation, respectively. The authors also note that such random growth leads to a stochastic exploration of model space.
