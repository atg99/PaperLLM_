The paper discusses the concept of sparsity in deep learning, emphasizing the differences between biological brains and silicon-based computational systems. It highlights that while biological brains utilize neuroplasticity and a three-dimensional structure for efficient learning, silicon devices face limitations in adapting their wiring, leading to overheads in memory and computation. This opens opportunities for designing mechanisms that may not be biologically plausible yet can outperform biological methods in specific contexts.

The authors argue that drawing inspiration from biological systems can guide innovation in deep learning, citing examples such as the use of weight sharing in convolutional networks, which, although not found in biological visual cortices, proves efficient in silicon. They also mention the optimization process, pointing out that while stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is commonly used, its biological plausibility remains uncertain, with some connections to Hebbian learning highlighted in recent research.

The paper further explores pruning techniques inspired by biological brains, acknowledging their limitations in achieving high accuracy for large-scale networks. It notes the sparse high-dimensional representation of biological brains, which could inspire future research if combined with theoretical reasoning and engineering principles.

Additionally, the authors discuss permutation groups and information loss, revealing that dense networks belong to large equivalence classes that produce identical outputs, suggesting that sparsified networks may contain exponentially more information. They reference studies that identify specific subnetworks during training that could lead to effective sparse structures, with some evidence indicating that retraining sparse networks can yield high performance, although not as high as pruned dense variants. The paper concludes by emphasizing the potential of exploring these concepts further to enhance deep learning architectures.
