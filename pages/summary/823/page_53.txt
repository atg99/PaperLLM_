The paper discusses the concept of sparsity in deep learning, particularly focusing on techniques for layer-wise addition and pruning to enhance model accuracy. Mostafa and Wang (2019) propose a method where parameters are initially distributed evenly across layers and then new parameters are added in proportion to the number of parameters retained after magnitude pruning, emphasizing the importance of significant layers. Gordon et al. (2018) further explore the idea of uniformly adding filters or neurons using a width multiplier, which proves effective in an iterative grow/prune methodology.

The authors also highlight that large dense models facilitate the optimization process, suggesting that learning in a dense space could be beneficial. Golub et al. (2019) note that randomly initialized weights that are later pruned still impact the optimization of non-pruned weights, proposing a method to recompute these weights on demand during training.

A gradient-based approach is discussed, where the importance of weights can be assessed by observing gradients during the backward pass. Although this method can increase memory and computation overhead, it offers valuable insights into weight significance. Dai et al. (2018a) link this method to biological learning principles, showing its mathematical equivalence to adding synapses between stimulated neurons.

Lin et al. (2020) propose maintaining an updated copy of all weights during training while using a mask to deactivate them in the forward pass, allowing for efficient architecture exploration. Wortsman et al. (2019) modify this by restricting gradient flow to pruned weights, while Dettmers and Zettlemoyer (2019) introduce a momentum term for weight growth based on multiple iterations of gradients. Their findings indicate that updating pruned weights during training is crucial for achieving optimal model accuracy.

To manage memory overhead associated with gradient storage, Evci et al. (2020a) suggest computing gradients layer-by-layer and discarding them post-regrowth decisions, acknowledging potential accuracy trade-offs. They explore various schemes for determining parameter counts per layer, including uniform distribution, scaling based on neuron counts, and incorporating kernel dimensions into scaling factors. Overall, the paper emphasizes the delicate balance between sparsity and model performance in deep learning.
