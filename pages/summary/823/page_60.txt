The paper discusses advancements in sparse gradient communication in distributed training systems, which has been overlooked in prior analyses. It highlights several key implementations and approaches to enhance efficiency while managing sparse gradients.

Initially, sparse communication was implemented in a parameter server architecture, where multiple workers communicate with a central server. However, many modern distributed training systems utilize all-reduce communication without a central node. The paper references Dryden et al. (2016), who developed a ring-based all-reduce method incorporating custom operators for uncompressing, summing, and recompressing vectors. Shi et al. (2019a) introduced the global top-k method, which focuses on selecting the top k gradients across all workers, while Shi et al. (2019b) provided convergence results for this method. Renggli et al. (2019) presented SparCML, a framework that efficiently aggregates sparse vectors while retaining non-sparse coordinates, offering flexibility in representation.

The paper also addresses gradient sparsification aimed at enhancing accuracy rather than merely reducing communication bandwidth. Shokri and Shmatikov (2015) explored this in the context of privacy, while Sinha et al. (2020) examined top-k sparsification to improve GAN training quality by using a critic network to select the best samples for updates.

The authors emphasize that parallelizing mini-batch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is fundamentally about calculating an average of gradients, which serves as a lower-variance estimate of the full gradient. Several studies have sought optimal communication bounds for distributed mean estimation. However, the discussed sparsification techniques do not achieve exact distributed mean estimation but rather use error feedback to address approximation errors.

Additionally, the paper highlights that the gradients of a layer's input, or "errors," can also be sparsified. Sun et al. (2017) introduced the meProp method, applying top-k sparsification to errors to reduce computational operations, resulting in sparse gradient updates. Wei et al. (2017) demonstrated that this approach can achieve up to 95% gradient sparsity, showcasing its potential for efficiency in distributed training systems.
