The paper discusses the concept of sparsity in deep learning, particularly focusing on probabilistic modeling of input-output relationships. It begins by establishing that each input example x has a probability of being assigned a label y, rather than a fixed output. This is formalized using the joint probability distribution Qx,y, which is derived from the input distribution Qx and the conditional output distribution Qy|x. The goal of learning in this context is to minimize the distance, typically measured by Kullback-Leibler divergence, between the target joint distribution and a learned distribution represented by the model parameters w.

The paper further explains that the task can also be framed as predicting the output y given an input x, which leads to the formulation of the conditional distribution P(y|x; w). The relationship between the loss function and the negative log-likelihood of the model's output distribution is highlighted, indicating that the loss for a sample can be expressed in terms of the model's probability density function.

A significant concept introduced is the Fisher information matrix, which serves a similar purpose to the Hessian matrix but in a probabilistic context. The Fisher matrix measures the distance in terms of KL divergence and is defined based on the model's conditional distribution. It is shown that when the model's output distribution matches the empirical distribution of the data, the Fisher and Hessian matrices become equivalent, allowing for a more efficient approximation of the Hessian using the Fisher matrix.

The paper concludes by addressing the empirical Fisher, which is a practical approximation of the Fisher matrix that utilizes the empirical training distribution instead of the model distribution. This simplification aids in the implementation of the discussed concepts in real-world deep learning applications.
