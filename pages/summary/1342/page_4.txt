The table evaluates various validation technologies for autonomous systems, highlighting their characteristics, tool support, coverage, regression strategies, strengths, weaknesses, effectiveness, and efficiency. 

1. **Modeling and Simulation Environments** (SIL, HIL, MIL): These environments can handle both static and dynamic testing, using tools like MATLAB and dSPACE. They reduce validation costs and allow decoupling of hardware and software development. However, they require significant computational power and primarily test known scenarios, leading to inefficiencies.

2. **Function Testing**: This dynamic approach utilizes modeling tools and unit testing frameworks such as JUnit. It encompasses all AI functions, validating functional requirements. Despite its strengths, it may not validate entire systems comprehensively.

3. **Integration Testing**: This dynamic method employs test suites and combinatorial tools to verify component integration. While effective in testing interfaces, it may overlook some connections and complicate fault localization.

4. **Fault Injection**: This static method estimates residual defects by introducing specific faults. It helps identify weaknesses but requires a solid understanding of the system architecture.

5. **Negative Requirements Testing**: This static approach focuses on safety, security, and usability by modeling negative scenarios. It effectively identifies scenarios to avoid and formalizes non-functional requirements, though it can be challenging to implement systematically and may not cover all negative cases.

Overall, each method presents unique benefits and limitations in validating autonomous systems, emphasizing the need for a combination of strategies for comprehensive evaluation.
