The paper discusses the integration of machine learning into epidemiologic practice, highlighting key concepts and terminology to bridge the gap between the two fields. It reviews basic machine learning principles, categorizing them into supervised, unsupervised, and semisupervised learning. 

Supervised learning is similar to traditional epidemiologic model-fitting, where outcome values are known, while unsupervised learning identifies natural relationships in data without predefined outcomes. Semisupervised learning combines labeled and unlabeled data to enhance model performance, which is particularly useful given the high cost of labeling large datasets.

The paper also differentiates between classification and regression algorithms within supervised learning, with classification predicting categorical outcomes and regression predicting continuous outcomes. Furthermore, it distinguishes between generative and discriminative algorithms, explaining that discriminative algorithms model the conditional probability of an outcome, while generative algorithms model the joint probability distribution, allowing for the generation of new data points.

Lastly, reinforcement learning is described as a trial-and-error approach where systems improve performance over time based on feedback, resembling aspects of supervised and semisupervised learning. The paper concludes by outlining recommendations for incorporating these machine learning techniques into epidemiology, addressing both opportunities and challenges.
