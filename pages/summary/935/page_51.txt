The paper discusses the evolution of artificial intelligence (AI) from its early concepts to its resurgence in the 1990s. Newell and Simon were pioneers in heuristic search, applying it to mathematical theorem proofs with their Logic Theorist and General Problem Solver programs. Early advancements in computer vision, such as character recognition and face recognition, were established by Selfridge and others. The late sixties saw the emergence of natural language processing and mobile robotics with Shakey, while Samuel's Checkers-playing program exemplified early machine learning systems. Rosenblatt's Perceptron laid the groundwork for artificial neural networks, and Feigenbaum promoted expert systems for specialized domains.

Despite these advancements, by the 1980s, AI faced challenges, including a disconnect between theoretical progress and practical applications. The emphasis on symbolic reasoning and Boolean logic neglected the need for physical grounding and uncertainty quantification, leading to a decline in interest and funding known as the "AI winter."

The 1990s marked a resurgence in AI, recognizing that traditional approaches were inadequate. Progress in hardware, data gathering through the Internet, and computational capabilities enabled the development of intelligent systems from the ground up. This shift allowed AI to significantly influence daily life. The paper concludes by highlighting that while specialization in AI sub-fields has driven technical advancements, synthesizing intelligence on a larger scale requires the integration of various ideas.
