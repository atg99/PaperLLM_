The paper discusses the framework and characteristics of Neural Architecture Search (NAS), illustrated in the provided figure. NAS typically begins with a predefined set of operations and employs a controller to generate numerous candidate neural architectures within a defined search space. These candidates are trained and ranked based on their performance on a validation set, and this ranking informs adjustments to the search strategy. The process continues until a termination condition is met, at which point the best architecture is evaluated on a test set.

The early NAS methods largely adhered to this framework, with notable approaches including NAS-RL, which uses recurrent neural networks (RNN) as controllers optimized through reinforcement learning; MetaQNN, which frames architecture selection as a Markov decision process utilizing Q-learning; and Large-scale Evolution, which employs evolutionary algorithms to derive optimal architectures with minimal human intervention. GeNet introduces a unique coding scheme for neural architectures using fixed-length binary strings, relying on genetic operations for modification.

Despite their contributions to automating neural architecture generation, early NAS methods faced limitations due to their reliance on a global search space, necessitating extensive search strategies across vast architecture possibilities, and a discrete search strategy that simplifies architectural variations to basic operations. This resulted in high search costs and restricted broader application of early NAS techniques.
