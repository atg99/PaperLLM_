The paper discusses advancements in Neural Architecture Search (NAS), particularly focusing on the MdeNAS approach, which introduces a performance ranking hypothesis. This hypothesis posits that the relative performance of neural architectures remains consistent from early training stages to convergence. MdeNAS validates this through extensive experiments, allowing for quicker performance ranking of candidate architectures, thus accelerating the NAS process.

The paper also emphasizes the necessity of training candidate architectures from scratch and to convergence, despite this area receiving less research attention compared to other optimization strategies. It categorizes and compares existing NAS methods, including reinforcement learning (RL), evolutionary algorithms (EA), gradient optimization (GO), random search (RS), and sequential model-based optimization (SMBO). Each method's framework is illustrated, highlighting how they sample and evaluate neural architectures to optimize performance.

Challenges in comparing NAS performance are acknowledged, particularly due to the lack of standardized baselines and the significant variations in preprocessing, hyperparameters, and search techniques. Despite these challenges, random search is noted to serve as a robust baseline in some studies.

Overall, the paper provides a comprehensive overview of the current state of NAS research, focusing on performance evaluation methodologies and the implications of early training results on the architecture search process.
