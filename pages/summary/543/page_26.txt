The paper discusses the current state and challenges of Neural Architecture Search (NAS), highlighting its applications in various fields such as recognition, machine translation, and video understanding. Despite significant research advancements, NAS remains in its early stages compared to the rapid evolution of artificial neural architecture design. The focus of current NAS efforts is primarily on enhancing image classification accuracy and reducing search time and complexity.

A critical aspect of NAS is establishing a proper baseline to ensure that search strategies are not overshadowed by powerful augmentation techniques and design tricks. The paper critiques existing NAS search strategies, particularly those based on supernets, for their theoretical shortcomings. Early NAS approaches, like Large-scale Evolution, aimed for minimal human intervention but struggled with performance due to evolutionary inefficiencies and vast search spaces.

Subsequent works, such as NASNet, introduced a modular search space to reduce search costs but potentially compromised the freedom of architecture design. This raises concerns about whether modular searches could overlook superior architectures available in a global search context. The paper emphasizes the need to balance search freedom with performance.

Two critical issues are highlighted: the lack of standardized baselines and experimental protocols, making comparisons between NAS algorithms difficult, and the parameter sharing strategy, which, while improving efficiency, may lead to sub-optimal architecture rankings. The paper calls for more ablation studies to identify effective NAS components and stresses the importance of theoretical analysis to address these challenges for future NAS development.
