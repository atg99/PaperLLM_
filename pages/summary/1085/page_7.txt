The paper discusses the behavior of neural networks, particularly focusing on the propagation of signals through layers and the effects of weight matrices initialized with random values. The author notes that, due to the use of ReLU activation, the norm of the output from each layer is expected to be bounded by O(L), where L represents the number of layers. However, the reuse of the weight matrix across layers complicates this expectation as it limits the introduction of new randomness.

To address this, the paper introduces a mathematical framework using Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization to handle the randomness effectively. It explains that while one part of the transformation relies on previously utilized randomness, another part maintains independence, allowing for a bound on the spectral norm of the output.

The key argument is that through careful bounding, the norms of the hidden neuron outputs can be controlled, ensuring they do not grow excessively. Additionally, the paper establishes that the amount of "fresh new randomness" introduced in each layer is significant enough to support the separability of data across layers. This is formalized through an inductive argument leading to the conclusion that pairs of vectors remain separable even after multiple layers of processing.

Furthermore, the paper addresses the importance of both forward propagation and backward propagation in training neural networks. It presents results that provide bounds on the behavior of intermediate layers and the effects of diagonal matrices, emphasizing the relationship between sparsity and the network's capacity to maintain signal integrity.

In summary, the paper provides a detailed analysis of how randomness and signal propagation interact in multi-layer neural networks, establishing foundational properties that influence training and separability of data throughout the network.
