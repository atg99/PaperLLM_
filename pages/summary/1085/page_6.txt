This paper provides the first proof of convergence for Gradient Descent (GD) and Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) in the context of non-linear neural networks with more than two layers. The authors leverage two key theorems to demonstrate that as long as the objective value is high, the gradient remains large, and moving in the negative gradient direction sufficiently decreases the objective value. This analysis ensures that GD and SGD do not encounter saddle points or undesirable local minima during training. 

The paper also highlights an open question regarding the polynomial dependencies in their proofs and the role of randomness in SGD. While the authors do not delve into the complexities of correlated randomness, they suggest that this could explain the practical observation that the required size of certain parameters is not excessively large.

The authors focus on recurrent neural networks (RNNs) rather than simpler feedforward networks due to the independent interest in RNN memorization capabilities. They confirm that RNNs can learn to map distinct input tokens to different output tokens using the same recurrent unit. 

Additionally, the paper introduces new analytical tools for multi-layer networks with ReLU activations, which could advance research in deep learning. The techniques discussed address the avoidance of exponential growth or decay in activations and ensure stability against adversarial weight perturbations.

In the proof sketch, the authors outline the challenges of establishing Theorems 3 and 4 while emphasizing the importance of managing randomness and preventing exponential blow-up in the parameters. They provide insights into the properties of RNNs at random initialization, demonstrating that forward propagation does not lead to extreme behavior in activations.

Overall, the paper presents significant findings that contribute to understanding the convergence behaviors of GD and SGD in deep learning architectures, particularly with RNNs, while also paving the way for further research on stability and activation dynamics.
