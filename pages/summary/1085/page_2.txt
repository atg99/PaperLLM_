This paper investigates the theoretical foundations and training dynamics of recurrent neural networks (RNNs), focusing particularly on the use of rectified linear units (ReLUs) as activation functions. RNNs are essential for tasks involving sequential data, such as natural language processing and speech recognition, yet their theoretical underpinnings remain poorly understood. The authors highlight the challenges associated with training RNNs, notably the vanishing and exploding gradient problems, which complicate the learning process.

The paper poses critical questions regarding the ability of ReLUs to stabilize training and enable RNNs to achieve near-zero training error under certain conditions. It underscores that while achieving low training error is often desirable, for RNNs, the capacity to memorize training examples can actually be beneficial, given their memory-oriented tasks.

A key result focuses on the classical Elman network structure with ReLU activation. The authors present a regression task framework where training sequences are assumed to be separable. By establishing a connection between RNNs and Turing machines, the paper emphasizes the potential of RNNs in algorithm learning, positing that understanding their trainability is a foundational step toward elucidating their broader implications.

Overall, this work aims to bridge the theoretical gaps surrounding RNNs and provide insights into their efficient training mechanisms, specifically when utilizing ReLUs as activations.
