The paper discusses the limitations of fully connected (FC) layers in neural networks, particularly in handling spatial information and the excessive number of parameters they generate, which can lead to overfitting. FC layers analyze individual pixel values without considering their spatial relationships, making it challenging to understand the input. For instance, a 100x100 RGB image results in a massive number of connections for each neuron, complicating the learning process.

In contrast, convolutional layers address these issues by utilizing filters that are 3D weight matrices designed to capture spatial features. Each filter is convolved over the input to produce feature maps, where lower layers detect simple features (like edges) and higher layers capture more complex patterns (like textures and shapes). This hierarchical feature extraction increases the receptive field, allowing for more sophisticated feature encoding as layers progress.

Convolutional layers promote efficiency by applying the same filter across the entire image, thereby enforcing spatial invariance and sharing parameters. For example, a 5x5 filter applied to a 100x100 RGB image results in significantly fewer parameters compared to FC layers, minimizing the risk of overfitting and aiding optimization.

Pooling layers typically accompany convolutional layers, facilitating dimensionality reduction and speeding up processing by aggregating pixel values. The paper references notable convolutional networks like LeNet5, AlexNet, Inception, and ResNet, which have advanced image classification tasks.

Additionally, the paper introduces depth-separable convolutional layers, which optimize the convolution process. This method involves two steps: first, a point-wise convolution using 1x1 filters to adjust depth, followed by a spatial convolution with larger filters. This approach drastically reduces the number of parameters and computational costs compared to traditional convolution, enhancing efficiency in processing while maintaining output dimensions.
