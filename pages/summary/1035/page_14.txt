The paper discusses the distinction between trust and mere reliance, emphasizing that trust encompasses both normative and affective components. Trust involves the expectation that a trusted individual will act based on normative reasons, whereas mere reliance does not imply such expectations. Affective responses, such as moral blame, arise when trust is betrayed, differentiating it from disappointment experienced when relying on non-agential entities like machines.

The author argues that artificial intelligence (AI) cannot be trusted in the same way as humans because it lacks the capability to act on reasons and cannot be held accountable for its actions. AI, described as a sophisticated tool rather than a moral agent, should not be anthropomorphized or viewed as a bearer of trust. This misconception can undermine the responsibility of those who develop and implement AI systems, thus obscuring accountability and the need for regulation.

The paper critiques the narrative of "trustworthy AI," suggesting it may serve commercial interests by delaying regulation and masking the ethical implications of AI development. The author warns against the instrumentalization of ethics influenced by political and economic factors, arguing for the necessity of holding developers and institutions accountable for AI systems rather than attributing trustworthiness to the technology itself.
