The paper critiques the role of political advisory groups in the realm of AI ethics, suggesting that their purpose may be to engage in 'ethics washing'—a term used to describe superficial ethical discussions aimed at delaying substantial legal regulations that could impose costs on the industry. The author highlights how AI-developing companies, often supported by the European Commission, create internal ethics units or fund academic research to project an ethical image while sidelining independent ethical expertise.

The central focus of the ethical guidelines for AI is the concept of 'trustworthy AI,' which is defined by three key components: legality, ethical adherence, and robustness. However, the author argues that these guidelines incorrectly position the AI system itself as the entity worthy of trust, neglecting the critical distinction between trusting the technology and trusting the people and institutions behind it. This oversight is described as a conceptual flaw that undermines the integrity of ethical assessments, as it fails to recognize that trust is fundamentally different from mere reliance.

The paper emphasizes that labeling AI systems as trustworthy can mislead stakeholders, creating a false sense of security while obscuring necessary ethical scrutiny. The critique is reinforced by referencing the insights of Metzinger, who warns that such terminology may serve more as a marketing strategy than a genuine ethical commitment. Ultimately, the author calls for a more nuanced understanding of trust in the context of AI, advocating for a critical examination of both the technology and the human elements involved in its development and deployment.
