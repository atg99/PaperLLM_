The paper critiques the prevalent deontological approach in academia regarding ethical principles, which often manifests as lists of values and maxims. This approach is characterized as a "law conception of ethics," which aims to mirror legal frameworks or contribute to their development. The author questions the efficacy and necessity of these lists, particularly in the context of the technology sector, where ethical guidelines proliferate, leading to confusion and "ethics shopping."

The work of Floridi and Cowls is highlighted as a notable example of this trend. They identify the problem of excessive ethical guidelines and propose a coherent framework that synthesizes existing principles while advocating for broader cultural and geographical perspectives. Their framework has reportedly influenced international recommendations on AI ethics, though the paper raises concerns about the implications of this influence and the exclusion of other guidelines.

The author emphasizes the need for a critical examination of how ethics are constructed and the motivations behind the creation of ethical guidelines, suggesting that commercial interests may distort the moral quality of these lists. The interaction between academic ethics and commercial practices is acknowledged as a significant factor in shaping the discourse on technology ethics.
