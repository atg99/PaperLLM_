The paper discusses the complexities and challenges in ethical decision-making, particularly in contexts involving diverse perspectives influenced by personal, religious, and educational backgrounds. It highlights how discussions among commissioners, contrary to expectations, often lead to agreements not through consensus on moral principles, but by focusing on the specific practical issues at hand. This distinction is drawn from the work of Jonsen and Toulmin, who identify two contrasting approaches to ethics: one that seeks universally valid principles and another that emphasizes understanding ethical issues in their specific contexts.

The historical roots of this distinction are traced back to Aristotle, who differentiated between two modes of knowing: episteme, which is theoretical and universal, and phronesis, which is practical and context-dependent. Phronesis involves making judgments in concrete situations without assuming universal applicability. This practical approach influenced the development of casuistry, a method of moral reasoning that analyzes issues within their context through case studies and comparisons. Jonsen and Toulmin summarize casuistry as a reasoning process that leads to expert opinions on moral obligations, framed in general but not universal terms, applicable to specific conditions.

The paper argues that the dominant understanding of ethics today still leans towards the theoretical approach, often resulting in argumentative deadlock, while a more practical, context-oriented understanding is essential for effective moral deliberation.
