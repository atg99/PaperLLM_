The paper discusses the practice of casuistry and its ethical implications, drawing parallels with medical ethics. It emphasizes the necessity of phronesis, or practical wisdom, in applying moral principles fairly across various cases. Critics, like Blaise Pascal, argue that casuists' detailed analyses diverged from core Christian moral beliefs, leading to accusations of moral laxity. This criticism prompted a push towards more stable ethical theories, often at the expense of practical application. 

The decline of the Catholic Church further complicated the practice of casuistry, highlighting the importance of institutional culture in shaping moral practices. Unlike medical ethics, which had a secure institutional framework, casuistry's grounding became unstable as Enlightenment thinkers sought more scientific foundations for morality. 

In the twentieth century, Jonsen and Toulmin note a continued shift from practical concerns to theoretical moral philosophy in Britain and the USA, with philosophers often engaging with practical issues primarily to evaluate theoretical positions. This contrasts with Aristotle's view of ethics as inherently practical. The paper underscores the need for a robust institutional context to support effective moral practices like casuistry.
