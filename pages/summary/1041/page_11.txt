The authors emphasize the importance of grounding ethical judgments in the specific practices they aim to evaluate. They argue that effective criticism and practical wisdom, or phronesis, require a deep understanding of the activity being assessed. This understanding is crucial for making sound judgments, as the legitimacy of one's moral evaluations is contingent upon their connection to the values and norms inherent in the practice in question. Additionally, the impact of ethical interventions is reliant on their acceptance and application in real-world contexts.

The paper also raises a critical question about the role of individual philosophers and their capacity to instigate meaningful social change. Citing Theodor Adorno, it posits that individual efforts, while noble, are often inadequate to address systemic injustices, which are deeply rooted in social and economic structures. Adorno's perspective suggests a skepticism toward the belief that individuals can effect significant change in a modern context fraught with complex challenges.

Furthermore, the text advocates for a broader understanding of ethics that transcends narrow definitions and focuses on diverse domains such as the arts, history, and politics. This expanded view is necessary to tackle issues like ethics washing, where ethical considerations are superficially engaged without addressing underlying problems. The work of philosopher Raymond Geuss is referenced as offering a more nuanced perspective on the implications for political philosophy and data ethics, urging a shift away from isolated ethical discussions toward a more integrated approach that acknowledges the interconnectedness of various societal factors.
