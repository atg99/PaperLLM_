The paper conducts an analysis of the performance of large language models (LLMs) in understanding verbal user commands related to automated driving (AD) systems. It compares the LLMs against two baselines: a random guessing strategy and a rule-based method that utilizes keywords generated by ChatGPT-4. The results indicate that LLMs, particularly the GPT series, outperform these baselines, with GPT-4 achieving a peak accuracy of 89.02% at the question level.

The evaluation highlights that while GPT-4 excels in identifying commands that require specific submodules, it struggles with in-cabin monitoring tasks, displaying an accuracy of only 74.89%. A significant portion of errors arises from misclassifying commands as requiring in-cabin monitoring, often linked to keywords like "multimedia" and "alert system." This ambiguity in defining in-cabin monitoring complicates the assessment of GPT-4's responses.

Two ablation studies were conducted to analyze the effects of instruction formats and the number of examples provided. The first study tested detailed explanations of instructions through step-by-step versus consolidated formats, revealing that the few-shot method yielded the best performance. The second study demonstrated that providing two examples significantly improved accuracy, although additional examples offered diminishing returns.

In conclusion, the paper emphasizes the importance of human intent in the design of AD systems and suggests future work should focus on integrating human feedback more effectively to enhance the reliability and reasoning capabilities of these systems.
