This paper investigates the impact of attention mechanisms on the classification performance of sound event recognition models using the UrbanSound8K dataset. The authors utilize the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, batch size of 100, and 200 training epochs, measuring effectiveness through classification accuracy.

The experimental results reveal several key findings: 
1. The implementation of attention mechanisms significantly enhances model classification performance compared to models without such mechanisms.
2. The frequency attention mechanism outperforms the temporal attention mechanism, suggesting that focusing on frequency features is more beneficial for environmental sounds, which are unstructured.
3. Among various combination strategies tested, the weight combination strategy yields superior performance, indicating that time and frequency features contribute unequally to classification.

Further analysis compares attention mechanisms derived from the HPSS algorithm and the original log-Mel spectrogram. Results indicate that the harmonic and percussion spectrograms provide clearer structural features, resulting in better classification performance.

The paper also explores the performance of different attention mechanisms across various sound events. While the overall model performance improves with attention mechanisms, their effectiveness varies by sound type. Temporal attention mechanisms notably enhance accuracy for transient sounds like "gunshot" and "dog bark," while frequency attention mechanisms are more effective for continuous sounds such as "siren" and "air conditioner." 

Despite improvements, some categories, including "air conditioner," "car horn," "children playing," and "gunshot," show limited performance enhancement, suggesting that a single set of learning parameters may not be optimal for all sound categories due to their unique time-frequency characteristics.

Finally, the confusion matrix highlights challenges in distinguishing certain categories, notably "children playing," while "siren," "gunshot," and "car horn" are classified with high accuracy, likely influenced by their sample size disparities.
