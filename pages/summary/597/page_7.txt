The paper discusses the challenges of domain adaptation, particularly in learning invariant representations when there is a discrepancy between the marginal label distributions of source and target domains. It emphasizes the importance of incorporating task-related information into domain adaptation algorithms. The authors introduce several information-theoretic concepts, notably the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence, which serves as a bounded measure of the difference between two distributions.

Key lemmas are presented that establish relationships between the JS distance of predicted and true label distributions and the accuracy of the prediction function. Specifically, they demonstrate that if the label distributions differ significantly, the induced distributions over an intermediate representation space must also differ considerably to minimize joint error.

The paper concludes with a theorem indicating a necessary condition for successful domain adaptation: if the marginal label distributions are very different, attempting to minimize the distance between the representations in the source and target domains could lead to increased errors in prediction. A corollary extends this finding to scenarios where different transformation functions are applied in the source and target domains, reinforcing that such approaches may not mitigate the issues arising from differing label distributions.

Overall, the findings underscore that achieving invariant representations is essential but complicated by the inherent differences between source and target domains, and provide a theoretical foundation for understanding the limits of current domain adaptation strategies.
