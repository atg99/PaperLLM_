The paper discusses the optimization of a reward function in the context of reinforcement learning (RL) and its application to Linear Quadratic Regulator (LQR) problems. It begins by suggesting that setting a specific parameter to zero can serve as a good initial guess for optimization, despite the expected reward being low at that point. The analysis emphasizes the strong convexity of the cost function, noting that the expected norm of the gradient plays a crucial role in determining the number of iterations required for convergence.

The stochastic gradient derived from sampling a Gaussian distribution is detailed, with its expected norm highlighting a dependency on the dimension of the search space. Previous studies have established that the complexity of this method is significantly influenced by the dimensionality and the maximum reward magnitude. For noisy function evaluations, the convergence rate is affected, with a slower rate observed when rewards are adjusted by a constant offset, illustrating how minor changes can dramatically impact algorithm performance.

The paper then transitions to numerical comparisons of various RL paradigms applied to LQR, specifically focusing on a discrete-time double integrator system. It examines the performance of a model-based approach that integrates supervised learning and robust control, which shows near-optimal performance under a limited sampling budget. In contrast, approximate dynamic programming and direct policy search methods demonstrate poorer performance and higher variance, respectively.

The double integrator example serves as a test case, representing a system where the state comprises position and velocity, and the goal is to reach a target state with minimal force expenditure. The paper sets up the LQR framework to steer the system from an initial condition towards the target while minimizing the associated costs.
