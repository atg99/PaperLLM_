The paper discusses the computational limits of deep learning, highlighting the tradeoff between model complexity and computational efficiency. It emphasizes that while larger models with many parameters can achieve better performance due to their flexibility, they also require significantly more computational resources and data. This results in a performance plateau for smaller models that lack essential parameters, as they cannot reach lower RMSE values regardless of computation.

Historically, the computational requirements of neural networks have restricted their capabilities. Early models, such as the 3-layer perceptron, faced limitations due to insufficient computing power, which hindered their ability to learn complex functions like XOR. Although deeper networks were suggested as a potential solution, advancements were stymied by the lack of available technology.

Over the decades, improvements in hardware have led to significant performance gains, with an approximate 50,000-fold increase in computing power. However, even by 2009, deep learning models were still too slow for large-scale applications. The pivotal moment came when deep learning transitioned to GPU and later ASIC implementations, resulting in substantial speedups and leading to breakthroughs in various domains, such as image classification and speech recognition.

From 2012 to 2019, the computational requirements for cutting-edge deep learning systems grew rapidly, approximately 10-fold per year, outpacing the improvements in hardware performance. This trend underscores the increasing demand for computational resources in the field of deep learning, raising concerns about sustainability and accessibility in the future.
