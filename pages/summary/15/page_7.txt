The paper discusses advancements in convolutional neural networks (CNNs), particularly focusing on ShuffleNet and GhostNet. ShuffleNet v1 introduces computationally economical pointwise group convolution and channel shuffle techniques, optimizing the architecture by removing a ReLU activation function and incorporating average pooling with concatenation to reduce parameters. 

ShuffleNet v2 builds on this by emphasizing the importance of Memory Access Cost (MAC) alongside computation complexity (FLOPs) in evaluating network speed. It proposes four design guidelines based on experimental findings: maintaining equal input and output channel ratios for minimal MAC, understanding the impact of group convolution on training speed, minimizing network fragmentation for better parallel efficiency, and reducing the frequency of elementwise operations to save time. The ShuffleNet v2 unit incorporates these principles, introducing a channel split mechanism for improved processing.

The paper also reviews historical CNN architectures, noting that LeNet-5 was the first modern CNN used effectively for handwritten digit recognition. Subsequent models like AlexNet, VGGNet, GoogLeNet, and ResNet contributed to deeper and wider network designs, addressing issues like gradient vanishing and enhancing accuracy. MobileNets and ShuffleNet were developed for mobile applications, focusing on lightweight designs suitable for devices with limited computational power.

GhostNet is introduced as a solution to the redundancy of features in existing CNNs, proposing a division of traditional convolution layers into two parts. This approach uses fewer convolution kernels initially and applies linear transformations to generate multiple feature maps, demonstrating that the Ghost module can be integrated into various CNN models. 

In summary, the paper highlights the evolution of CNN architectures, emphasizing efficiency and computational effectiveness through innovative design strategies.
