The paper addresses the limitations of current self-supervised representation learning methods, which primarily focus on image-level comparisons, leading to representations that are not well-suited for pixel-level tasks such as object detection and semantic segmentation. To tackle this issue, the authors introduce pixel-level pretext tasks for self-supervised visual representation learning.

The first proposed method, PixContrast, employs pixel-level contrastive learning, treating each pixel as a unique class. Features from the same pixel, extracted from two random image crops, form positive pairs, while features from different pixels serve as negative pairs. A contrastive loss is then applied to learn the representation.

The second method, PixPro, relies on pixel-to-propagation consistency. Here, positive pairs are generated through two asymmetric pipelines. One pipeline uses a standard backbone network, while the other includes a pixel propagation module that smooths pixel features by incorporating information from similar pixels. Unlike PixContrast, PixPro does not rely on negative pairs, which can complicate contrastive learning.

Empirical results demonstrate that PixPro outperforms PixContrast across various downstream tasks. The pixel-level pretext tasks effectively initialize both backbone and head networks in dense downstream tasks, which is particularly beneficial for scenarios with limited annotated data. The authors emphasize that the pixel-level approach complements existing instance-level methods, combining strengths in spatial sensitivity and categorization ability.

The paper presents substantial advancements in performance metrics, achieving state-of-the-art results on benchmarks like Pascal VOC for object detection and Cityscapes for semantic segmentation. The authors advocate for a shift in focus from linear classification on ImageNet to evaluating performance on downstream tasks, highlighting the promise of pixel-level approaches in unsupervised representation learning. 

In the related works section, the authors contrast their pixel-level pretext tasks with existing instance discrimination methods, noting that while instance-level approaches have made significant progress, they have not translated to substantial improvements in downstream task performance. The paper asserts the potential of pixel-level representation learning to achieve superior results compared to traditional instance-level methods.
