This paper discusses the role of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) in the immune response to pathogens, particularly focusing on flagellin-mediated interactions and various receptor types, including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs), and RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs).

TLRs, such as TLR1-6, function as monomers in solution and dimerize upon ligand binding, while TLR8 and TLR9 are preformed dimers that undergo conformational changes upon ligand interaction. TLR10 recognizes double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) at endosomal pH, facilitating MyD88 recruitment and inhibiting type I interferon production. TLR11 and TLR12 are crucial in recognizing Toxoplasma gondii through specific motifs.

NLRs are intracellular receptors essential for detecting cytoplasmic pathogens. They consist of a nucleotide-binding domain (NBD), leucine-rich repeats (LRRs), and an N-terminal effector domain. NOD1 and NOD2 are the most studied NLRs, with NOD1 recognizing components from Gram-negative bacteria and NOD2 detecting muramyl dipeptide and complete viral single-stranded RNA. Their activation involves conformational changes that expose the NBD, leading to oligomerization and recruitment of signaling proteins.

RLRs, including RIG-I, MDA5, and LGP2, play a vital role in antiviral immunity by recognizing viral nucleic acids. RIG-I contains a helicase domain and CARDs for signaling, while MDA5 lacks self-inhibitory functions. LGP2 regulates RIG-I and MDA5 activity and can negatively influence their signaling pathways.

The paper emphasizes the importance of PRR modifications, such as palmitoylation, in regulating immune signaling, providing insights into host innate immune responses against pathogens.
