The training time for artificial neural networks (ANN) is influenced by a combination of factors including mini-batch size, learning rate, and optimizer selection. The study indicates that after hyperparameter optimization (HOpt), the training time for ANN using the TbPT method is slightly reduced due to a larger learning rate that accelerates convergence. However, other structural configurations exhibit slower convergence despite having larger learning rates, attributed to smaller mini-batch sizes that increase the number of batches trained per epoch, thereby extending training time but reducing the total epochs needed for convergence. This suggests that mini-batch size is the primary factor affecting training time, followed by learning rate, while other hyperparameters tend to impact model accuracy more than training costs.

When comparing the training times of ANN and Random Forest Regression (RFR) with Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) and Support Vector Machines (SVM), it is noted that ANN and RFR are more sensitive to hyperparameter adjustments. HOpt significantly enhances ANN performance, making it a preferred method if computational resources are sufficient; otherwise, GPR is recommended for HOpt. Caution is advised when applying HOpt to SVM, as small epsilon bounds or high error penalization (C) can lead to complex training objectives and increased convergence costs.

The discussion section analyzes how HOpt improves machine learning algorithm (MLA) performance in relation to design problem characteristics such as structure type, design variables, load type, and continuity of the design domain. The findings highlight that the dimensionality and nonlinearity of design problems, influenced by structure and load types, significantly affect the accuracy and robustness of surrogate models. Constraints lead to discontinuous feasible domains, complicating MLA model training, particularly for mixed-variable problems. The study provides a summary of accuracy and robustness improvements for each MLA model post-HOpt in relation to benchmark problems, quantified as a percentage reduction in mean values.
