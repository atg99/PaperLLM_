The paper highlights a significant issue known as the robustness problem, where models demonstrate high accuracy under standard conditions yet fail dramatically when faced with even slight distributional shifts. Despite achieving over 30% accuracy on aggressively low-pass filtered images and more than 50% on high-pass filtered images, it raises questions about how models can perform well using input features that are nearly invisible to humans.

The authors argue that models' high performance in controlled settings is misleading, as their reliance on particular input-output correlations can lead to poor generalization when these correlations are disrupted. They illustrate this with an example from prior work where models trained on a variant of the MNIST dataset struggled when a crucial pixel was removed, showcasing how the simplest statistical explanations can fail in less ideal scenarios.

Furthermore, the paper discusses the texture hypothesis, which posits that models trained on natural images often depend on local statistical features related to texture. Such reliance makes them vulnerable to real-world corruptions, which can distort these features and compromise robustness. The authors conclude that a wide array of correlations in image data, including colors, textures, and high-frequency patterns, can contribute to a model's remarkable performance under ideal conditions but also highlight its brittleness when faced with unexpected variations.
