The paper discusses the blockchain as an immutable database maintained by its users, where transactions are encrypted and validated through consensus protocols like proof-of-work or proof-of-stake. Each node in the network holds an identical copy of the blockchain, ensuring that all transactions are permanently recorded and linked through traceable hashes. Any tampering with a block would necessitate altering all subsequent blocks across all copies, making it highly secure. The longest chain of events is deemed valid, and attempts to create a rival chain require significant computational resources, incentivizing miners with rewards such as bitcoin for validating blocks.

In the context of healthcare, blockchain can create a common, distributed, and immutable database of medical information, allowing healthcare providers to access data securely and efficiently. This decentralization eliminates single points of failure, enhances security and privacy, reduces administrative burdens, and improves data sharing among providers. The blockchain democratizes data management, enabling anyone to contribute and ensuring that the most accurate version of events is maintained through consensus.
