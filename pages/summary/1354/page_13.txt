The paper discusses systemic perception faults that occur in significant regions of the input space and persist over time, leading to misinterpretation of the environment. These faults can arise from measurement errors, such as misplacement of objects or inaccuracies in bounding boxes, as well as misidentification of objects like stop signs. It emphasizes the necessity of excluding these systemic faults during development, proposing simulation as an effective method for detection. By creating a simulated model of the world and comparing the constructed model from sensor interpretation against the original, it is possible to assess the presence of systemic faults.

The authors differentiate between systemic faults and local perception faults, the latter being isolated issues that can be managed through methods like freezing or extrapolating values or utilizing redundant sensors. If prediction errors are persistent or shared among multiple sensors, this indicates a significant surprise, necessitating action from higher-level functions to repair or regenerate the model.

The paper also highlights that the primary role of AI functions at this level is to address uncertainties in sensor interpretation and enhance situation awareness, which ultimately contributes to the safety of the model's approximation of reality. The safety argument presented diverges from traditional sensor fusion approaches, leveraging predictive processing to create a more structured and potentially deductive assurance case. The paper concludes by acknowledging that further investigation is required in this area.
