This paper discusses a pruning algorithm for neural networks that leverages importance approximations based on gradients. The key concept is the calculation of the importance of neuron parameters, represented as I(1)m(z), which connects to information theory through the variance of gradients and the expected Fisher information matrix. 

The pruning process involves iteratively fine-tuning a trained network by removing neurons with the lowest importance scores. This is achieved through the following steps: computing parameter gradients and updating weights, averaging importance scores over minibatches, and pruning the least important neurons after a set number of minibatches. The algorithm accommodates for computational constraints by approximating the Hessian and employing an exponential moving average for importance score accumulation.

Implementation details include placing gates after batch normalization layers to capture contributions from scaling and shifting, and determining the number of neurons to prune based on their correlation. The experiments are conducted on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet datasets, examining various network architectures and methods to identify the most effective pruning strategies. The findings indicate that careful tuning of pruning parameters and strategies can significantly impact performance outcomes.
