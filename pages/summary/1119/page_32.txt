The selection of an algorithm for optimization problems should consider both the dimensions of the problem and the algorithm's capability to operate within a specified budget of function evaluations. Most algorithms have been tested primarily on bi-objective problems, with limited exploration of higher dimensions in both objectives and decision variables. This limitation is evident in the literature, where only a few algorithms have been evaluated on problems with more than 30 decision variables.

Moreover, many real-world scenarios impose strict limits on the number of function evaluations due to time constraints, making it critical to choose algorithms that can deliver effective solutions within a smaller budget, often far below the 50,000 evaluations used in benchmarking studies.

The paper identifies several key issues with current algorithms in the literature, including the types of test problems utilized, the dimensions of these problems, and the underutilization of constrained problems. Other concerns involve the neglect of training time for metamodels, insufficient accuracy of these metamodels, poorly structured ensemble approaches, and a lack of algorithms that leverage available problem information.

Benchmark problems are typically simpler and quicker to evaluate, serving to test the efficiency of algorithms without the computational burden of real-world problems. However, real-world problems present significant challenges due to their complexity and uncertainty, which complicates the thorough testing of EAs. The authors advocate for a balanced approach that includes a variety of benchmark and real-world problems to improve the robustness of EAs, while noting that the scarcity of accessible real-world problems poses a significant barrier to this practice. 

Overall, the paper highlights the need for greater attention to the dimensions of optimization problems, the constraints involved, and the overall efficacy of algorithms in addressing real-world challenges.
