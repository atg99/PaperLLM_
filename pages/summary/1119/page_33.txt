This paper discusses the limitations and challenges in the field of many-objective optimization, particularly in the context of surrogate-based algorithms. It highlights that most existing studies focus on problems with three or fewer objectives and typically involve fewer than 60 decision variables. The authors point out the scarcity of research addressing the efficacy of various metamodels as the number of decision variables increases, suggesting a need for further exploration in this area.

The paper identifies key issues related to metamodel usage, particularly the often-overlooked training time required to build or update metamodels, which may not always be negligible compared to the time taken to compute objective and constraint values. Furthermore, many algorithms employ a fixed evolution control strategy, neglecting the importance of metamodel accuracy. The authors propose an adaptive evolution control strategy that adjusts based on metamodel performance, allowing for predefined or user-adjusted accuracy metrics.

Additionally, the selection criteria for individuals re-evaluated with original functions during metamodel updates are underexplored, despite their potential impact on solution quality. The paper emphasizes the need to consider multiple criteria, such as accuracy, diversity of individuals, and training time, when structuring ensembles of metamodels.

Finally, the authors advocate for incorporating information regarding computational costs associated with different objective and constraint functions into evolutionary algorithms, suggesting that a more nuanced understanding of these factors could enhance optimization efficacy. Overall, the paper calls for more comprehensive research into the dimensions and types of metamodels used in many-objective optimization, as well as the strategies for improving computational efficiency and solution quality.
