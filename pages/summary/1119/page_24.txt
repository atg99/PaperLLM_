The paper discusses the utilization of benchmark and real-world problems in algorithm testing, highlighting that seven algorithms were evaluated on benchmark problems, seven on real-world problems, and seven on both. It emphasizes the significance of computational efficiency, particularly for real-world applications, noting the challenges of optimization without approximations due to high computation times.

Regarding metamodel selection, the paper states that there is no universal guideline linking a specific metamodel to a problem type. It notes a preference for single metamodels over multiple or ensemble metamodels, with Kriging being the most frequently used, followed by neural networks, support vector regression, and polynomial regression. The authors argue that Kriging is one of the most effective interpolation methods available, supported by its strong mathematical foundation.

The management of metamodels and evolution control strategies is also crucial for performance. The study indicates that fixed evolution control strategies are more common than adaptive ones, which rely on the accuracy of the metamodel. Only a few algorithms employed adaptive strategies, which involve comparing the uncertainty of approximated values against predefined thresholds to determine whether to use the metamodel or the original function.

Additionally, the paper points out the underappreciation of training times for different metamodels, even though these can be significant. It finds that dominance-based evolutionary algorithms (EAs), particularly NSGA-II, are more prevalent than other types like decomposition-based or indicator-based algorithms.

Finally, the paper addresses problem and fitness approximations, which aim to simplify the original problem to enhance computational efficiency, particularly in complex scenarios like computational fluid dynamics or structural analysis.
