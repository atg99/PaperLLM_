discusses various strategies to reduce the computational burden associated with Neural Architecture Search (NAS). One method involves using lower fidelity performance metrics, such as shorter training times, smaller datasets, or reduced image resolutions. While these proxy metrics can decrease computational costs, they may lead to biased performance estimates, often underestimating true performance. This issue might be less critical if the search strategy focuses on ranking architectures, as long as the relative rankings remain stable. However, significant discrepancies between low-fidelity estimates and full evaluations can alter these rankings, suggesting a need for a gradual increase in fidelity during the search.

Another approach discussed is learning curve extrapolation, where initial learning curves are used to predict performance and terminate poor-performing architectures early in the search process. This method aims to make effective predictions based on limited evaluations in a large search space. Additionally, training surrogate models to predict performance based on architectural properties is proposed, emphasizing the challenge of making accurate predictions efficiently.

The paper also highlights the use of weight initialization from previously trained architectures to enhance performance estimation. Techniques like network morphisms allow for architectural modifications without losing the network's functionality, enabling incremental increases in network capacity. However, strict network morphisms may lead to overly complex architectures, a challenge that can be mitigated by approximate morphisms that allow for size reduction.

Lastly, One-Shot Architecture Search is presented as a promising method that treats all architectures as subgraphs of a single supergraph, sharing weights among them. This approach allows for quick evaluation of architectures by leveraging pre-trained weights from the one-shot model, significantly speeding up the performance estimation process, as it eliminates the need for separate training of each architecture.
