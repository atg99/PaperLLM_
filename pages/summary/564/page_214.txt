The analysis of the AutoML Challenge Series from 2015 to 2018 reveals several key insights regarding the methodologies employed by participants. One prominent finding is the prevalent use of one-hot encoding for categorical variables and simple normalization techniques. Most participants, particularly the top-ranking ones, did not engage in feature selection, and many ignored irrelevant variables, suggesting that ensembling methods demonstrated intrinsic robustness against such variables.

Unsupervised learning was minimally utilized, with participants primarily employing classical dimensionality reduction techniques like Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and Principal Component Analysis (PCA). In terms of transfer learning and meta-learning, only one team, aad_freiburg, utilized meta-learning to enhance hyper-parameter searches, highlighting a missed opportunity given the range of datasets available.

Deep learning was largely excluded from the AutoML phases due to resource limitations, except in the GPU track, where it did not outperform other methods. However, the aad_freiburg team did find success in utilizing deep learning during specific Tweakathon rounds.

The challenge encompassed four types of tasks—regression, binary classification, multi-class classification, and multi-label classification—alongside six scoring metrics. Despite the varying class balances and complexities, participants primarily employed generic methods, often post-fitting results to target metrics through cross-validation or ensembling.

A significant lesson learned is that many methods struggled to produce any reasonable results, with failures attributed to time constraints, memory issues, and numerical instabilities. This indicates that a reliable "basic model" capable of functioning across all datasets remains elusive. The auto-sklearn framework demonstrated strength in filtering out failing models, ensuring at least one viable result.

Lastly, the challenge infrastructure allowed for parallel processing, which teams utilized to varying degrees, with the aad_freiburg team effectively launching parallel model searches across multiple datasets. This strategic use of computational resources was reflected in their learning curves.
