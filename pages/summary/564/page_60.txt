Meta-learning, particularly in the context of neural networks, focuses on enhancing the transferability of learned models across various tasks. While large datasets like ImageNet have proven effective for pre-training models, their utility diminishes when the target tasks diverge significantly from the training data. To address this, meta-learners incorporate inductive biases derived from multiple related tasks, facilitating quicker learning for new problems.

Early approaches to meta-learning involved recurrent neural networks (RNNs) that could adjust their own weights based on task-specific errors, employing a differentiable, end-to-end training process. Subsequent advancements utilized reinforcement learning to adapt strategies such as learning rates. Researchers like Bengio et al. explored biologically-inspired weight updating rules, while others, like Santoro et al., introduced memory-augmented networks to enhance memory retention and retrieval from prior tasks.

Andrychowicz et al. innovated by substituting traditional optimizers with LSTMs trained on previous tasks, optimizing the meta-learner's loss based on the performance of base learners. This approach has been expanded to work with synthetic functions, allowing optimization without direct gradient access. Additionally, Li and Malik framed optimization algorithms as policies learned through reinforcement learning, with further studies applying this to shallow networks.

Overall, the field continues to evolve, incorporating various strategies like Bayesian modeling to enhance neural architecture search and optimize performance across tasks.
