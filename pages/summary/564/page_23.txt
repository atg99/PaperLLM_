Gaussian processes are a statistical method used for hyperparameter optimization, characterized by a mean function and a covariance function. In Bayesian optimization, the mean is often assumed constant, and predictions for noise-free scenarios can be derived using specific equations involving covariance matrices and observed values. The effectiveness of Gaussian processes largely hinges on the choice of the covariance function, with the Mátern 5/2 kernel being a common option.

However, standard Gaussian processes face limitations due to their cubic scaling with respect to data points, making them less suitable for scenarios with numerous evaluations. To address this, scalable approximations, such as sparse Gaussian processes, have been developed, though they raise concerns regarding the accuracy of uncertainty estimates and their effectiveness in traditional hyperparameter optimization.

Additionally, Gaussian processes struggle with high-dimensional spaces, prompting the development of various extensions to improve scalability and efficiency. These include techniques such as random embeddings and partitioning the configuration space.

In contrast, other machine learning models, particularly deep neural networks, offer greater scalability and flexibility for Bayesian optimization. They can serve as feature extractors or be integrated into a fully Bayesian framework. Neural networks typically outperform Gaussian processes in speed after a certain number of function evaluations, facilitating large-scale parallelism and allowing for optimization on more complex tasks. For example, variational auto-encoders can transform intricate inputs into a format manageable by Gaussian processes.

Overall, while Gaussian processes are foundational in hyperparameter optimization, advancements in alternative models, particularly neural networks, present promising avenues for improving scalability and handling complex optimization tasks.
