This paper explores the classification accuracy of various graph neural network models on the Cora dataset, particularly focusing on the impact of feature removal and noise. It presents findings that indicate models utilizing unmodified random walks, such as N-GCN, N-SAGE, and DCNN, perform better than convolutional models like GCN and SAGE when features are randomly removed. The results suggest that these random walk-based models can effectively recover lost features by leveraging information from neighboring nodes, with performance diminishing as more features are removed.

Additionally, the paper discusses the relationship between random walk steps and the depth of GCN layers. It clarifies that while both methods allow nodes to aggregate information from neighbors, they operate differently, with random walks relying on co-visit statistics and GCN introducing non-linearities at each layer. Experiments show that increasing the depth of GCN and SAGE models does not necessarily correlate with improved accuracy, contrasting with the performance benefits observed in the proposed models due to their broader receptive fields.

The paper also classifies existing graph learning algorithms into unsupervised embedding learning and supervised methods, laying the groundwork for a deeper exploration of the capabilities and limitations of these approaches. Overall, the findings underscore the robustness of random walk-based models in the presence of feature noise and their ability to maintain performance through effective information aggregation.
