The paper discusses the concept of dynamic certification for autonomous systems, emphasizing the need for a multi-stage testing approach that includes early-phase, transitional, and confirmatory testing. This iterative process allows for movement between stages based on knowledge and deployment intentions, akin to the FDA's method of gradually approving drugs through increasing scrutiny and oversight.

The authors argue that technology development involves both design and deployment decisions, which are often treated separately in static certification systems. They propose that dynamic certification should integrate design decisions, especially in early stages when changes are most impactful and cost-effective. Formal methods can aid in specifying autonomous systems, assessing acceptable risks, and tracking design changes over time.

Dynamic certification necessitates an ongoing evaluation of risks and benefits associated with deploying autonomous systems in varying contexts. While formal methods provide a foundation for specification and verification, they must be complemented by frameworks that accommodate ambiguities and uncertainties. The paper also highlights the importance of co-evolving regulatory and formal frameworks, as well as considering additional analysis methods such as assurance cases and structured interrogation of requirements.

To illustrate their framework, the authors present a scenario involving an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) and a ground-based delivery robot. The UAV must maintain a wireless connection to avoid hazardous situations, particularly when landing in proximity to the robot. The scenario underscores the need for a dynamic certification framework that can adapt to different operational contexts and safety properties, ensuring that autonomous systems operate safely and effectively within their environments.
