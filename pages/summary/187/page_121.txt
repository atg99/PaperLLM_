This paper discusses the peculiar trapping effects observed in power transistors with carbon-doped buffers, explained through the "leaky-dielectric" model. These effects arise from charge injection into the buffer region and hole redistribution within it. The physical origins of buffer traps are linked to either intrinsic defects in GaN or intentionally introduced impurities, particularly iron (Fe) and carbon (C), which serve to suppress n-type conductivity.

Fe is commonly used for RF transistors, introducing a deep level in the GaN bandgap, behaving as an acceptor-like electron trap located around 0.5-0.6 eV from the conduction band edge. In contrast, C is primarily used in power devices and can be incorporated through tuning growth parameters or by adding carbon precursors during chemical vapor deposition.

Early measurements indicated two deep levels associated with C doping: C_N (acceptor) and C_Ga (donor). Recent calculations have refined these levels, suggesting C_N at E_V+0.9 eV and C_Ga above E_C, along with other C-related centers. The consensus is that trapping effects in C-doped GaN transistors are primarily linked to C_N traps, although some studies indicate auto-compensation between acceptors and donor traps, which may reduce effective acceptor density.

Experimental evidence supports a higher donor concentration in GaN:C compared to undoped samples. The C_N level can induce compensation in the buffer region at appropriate concentrations, resulting in unique trapping effects that remain a topic of ongoing research.
