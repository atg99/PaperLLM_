The paper discusses the gate-charging process during the turn-on event of semiconductor devices, particularly focusing on the differences between GaN, Si, and SiC transistors. At turn-on, the gate-source voltage (VGS) increases, reaching the threshold voltage (Vth), which initiates current flow. The gate-source capacitance (CGS) is charged until the Miller voltage is reached, at which point the drain current stabilizes. The drive current then charges the Miller capacitance (CGD) until both capacitances are fully charged, allowing the gate voltage to rise further.

The total gate charge (QGS + QGD) is crucial as it represents the minimum charge needed to turn on the transistor and is indicative of switching losses. A lower total gate charge facilitates higher commutation speeds and reduces switching losses significantly, particularly for GaN devices compared to silicon counterparts. The paper highlights that the Ron·QG product, which combines on-resistance and gate charge, is essential for assessing switching efficiency. 

In a comparative analysis, GaN E-mode HEMTs show a much lower Ron·QG product (290-300 mΩ·nC) than Si superjunction MOSFETs (around 3800 mΩ·nC) and SiC FETs (1950-3480 mΩ·nC), demonstrating their potential for reduced switching losses. Additionally, the absence of a reverse-recovery charge in GaN devices, due to their lack of an intrinsic body diode, further enhances their efficiency, contrasting sharply with Si-based transistors that experience significant reverse recovery losses. This underscores the advantages of GaN technology in high-performance applications.
