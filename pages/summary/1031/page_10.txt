The paper discusses the balance between devolved and centralized responsibility in the context of algorithmic accountability and transparency. It highlights the potential for external algorithmic audits as a means to address issues related to bias and harm in AI systems. Tools such as 'Aequit as' and 'Turingbox' are mentioned as examples of platforms that can evaluate algorithms for fairness and explainability. The UK’s Information Commissioner's Office is developing an AI auditing framework to ensure compliance with data protection and ethical standards, while PwC incorporates ethical audits in its responsible AI framework.

However, the paper notes limitations of external audits, such as their focus on specific system components rather than overall functionality, the inability to uncover all harmful inputs, and the timing of audits, which often occur post-deployment. Legal barriers concerning consumer data and trade secrets further complicate auditing efforts. Additionally, the dynamic nature of AI development and lack of documentation present challenges to effective audits.

The authors suggest that internal audits, conducted by dedicated teams with access to data and an emphasis on ethical foresight, could be a more practical solution. They argue that this approach may better prevent future harms, pointing to the need for a clearer understanding of what constitutes an audit in this context.
