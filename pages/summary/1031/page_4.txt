The paper discusses the proliferation of AI ethical guidance documents produced by various stakeholders, highlighting the existence of over 160 such documents as noted by the Global Inventory of AI Ethics Guidelines. While these documents can be summarized under key principles like beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability, they encompass a wide array of concepts including transparency, fairness, and responsibility. The authors point out that the ambiguity in these principles often leads to confusion and ineffective responses to ethical harms, as many ethical issues are poorly defined.

The paper argues that ethical principles, though sometimes criticized for being too flexible, should be viewed as foundational rather than prescriptive, akin to a Constitution that provides a framework without detailing specific regulations. To address the challenge of translating abstract ethical principles into actionable guidelines, the authors advocate for lowering the level of abstraction in AI ethics by developing tools and methods that connect ethical concepts to technical specifications. They acknowledge that while this approach, explored in their previous research and in practice by Digital Catapult, can be beneficial, it remains a partial solution. The authors caution that such translational tools may still leave unresolved issues and can be exploited by unethical actors.
