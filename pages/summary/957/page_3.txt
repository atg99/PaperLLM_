The paper discusses the spectral bias of neural networks, which refers to the tendency of these networks to learn lower frequencies first during training. This phenomenon is evidenced through experiments that demonstrate how the Fourier spectrum of a neural network evolves as training progresses.

In the first experiment, a 6-layer deep ReLU network is trained to regress a function composed of various frequencies, showing that lower frequencies are prioritized in learning, regardless of their amplitudes. The second experiment complements this finding by illustrating that lower frequencies are more robust to parameter perturbations, indicating that higher frequencies require finer tuning of parameters to express them effectively.

The authors provide a theoretical framework to explain these observations, suggesting that the learning of higher frequencies is delayed due to constraints on the Lipschitz constant of the function, which is linked to the parameter norm. This results in a slower rate of change for residuals associated with higher frequencies. 

To further investigate the implications of spectral bias in real-world scenarios, the authors conduct an experiment on the MNIST dataset, which indirectly measures the impact of frequency noise on validation performance.

Overall, the findings highlight key aspects of how neural networks process frequency information, revealing a structured approach to understanding the learning dynamics in deep learning models.
