The paper explores the spectral bias of neural networks, particularly how the shape of the data manifold influences the learnability of high-frequency functions. It establishes a connection between the manifold hypothesis, where data lies on a lower-dimensional manifold, and the behavior of neural networks in learning functions defined on this manifold.

The authors describe a scenario where a target function defined on the manifold can be represented as a function on a lower-dimensional latent space. They derive a mean square error (MSE) expression that highlights the existence of numerous degenerate solutions in the function space that minimize the MSE. The findings suggest that neural networks inherently favor low-frequency solutions, which indicates a bias in their learning process.

In their experiments, the authors control the shape of the data manifold and the target function, using flower-shaped curves and a specific mapping to generate the data. They present results showing that while two identical neural networks can achieve close to perfect classification accuracy on the manifold, they can produce significantly different outputs outside of it. This illustrates the networks' bias towards low-frequency functions and the impact of the manifold's shape on learning.

Overall, the paper provides a theoretical analysis supported by empirical evidence, demonstrating the spectral bias inherent in neural networks and its implications for learning complex functions defined on intricate manifolds.
