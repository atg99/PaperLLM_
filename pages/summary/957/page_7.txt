The paper discusses the spectral bias of neural networks, particularly focusing on how the geometry of the data manifold influences the learning process. The authors present experiments that illustrate how different manifold shapes, indicated by the parameter L, affect the network's ability to learn higher frequency components of target functions. Specifically, they show that networks trained on manifolds with larger L values can learn higher frequencies more easily, as evidenced by the evolution of the network spectrum during training and the corresponding learning curves.

In Experiment 6, the study adapts a previous setup to binary classification by thresholding the target function. The results, represented in a heatmap, demonstrate that as L increases, the classification performance improves for the same target signal. This pattern aligns with findings from prior experiments, suggesting a consistent relationship between the manifold shape and learning difficulty.

The authors argue that the effective difficulty of fitting a signal is influenced by the relationship between the frequency spectrum of the network and that of the target function, mediated by the manifold's embedding map. They propose that a signal defined on a manifold is easier to fit when the manifold's coordinate functions possess higher frequency components. This relationship is further analyzed mathematically through the Fourier transform of the composite function, elucidating how the geometry of the data manifold impacts the learning process.

Overall, the findings indicate a complex interplay between manifold shape and learning efficiency, highlighting the significance of spectral bias in neural network training.
