This paper discusses various neural network architectures used for language modeling, specifically focusing on Forward Feedback Neural Networks (FFNN), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), and Pre-Trained Models. The authors highlight the limitations of traditional statistical language models, such as maximum likelihood estimation, which struggles with long-term dependencies and sparse data. In contrast, neural language models leverage distributed representations to effectively model natural language sequences, allowing for better recognition of similar words.

The FFNN architecture computes the probability of the next word based on the preceding words, utilizing a projection matrix to map words into a continuous feature space. The output is generated through a combination of weight matrices and bias values, but this structure restricts the model's capability to handle longer input sequences.

To address these limitations, RNNs are introduced, which can process variable-length inputs and maintain internal states to reduce computational redundancy. This makes RNNs advantageous over FFNNs due to their efficiency and flexibility in handling sequential data.

The paper also explores pre-trained language models, which are initialized with parameters obtained from pretraining on various tasks. Examples include fixed embedding models like Word2Vec and GloVe, as well as variable embedding models like ELMo, GPT, and BERT. The GPT model is highlighted for its two-stage training process, which includes text prediction using a Transformer decoder and subsequent fine-tuning for specific tasks.

Overall, the paper emphasizes the evolution of language modeling techniques from traditional statistical methods to more advanced neural network architectures that better capture the complexities of natural language.
