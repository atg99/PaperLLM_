In this paper, the architectural design of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) is discussed, focusing on how neurons in a layer connect to a subset of neurons in the previous layer due to the impracticality of full connectivity with high-dimensional inputs like images. The core components of CNNs—convolution, pooling, and Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)—function together to transform input volumes into output volumes effectively.

Key hyperparameters such as filter size, depth, stride, and zero-padding play crucial roles in determining the output volume's size and feature extraction. The convolution operation is mathematically represented for both textual and visual inputs, highlighting the importance of filter size in feature extraction.

Pooling layers, which follow convolutional layers, downsample the output and help extract dominant features, with various types of pooling (e.g., max-pooling, average-pooling) selected based on application needs. For instance, max-pooling emphasizes features like negation in textual or visual data.

The paper also describes the use of ReLU as a non-linear activation function, which aids in introducing non-linearity to the model. A typical CNN architecture consists of multiple convolutional layers, optional pooling layers, followed by fully connected layers and a SoftMax layer for probability estimation. The representation of these fundamental layers illustrates the stacking process used to capture important features within acceptable spatial limits.
