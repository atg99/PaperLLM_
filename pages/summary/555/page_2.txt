The paper presents a novel approach to object detection using a framework based on FCOS, which is an anchor-free, one-stage detection method. The authors introduce a fast and memory-efficient Neural Architecture Search (NAS) method that effectively identifies optimal architectures for both the feature pyramid network (FPN) and prediction head. Key contributions include:

1. Development of a NAS method that efficiently searches over 3,000 architectures within 28 GPU-days by employing proxy tasks, a well-defined search space, and improved evaluation strategies.
2. Introduction of a fast training scheme that omits the backbone fine-tuning stage, a progressive search strategy to minimize time costs, and a more discriminative evaluation criterion.
3. Exploration of the workload relationship between FPN and head architectures, emphasizing the significance of weight sharing in the head component.
4. Demonstration of the generality and flexibility of the NAS-FCOS structure, which can be integrated with various backbones such as MobileNetV2, ResNet-50, ResNet-101, and ResNeXt-101, achieving substantial improvements in average precision (AP) ranging from 1.5 to 3.5 points compared to their FCOS counterparts.

The paper also discusses the landscape of object detection frameworks, distinguishing between one-stage and two-stage detectors. It notes the computational efficiency and simplicity of one-stage methods, particularly anchor-free detectors, which mitigate the challenges posed by anchor boxes used in traditional models.

In the realm of Neural Architecture Search, the authors highlight advancements from previous methods, noting the challenges of time consumption and memory allocation in searching complex structures. They position their work within the context of existing research, clarifying that their focus differs from previous approaches by targeting both FPN and prediction head designs rather than solely backbone architectures or FPN alternatives.

Overall, the proposed NAS-FCOS framework achieves state-of-the-art results while maintaining a comparable computational footprint, making it a significant contribution to the field of object detection.
