This paper discusses advancements in generative modeling, particularly focusing on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and likelihood-based methods. GANs operate through a minimax objective, utilizing a generator that creates images from random noise and a discriminator that evaluates these images as real or fake. While GANs have achieved high-quality image generation, they struggle with diversity and evaluation, lacking reliable measures for generalization and overfitting. Common metrics like Inception Score (IS) and Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) have been employed for model comparison.

In contrast, likelihood-based approaches optimize negative log-likelihood (NLL), allowing for better model comparison and generalization to unseen data. These models can capture the full data distribution and avoid issues like mode collapse, unlike GANs. However, maximizing likelihood directly in pixel space presents challenges, as NLL does not always correlate with sample quality and lacks focus on global structure. Inductive biases, such as multi-scale approaches, can help mitigate some of these challenges.

The paper introduces a novel method inspired by lossy compression techniques, leveraging concepts from JPEG to minimize the generative model's burden of modeling unnecessary information. By employing vector quantization of intermediate representations from an autoencoder, images can be compressed into a discrete latent space, significantly reducing data size while maintaining quality. This latent space can be modeled using PixelCNN with self-attention, allowing for high-quality image generation that is also computationally efficient—30 times faster than pixel-based approaches. The proposed method retains the simplicity of the original Vector Quantized Variational AutoEncoder (VQ-VAE) framework, making it suitable for applications requiring quick and efficient encoding and decoding of large images.

The VQ-VAE model functions analogously to a communication system, where an encoder maps observations to discrete latent variables and a decoder reconstructs the observations. The encoder quantizes its output based on proximity to a codebook of prototype vectors, facilitating data transmission to the decoder. This process incorporates losses that align the codebook with the encoder's output, ensuring the model learns effective mappings for both encoding and decoding.
