The referenced papers cover various aspects of protein structure prediction and classification, highlighting advancements and methodologies in the field. 

Ma et al. (2015) discuss a novel approach for protein contact prediction that combines evolutionary coupling analysis with supervised learning techniques. This integration aims to improve the accuracy of predicting residue-residue contacts based on evolutionary data.

Moult et al. (2018) provide a critical assessment of protein structure prediction methods through the twelfth round of the Critical Assessment of Techniques for Protein Structure Prediction (CASP), emphasizing the ongoing challenges and progress in the field.

Yang et al. (2016) reflect on sixty-five years of developments in protein secondary structure prediction, suggesting that the field is nearing a critical juncture with the potential for significant advancements.

Stevens (2003) examines the economic implications of three-dimensional protein structures, discussing their value in drug discovery and the associated costs.

Fox et al. (2013) present the SCOPE database, which integrates and extends the Structural Classification of Proteins (SCOP) and ASTRAL data, facilitating the classification of new protein structures.

Berman et al. (2000) provide an overview of the Protein Data Bank (PDB), a crucial resource for researchers needing access to structural data.

Castelle and Banfield (2018) highlight the discovery of new microbial groups that expand the diversity of life, reshaping our understanding of the tree of life.

Seemayer et al. (2014) introduce CCMpred, a tool for fast and accurate prediction of protein residue contacts based on correlated mutations, enhancing computational efficiency in structural biology.

Chen et al. (2016) contribute to the discussion of deep learning by presenting techniques for training deep networks with reduced memory requirements, indicating advancements in computational methods applicable to bioinformatics.

Joosten et al. (2010) describe various databases related to the PDB that cater to everyday research needs, underscoring the importance of accessible structural data in the scientific community. 

Overall, these references collectively underscore the dynamic nature of protein structure research, the integration of computational techniques, and the importance of databases in advancing our understanding of protein functions and interactions.
