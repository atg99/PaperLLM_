The study aimed to analyze the relationship between clinical and laboratory data, lung involvement observed on baseline chest CT, and patient prognosis in individuals diagnosed with COVID-19 pneumonia. It included 103 patients, with an average age of 68.8 years, who were evaluated using RT-PCR tests. All participants underwent chest CT scans upon admission, and these were assessed using both a structured reporting system and an artificial intelligence tool for quantitative evaluation of lung involvement.

Key findings indicated that certain laboratory markers such as SpO2, HS CRP, leukocyte count, and others were significantly correlated with patient outcomes. Specifically, lower SpO2 levels were associated with critical conditions or mortality, while higher values of various biomarkers were linked to poorer outcomes. The majority of patients exhibited ground-glass opacities (GGO) and consolidations on CT, with a notable prevalence of bilateral and peripheral lung involvement.

Quantitative assessments revealed that volumes of consolidation, emphysema, and residual healthy lung parenchyma differed significantly among patients based on their outcomes. In contrast, GGO volume did not appear to affect prognosis. The overall radiological severity score, particularly a score of 8 or higher, effectively predicted patient outcomes. The most accurate predictive model combined clinical and laboratory findings with CT data, achieving a sensitivity of 88%, specificity of 78%, and accuracy of 81% in distinguishing between patients who were discharged or stable and those who were critical or deceased.

In conclusion, both visual scoring of chest CT and automated quantification of lung involvement are valuable independent predictors of outcomes for patients with COVID-19 pneumonia.
