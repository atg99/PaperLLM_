The paper discusses the evolution of learning theory through the lens of statistical physics, particularly focusing on the insights gained from Gardner's method, which allows for the explicit calculation of learning curves in neural networks. This method revealed that learning dynamics can show complex behaviors, including phase transitions in generalization capabilities, which contrasts with the simpler predictions of traditional PAC bounds.

The resurgence of deep learning since 2010, characterized by the application of larger-scale feed-forward neural networks, has led to remarkable successes in various machine learning tasks. However, existing theoretical frameworks struggle to explain why these deep networks, despite having more parameters than training samples, generalize well. This gap in understanding has been highlighted by recent studies showing that state-of-the-art networks can perfectly classify random labels, challenging conventional learning theory.

The paper also points out the challenges in identifying computationally tractable learning problems, especially given that many are NP-hard. The selection of hyper-parameters and architectures remains largely empirical, underscoring the need for more systematic, theory-driven approaches in deep learning. Fundamental questions about sample size requirements for effective learning are still unanswered.

Moreover, the authors draw parallels between the current state of deep learning research and pre-quantum physics, where unexplained phenomena prompted the development of new theoretical frameworks. They suggest revisiting statistical physics concepts to address contemporary challenges in machine learning.

The review emphasizes contributions from statistical physics to unsupervised learning methods, highlighting techniques such as low-rank matrix decomposition, which are foundational in clustering and dimensionality reduction. The focus is on understanding how these methods can extract significant structures from high-dimensional datasets, laying the groundwork for further advancements in machine learning theory.
