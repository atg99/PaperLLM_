This paper discusses the architecture space of neural networks, emphasizing the structure and types of layers involved. Each layer in a neural network is represented as a node in a graph, with edges indicating the flow of data from one layer to the next. The search space for constructing neural networks is defined by three key parameters: the maximum number of layers, the types of operations each layer can perform (e.g., convolution, pooling), and the associated hyperparameters (e.g., number of filters, kernel size). 

The authors highlight that the design of the search space is conditional, meaning that hyperparameters depend on the layer operations chosen. Recent advancements in Neural Architecture Search (NAS) incorporate sophisticated design elements like skip connections, enabling the creation of complex multi-branch networks. These architectures provide greater flexibility, allowing different combinations of layer outputs. 

The paper further categorizes multi-branch architectures into several special cases, including chain-structured networks, Residual Networks, and DenseNets, each defined by specific functions that combine outputs from previous layers. The authors suggest that searching for repeated motifs or blocks within these architectures, as seen in hand-crafted designs, can enhance the efficiency of the search process in NAS.
