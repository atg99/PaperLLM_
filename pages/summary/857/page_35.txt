Federated Learning (FL) employs frameworks like Stackelberg-based game theory for motivation mechanism design. Sarikaya and Ercetin (2019) propose an inventive mechanism to encourage workers to allocate more CPU resources for local training. Khan et al. (2019) focus on adaptive local iteration times using Stackelberg incentives for optimal effectiveness. Additionally, Pandey et al. (2019) utilize a two-stage Stackelberg model in a crowdsourcing framework to maximize utility for both clients and servers. Future research suggests incorporating frameworks such as matching theory and auction theory to balance participant numbers with update latency.

A significant challenge in FL is the reliance on the assumption that clients are semi-honest, which may not hold true in practice. Clients might intentionally or unintentionally send faulty models, leading to deviations in the global model, particularly in sensitive applications like wearable medical systems where adversaries can produce misleading data (Cai and Venkatasubramanian, 2018). This highlights the need for Byzantine fault-tolerant systems that maintain collaborative training despite client non-compliance or malicious behavior.

To identify anomalous model updates, Li et al. (2019) suggest using an autoencoder to replace model parameters with a low-dimensional vector, facilitating the detection of irregular weight updates. Muñoz-González and Lupu (2019) explore the use of a Hidden Markov Model in adaptive FL to assess model quality and filter out abnormal updates. Traditional Byzantine fault tolerance typically relies on defense mechanisms rather than direct detection of malicious clients, which raises concerns about potential accuracy loss.
