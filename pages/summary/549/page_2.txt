This paper presents an automated neural architecture search approach tailored for designing mobile convolutional neural networks (CNNs). The key innovations include a latency-aware multi-objective reward system and a novel search space. The authors identify two main issues with previous methods: reliance on FLOPS as an approximation for inference latency, which can be inaccurate, and a limited diversity in layer types due to the common practice of stacking identical cells.

To address these challenges, the authors propose framing the design problem as a multi-objective optimization that incorporates both accuracy and real-world inference latency, measured directly on mobile devices. They introduce a factorized hierarchical search space that promotes architectural diversity while maintaining a manageable search space size.

The approach is applied to tasks such as ImageNet classification and COCO object detection. Results show that their MnasNet models outperform existing models like MobileNetV2, achieving a 3.0% improvement in accuracy with similar latency, and are significantly faster when accuracy is constrained. Comparisons with ResNet-50 reveal that MnasNet achieves higher accuracy with significantly fewer parameters and operations.

The paper contributes to the field by proposing a comprehensive multi-objective architecture search method, introducing a new search space design that enhances layer diversity, and demonstrating state-of-the-art performance under mobile latency constraints. 

The related work section discusses various strategies for improving CNN efficiency, including quantization, pruning, and handcrafted architectures. It highlights the limitations of existing methods, particularly their lack of focus on creating novel CNN compositions and the absence of mobile platform considerations in architecture search processes. The authors emphasize their unique contribution in optimizing for real-world latency while targeting larger-scale tasks, differentiating their work from prior studies that primarily addressed smaller datasets.
