The motivation for understanding the generative processes of data lies in their ability to reveal causal relationships, which tend to generalize better to new situations compared to mere correlations. For example, knowledge about the generative process of earthquakes can be applied across different regions. To utilize a generative model as a discriminator, Bayes' rule is employed to compare models and compute probabilities for specific events, though this can be computationally intensive.

Discriminative methods, in contrast, focus on learning a direct mapping from data inputs to outputs, which can be more efficient when large amounts of data are available. While generative models often make stronger assumptions and can lead to higher bias when incorrect, discriminative models typically result in fewer errors in classification tasks. However, generative models can still be valuable, particularly in semi-supervised learning scenarios where there are more unlabeled than labeled examples. In such cases, generative modeling can enhance classification performance by guiding the training of discriminators. Overall, generative modeling serves as a useful auxiliary task in understanding and predicting data.
