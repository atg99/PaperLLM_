The paper provides a comprehensive overview of pruning criteria in static-mask and dynamic-mask sparse training methods for neural networks. It categorizes various methods based on their pruning and growing criteria, highlighting key contributions and developments over the years. 

In static-mask methods, several pruning criteria are outlined, including Skeletonization, OBD, Taylor-FO, SNIP, GraSP, and SynFlow, with a focus on the loss function and Hessian. Notably, SynFlow introduces a data-independent criterion that accounts for inter-layer interactions, while SupSup facilitates continual learning with supermasks. The dual LTH (DLTH) method shifts the focus from finding masks to determining initial weights with the use of growing L2 regularization.

Dynamic-mask methods allow for adaptive changes to masks during training. Techniques such as DeepR, SET, DSR, SNFS, and RigL are discussed, emphasizing the use of magnitudes and gradients for pruning and growing criteria. The ability for zeroed weights to re-enter training is a significant aspect of these methods, which rely on efficient criteria due to the frequent re-evaluation of masks.

The paper also discusses the optimization of subnet values post-selection, contrasting traditional optimization with the innovative approach of optimizing network topology. The notion of supermasks is introduced, with pivotal works demonstrating that selected subnets can achieve competitive accuracy without further training. The strong LTH concept posits that a sufficiently over-parameterized network can contain sub-networks that perform well from random initialization.

Theoretical advancements are highlighted, with foundational work establishing that pruning a randomly initialized network can be as effective as optimizing weights. Later contributions refine these theories, suggesting less restrictive conditions for achieving competitive accuracy in over-parameterized networks.

In summary, the paper encapsulates the evolution of pruning techniques in sparse training, emphasizing both technical innovations and theoretical insights that enhance our understanding of network efficiency and performance.
