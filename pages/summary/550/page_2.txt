The paper emphasizes the critical importance of reproducibility in Neural Architecture Search (NAS) research due to the empirical nature of the field, the complexity of NAS methods, and the high computational costs involved. It distinguishes between two types of reproducibility: "exact" reproducibility, which assesses whether reported experimental results can be reproduced, and "broad" reproducibility, which evaluates the robustness and generalizability of these results. The authors note that most published NAS results lack even exact reproducibility, citing that none of the 12 recent papers introducing novel NAS methods at major conferences are exactly reproducible.

To address these challenges, the authors present several contributions:

1. They provide a comparative analysis between traditional hyperparameter optimization and leading NAS methods. By employing a simple hyperparameter optimization technique that combines random search with early stopping, they achieve competitive results on standard NAS benchmarks (CIFAR-10 and PTB) using similar computational resources as state-of-the-art methods like DARTS. Their findings indicate that the performance gap is not as substantial as previously reported.

2. The authors identify key components of NAS that can yield good empirical results and construct a straightforward algorithm based on tuned random search with weight-sharing. They explore the impact of several meta-hyperparameters and demonstrate competitive performance against more complex methods without additional tuning of final architectures.

3. They commit to open-sourcing the code, random seeds, and documentation necessary for reproducing their experiments, ensuring exact reproducibility through a deterministic setup. They also investigate the broad reproducibility of their method, revealing variance across independent runs and emphasizing the need for improved reporting and transparency in NAS research.

The paper ultimately advocates for a more rigorous approach to experimental reporting and performance quantification in NAS, highlighting the necessity for community-wide efforts to enhance reproducibility in the field.
