The paper discusses the limitations of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) visualizations and interpretability techniques, particularly in the context of adversarial attacks and their effects on neural network predictions. It highlights how small perturbations to input data can significantly alter feature importance maps while maintaining high prediction accuracy, revealing flaws in gradient-based techniques.

The paper contrasts human evaluators' interpretations of visual outputs with those generated by methods like Grad-CAM, LIME, and SHAP. It notes that LIME and SHAP focus on different aspects of the input, with LIME emphasizing objects like chairs and SHAP focusing on the beach, yet both struggle to align with human reasoning in decision-making scenarios.

Key limitations identified include: 
1. Human inability to effectively utilize XAI explanation maps for decision-making.
2. The lack of quantitative measures for the completeness and correctness of these maps.

The authors advocate for a reevaluation of XAI visualization techniques, especially for critical applications, and suggest improvements in how explanations are represented. They reference studies indicating that providing explanations, such as those from SHAP, did not enhance decision-making, emphasizing the need for a deeper understanding of explanation modes.

Further research cited demonstrates that small input changes can result in significant shifts in output interpretations from popular XAI methods, and that biases in model parameters can skew attributions. The paper also discusses the manipulability of explanations and the dependency of gradient-based methods on model architecture and data generation processes. It suggests randomization tests to assess the reliability of these methods, calling for a more robust approach to generating and understanding explanations in XAI.
