Graphs consist of nodes and edges, with WordNet serving as a prominent example where each node represents a word, such as "tiger," connected through synset relationships that group words by semantic similarity. This structure facilitates synonym replacement and illustrates how nodes relate through synsets. Beyond WordNet, Knowledge Graphs expand this concept with more detailed edge classifications, as seen in CoV-KGE, which categorizes biomedical concepts through various edge types. Huang et al. showcase how knowledge graphs can aid in maintaining semantic consistency in summarization tasks.

Syntactic parsing is another area of interest that involves analyzing text structure, though recent findings by Glavas and Vulic indicate limited benefits from supervised parsing in the context of large language models. The integration of metadata, particularly in scientific literature, enhances natural language processing applications by utilizing citation networks alongside textual information, as demonstrated by Li et al. in fields like genomics and healthcare.

The exploration of data augmentation for graph-structured data is nascent, with techniques like edge augmentation proposed by Zhao et al. showing promise in improving accuracy in node classification tasks. Kong et al. further validate the benefits of adversarial node feature augmentation. Future sections will discuss practical implementation strategies, including consistency regularization and contrastive learning, which leverage graph structures for enhanced training outcomes. Neural Structured Learning exemplifies this approach by connecting instances with similar labels to refine classification accuracy.
