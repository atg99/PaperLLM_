In this section, the authors categorize workers' revisions into specific types, such as modifying actions, substituting entities, and inserting relationships, to test generalization through counterfactual examples. They raise the question of whether generative models, like GPT-3, can perform these edits effectively. The authors emphasize the significance of their dataset, which cost $10,778.14 to construct, suggesting that inference costs for generative models are generally lower unless dealing with very large models. They propose that a categorization similar to that of Kaushik et al. could enhance understanding of the linguistic aspects of generalization tests.

The discussion also introduces Generative Data Augmentation as a new perspective on studying generalization. Nakkiran et al. introduce the "Deep Bootstrap Framework," comparing Online test error, which reflects performance on an infinite data stream, to Bootstrap test error, common in traditional training setups. They simulate Online learning using a generative model, generating 6 million examples as opposed to the typical 50,000 for CIFAR-10. Garg et al. propose RATT, analyzing generalization when unlabeled data is added to training batches, suggesting that the augmentations discussed could simulate this unlabeled data.

The authors conclude by questioning the implications of overfitting and the extent to which modern neural networks can cover data distributions. They suggest that Data Augmentation could help expand training distributions and mitigate out-of-distribution shifts in test sets. Additionally, they draw parallels between Text Data Augmentation and Image Data Augmentation, noting similarities in implementation and applicability across various text and image-related tasks.
