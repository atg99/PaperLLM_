This section discusses the merits and demerits of feature selection (FS) methods, specifically Filter, Wrapper, and Embedded methods, as summarized in Table 3. 

Filter methods are praised for their efficiency and speed, being independent of any learning algorithms, making them suitable for low-dimensional data. However, they do not account for correlations among classifiers or features, which can lead to inadequate pattern recognition during the learning phase.

Wrapper methods, while more accurate and capable of considering feature dependencies and correlations with class labels, are criticized for their computational complexity and potential for overfitting. They require iterative evaluations of selected feature subsets, risking the exclusion of potentially valuable features from the outset.

Embedded methods strike a balance by being computationally efficient compared to Wrapper methods and generally more accurate than both Filter and Wrapper methods. Nonetheless, they are computationally more demanding than Filter methods and struggle with high-dimensional data, exhibiting poor generalizability.

The findings reveal that many Filter methods rely on statistical measures for feature selection. An example is the Relief method by Kira and Rendell, which, despite its distance measure approach, does not effectively eliminate redundant or incomplete features. This highlights ongoing challenges in the field of feature selection.
