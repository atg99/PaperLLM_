The mutual information (MI) method is a statistical approach used in feature selection (FS) to measure the dependency between two variables. It quantifies the amount of information one variable provides about another, calculated using joint and marginal probability distributions. For discrete variables, the MI is computed using a summation formula, while for continuous variables, a double integral is employed.

In the filter method of feature selection, features are ranked based on their statistical scores derived from measures like information gain and correlation. Although this method is computationally efficient, it may lead to the selection of redundant features as it does not account for correlations among independent variables.

The Relief algorithm, proposed by Kira and Rendell in 1992, utilizes instance-based learning to evaluate feature relevance through the identification of Near-hits and Near-misses, but it is limited to binary classification tasks. Subsequent extensions, such as Relief-A, Relief-B, and Relief-F, were developed to address issues related to incomplete data and multiclass problems, yet they still struggle with eliminating redundant features.

To enhance feature selection, Batiti introduced a mutual information-based approach in 1994, which assesses both the relevance of features to the target class and their interdependencies, effectively filtering out redundancy. Yang and Moody's Joint Mutual Information (JMI) method further refines this process for non-linear data classification and visualization, offering a more nuanced understanding of feature interactions.
