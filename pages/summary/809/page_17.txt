The paper discusses various pruning techniques used in neural networks to enhance efficiency while maintaining performance. Key methods include:

1. **Iterative Pruning**: This method involves repeated cycles of scoring, pruning, and retraining the network.
2. **Local Pruning**: This technique prunes networks by removing a percentage of weights or filters from subsets, like layers.
3. **Global Pruning**: Unlike local pruning, global pruning targets structures across the entire network until a desired pruning ratio is achieved.
4. **Dynamic Pruning**: This approach generates different subnetworks based on specific input samples.
5. **Static Pruning**: A fixed pruned model is utilized across various inputs, contrasting with dynamic pruning.
6. **Lottery Ticket Hypothesis**: This hypothesis posits that a dense network contains a sparse trainable subnetwork that can perform comparably to the original.
7. **Winning Tickets**: These are subnetworks that, after training, can match the performance of the original network.
8. **Layer Collapse**: A situation where the removal of all weights in a layer renders the network untrainable.
9. **Weight Rewinding**: This technique involves reverting the weights of a subnetwork to earlier training values.
10. **Learning Rate Rewinding**: Training remaining weights using a specified learning rate schedule after pruning.
11. **FLOPs and MACs**: Metrics used to evaluate the computational efficiency of pruned models.
12. **Fine-tuning and Training from Scratch**: Fine-tuning adjusts preserved weights post-pruning, while training from scratch resets weights to initial values.

The experimental settings involve evaluating pruning methods on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet datasets, using various architectures like VGG-16 and ResNet. The experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A100 GPUs, employing specific training protocols including learning rate adjustments and batch sizes.

In addition, the paper assesses pruning techniques on language models like LLaMA-7B, evaluating their performance on tasks such as zero-shot language generation and classification across several datasets. The relevant codebases for the discussed methods are provided for further exploration.
