The paper discusses the evolution of human-robot interaction (HRI) frameworks, emphasizing the need for a comprehensive taxonomy to facilitate knowledge accumulation and comparison of findings across different studies. It highlights several existing frameworks, starting with Granda et al.'s 1990 model that categorizes robot autonomy into five stages, from bounded autonomy to virtual symbiosis. While this model offers a valuable overview of autonomy levels, its overlapping stages complicate clear differentiation.

Scholtz's framework, introduced twelve years later, focuses on human roles in HRI, detailing various positions such as supervisor, operator, mechanic, team partner, and bystander. Despite its strengths, it lacks specificity regarding the interaction dynamics and the predominance of certain roles in specific contexts.

Further, Schmidtler et al. propose a classification based on interaction types, examining proximity and dependency, which includes coexistence, cooperation, and collaboration. However, this framework is primarily industrial-focused and may not adequately capture the nuances of HRI in other domains.

Lastly, Yanco and Drury expand upon Scholtz's roles by introducing ten categories related to interaction and robot characteristics. While some categories are well-defined, others lack detail, limiting their practical applicability. Overall, the paper underscores the necessity for a well-structured taxonomy to enhance the understanding and development of HRI across various applications.
