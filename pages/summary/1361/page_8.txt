The paper discusses the categorization of robots in human-robot interaction (HRI), particularly within educational and therapeutic contexts. It identifies various roles of robots, such as facilitating verbal and nonverbal communication, emotional stimulation, and physical rehabilitation. The authors emphasize the importance of robot morphology—how robots look and behave—in shaping user expectations and interaction styles. They propose a nuanced taxonomy that classifies robots into anthropomorphic, zoomorphic, and technical categories based on four dimensions: appearance, communication, movement, and context. This classification moves beyond mere visual representation, acknowledging that other factors can influence user perceptions.

The authors highlight limitations in existing questionnaires for assessing robot morphology, criticizing their focus on the uncanny valley effect and the lack of measures for animal-like characteristics. They call for the development of new tools to reliably evaluate the degrees of technical, zoomorphic, and anthropomorphic features in robots.

Additionally, the paper addresses robot autonomy, proposing a classification that includes stages of information acquisition, analysis, action selection, and implementation. This framework allows for a clearer understanding of the levels of human intervention required during interactions with robots, emphasizing the potential for varying degrees of autonomy in HRI. The authors advocate for a comprehensive approach to studying and designing robots that considers these diverse classifications to enhance user experience and interaction outcomes.
