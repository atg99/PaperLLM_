Anthropomorphism is defined as the attribution of human-like qualities to nonhuman agents, which can influence social interactions and acceptance of robots.

Several studies are analyzed to illustrate the differing impacts of anthropomorphism on empathy and decision-making regarding robots. For instance, Darling et al. found that anthropomorphic framing increases empathy towards robots, while Nijssen et al. revealed that human-like robots are less likely to be sacrificed in moral dilemmas compared to machine-like ones. However, both studies utilized setups that lacked genuine interaction, raising questions about their ecological validity.

Further research by Onnasch and Roesler indicated that anthropomorphism may negatively affect the willingness to 'save' a robot unless its functional importance was emphasized. Additionally, conflicting results were observed regarding the influence of robot movement on action identification, with Kuz et al. supporting anthropomorphic movements while Riek et al. favored machine-like trajectories.

The paper underscores the challenges in drawing comprehensive conclusions from these studies due to varying definitions, contexts, and types of robots used in experiments. It calls for a more structured approach to research in HRI, advocating for clearer operational definitions and methodologies to facilitate comparability and replicate findings. The goal is to build a robust scientific foundation that can advance knowledge in the field of HRI.
