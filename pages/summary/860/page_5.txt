The paper discusses the architecture of self-attention networks (SAN) and their operational characteristics, particularly focusing on transition layers and the footprint of self-attention. Transition layers reduce spatial resolution to decrease computational load while expanding the receptive field. Each transition includes batch normalization, ReLU activation, max pooling, and a linear mapping to expand channel dimensionality.

The local footprint of self-attention, denoted as R(i), determines the context gathered from preceding layers. A footprint size of 7x7 is used for the last four stages, while a size of 3x3 is applied in the first stage to manage high resolution and memory consumption. The paper notes that changing the footprint size does not affect the number of parameters in pairwise self-attention.

The number of self-attention blocks can be altered to create networks with varying capacities, mirroring the number of residual blocks in ResNet architectures. The paper compares self-attention operators to other methods such as convolution and scalar attention, highlighting that convolution does not adapt to image content, while scalar attention provides scalar weights that lack channel variability. In contrast, the proposed vector attention allows for adaptive weights across both spatial dimensions and channels, offering significant flexibility and computational efficiency.

Experiments conducted on the ImageNet dataset involved training models from scratch and utilizing a cosine learning rate schedule. The dataset consists of 1.28 million training images and 50,000 validation images across 1,000 classes. Accuracy comparisons between self-attention networks and convolutional networks like ResNet are detailed, ensuring that validation sets are distinct from those used for architectural decisions. The implementation specifics are designed to maintain rigor in training and evaluation.
