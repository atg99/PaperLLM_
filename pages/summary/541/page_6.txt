The paper discusses the relationship between encoding space, search space, and initial space in the context of evolutionary neural architecture search (ENAS) methods. It highlights how encoding strategies impact the discovery of neural architectures. The encoding space serves as the foundation for both the search space and the initial space, with the initial space often being a subset of the encoding space. 

Three types of architecture initialization approaches are identified: trivial, random, and well-designed spaces. The trivial space consists of basic layers, demonstrating that minimal initial conditions can lead to competitive architectures. In contrast, the well-designed space includes advanced architectures that may restrict evolutionary potential. The random space involves generating individuals randomly, minimizing human intervention.

After initialization, ENAS methods explore architectures within the search space, which can expand beyond the initial space, especially when the latter is limited. The paper emphasizes that many methods do not explicitly define the search space but instead use evolutionary operators to guide the search.

Regarding encoding strategies, they can be categorized into fixed-length and variable-length approaches. Fixed-length encoding simplifies the implementation of evolutionary operations but requires a predetermined maximum length, which may depend on expert knowledge of optimal architecture depth. Variable-length encoding, by contrast, allows for more flexibility and automation in discovering optimal depths without prior expertise, although it may complicate the use of traditional evolutionary operators. 

The paper concludes that both encoding strategies have their advantages and challenges, influencing the effectiveness of ENAS methods in discovering novel neural architectures.
