The paper discusses various encoding spaces used in neural architecture search (NAS) and their associated constraints. It categorizes these spaces into four types: layer-based, block-based, cell-based, and topology-based. 

The layer-based encoding space, while comprehensive, results in a vast search space that complicates the identification of optimal deep neural networks (DNNs) due to the extensive number of primitive layers involved. This complexity can hinder the discovery of effective architectures, as exemplified by the limitations of primitive layers in capturing advanced structures like skip connections in ResNet.

To address these challenges, the block-based encoding space consolidates different types of layers into blocks, which are designed with specific topological relationships. This approach simplifies the search process and typically results in better performance with fewer parameters compared to the layer-based space.

The cell-based encoding space is a more constrained variant of the block-based approach, focusing on repeating identical blocks (cells). It separates the architecture into micro (parameters of cells) and macro (connections between cells) components, streamlining the search for effective architectures. However, some researchers argue that the theoretical basis for the efficacy of the cell-based space is lacking.

In contrast, the topology-based space emphasizes the interconnections between units rather than their parameters or structures. Techniques like one-shot methods and pruning exemplify this approach, prioritizing the selection of connections to form the architecture.

The paper also identifies three primary constraints affecting the encoding space: fixed depth, rich initialization, and partial fixed structure. Fixed depth limits all individuals in the population to the same depth, significantly reducing the search space. Rich initialization involves manually designing initial architectures, which can contradict the goals of NAS. Partial fixed structure implies that certain aspects of the architecture are predetermined.

Lastly, the paper notes that some methods may have relatively few constraints, but this does not mean they are entirely unrestricted, as specific design choices still guide their architecture development.
