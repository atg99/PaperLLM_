In multi-objective optimization, the presence of competing objectives complicates the identification of a single optimal solution. Each objective has its own fitness landscape, and improving one objective may lead to the deterioration of another. This necessitates the use of dominance to evaluate solutions. A solution is considered to dominate another if it performs better in at least one objective without being worse in others. When two solutions dominate each other in different objectives, neither can be deemed superior without subjective judgment regarding the importance of each objective. 

This scenario leads to the formation of the Pareto front, which consists of non-dominated solutions that represent the best trade-offs among competing objectives. The paper illustrates these concepts through hypothetical examples involving two decision variables and two objective functions, demonstrating how the Pareto front can be visualized in the objective space.

The optimization process for single-objective problems involves searching for the best peak or trough in the fitness landscape, while for multi-objective problems, it requires identifying non-dominated solutions across multiple landscapes. Since optimal values are typically unknown in real-life scenarios, performance assessment of optimization algorithms is challenging, except in model calibration contexts where a known target exists. In such cases, split sample testing can provide insights into the robustness of the calibration process, though it remains influenced by various factors.
