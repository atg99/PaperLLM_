The paper presents a novel approach to model compression by focusing on the discriminative power of complete sub-networks while disregarding the magnitude of individual channels. The authors propose an efficient optimization method that leverages the straight-through estimator (STE) to navigate the challenges of optimizing discrete variables, which are typically non-smooth and NP-hard. Unlike Automatic Model Compression (AMC), which relies on reinforcement learning, the proposed method utilizes the gradients of the loss function to guide the exploration of sub-networks, allowing for efficient discovery of high-performance sub-networks on datasets like ImageNet, achieving results within 2% of regular training time.

The main contributions of the study include: 
1. The application of discrete gates on each channel to ensure that outputs from sub-networks remain correct and unbiased.
2. The use of STE to facilitate back-propagation through discrete variables, enhancing the search space for sub-networks by introducing a stochastic version of the discrete gate.
3. The introduction of resource regularization to ensure that the model adheres to given computational budgets.
4. Extensive experimental results demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on CIFAR10 and ImageNet with architectures like ResNet and MobileNetV2.

The paper also reviews existing model compression techniques, categorizing them into four main types: weight pruning, structural pruning, weight quantization, and knowledge distillation. It highlights the limitations of weight pruning, particularly the need for specialized libraries for acceleration, and contrasts its approach with structural pruning methods that rely on channel magnitude. The paper emphasizes the innovation of using local discriminative power for channel pruning, positioning the proposed method within the realm of existing research while showcasing its unique ability to optimize discrete channel settings through gradient descent. Additionally, it draws connections between the proposed method and weight quantization techniques, noting the shared use of STE for estimating gradients in discrete settings.
