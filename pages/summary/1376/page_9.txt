The paper discusses the representation of knowledge and decision-making processes in agent-based modeling systems (ABMS) and multi-agent systems (MAS). Knowledge in ABMS is often simplified as properties and values or modeled as state-machines. More sophisticated representations, such as fuzzy cognitive models and ontologies, have emerged, particularly from a MAS perspective. The decision-making process of agents relies on their current knowledge, which informs their actions and behavior. Basic decision-making can involve random number generation, but more structured approaches utilize predefined condition/action rules.

Intentional systems have influenced developments in decision-making models, particularly the belief-desire-intention (BDI) model, which mimics human decision-making by categorizing perceived information as beliefs and future states as desires and intentions. The paper reviews various cognitive and mathematical models for human decision-making in ABMS, highlighting the three main classes of agent architectures: reactive, deliberative, and hybrid, with BDI models being significant for deliberative architectures.

The interaction and coordination models are crucial for collective system behavior, facilitating communication between agents. Interactions can be spontaneous or goal-oriented, necessitating coordination to manage interdependencies. Coordination can occur through direct communication, utilizing defined languages and message types, or through indirect observation of environmental changes. Consensus among agents is vital for coordination, achievable through rules of information exchange.

The paper presents various techniques for coordination, including organizational structures, contracting, planning, and negotiation. It notes that the distinction between negotiation and consensus is often blurred in MAS literature. Additionally, nature-inspired coordination models, such as stigmergy and chemical coordination, are discussed, with examples drawn from animal organization and swarm intelligence. The paper concludes with an overview of common coordination models found in the literature.
