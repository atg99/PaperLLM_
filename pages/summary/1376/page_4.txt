The paper discusses various types of complex networks and their properties. It begins by defining a scale-free network, characterized by a power-law distribution of node degrees, indicating that some nodes have significantly more connections than others. This contrasts with random graphs, which follow a Poisson distribution.

Next, the paper describes planar or quasi-planar networks, where edges do not cross except at their endpoints. These networks are relevant for real-world applications, including street and road networks.

The community structure is highlighted, where nodes form dense connections within subsets but have sparser connections between them. Modularity is used to detect these structures, measuring the strength of division into communities.

The core-periphery structure is another important concept, consisting of a core of densely connected nodes and a sparsely connected periphery. This structure is of significant interest in complex network modeling.

The paper also outlines centrality measures, which describe network connectivity. Key measures include degree centrality (number of links per node), betweenness (shortest paths through a node), closeness (average distance between nodes), and eigenvector centrality (importance based on connections to high-scoring nodes). These measures can be extended to links as well.

Finally, the concept of transitivity or clustering is introduced, quantifying how nodes tend to cluster together by counting the number of triangles formed in the network. This property is essential for understanding the interconnectedness of nodes within complex networks.
