The paper provides a comprehensive overview of quantitative measures used to assess the quality of medical images, categorized into three main sections: overall image quality without reference, overall image quality with respect to a ground truth, and local image quality. 

Key measures without reference include human observer assessments, kernel density functions, inception scores, JS divergence, Wasserstein distance, global contrast factors, and vessel-based quality metrics. These measures often serve as gold standards or utilize advanced statistical methods to estimate image quality, though they can be costly and challenging to scale.

For overall image quality with respect to a ground truth, several metrics are highlighted, including normalized mean absolute/square errors, peak signal-to-noise ratios, structural similarity, visual information fidelity, and universal quality indices. These metrics provide a quantitative basis for comparing generated images against established standards.

Local image quality assessments focus on specific regions within images, employing measures such as line profiles, noise levels, contrast to background ratios, and clinical measures like standard uptake values. These metrics are crucial for evaluating localized image features and ensuring diagnostic accuracy.

The paper also discusses image quality analysis related to auxiliary tasks, emphasizing the importance of downstream tasks like classification, detection, segmentation, and cross-modality registration. The integration of segmentation maps and constraints in image synthesis is detailed, highlighting techniques that improve boundary accuracy and mitigate geometric distortions.

Moreover, the paper reviews advancements in segmentation methods, contrasting traditional pixel-wise loss functions with more sophisticated approaches that incorporate spatial consistency and high-order potentials through adversarial losses. This approach enhances the robustness of segmentation maps, particularly for compact shapes.

Overall, the paper systematically presents a variety of quantitative measures and methodologies for evaluating image quality in medical imaging, emphasizing the significance of both objective metrics and contextual considerations in the assessment process.
