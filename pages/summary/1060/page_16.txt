The paper discusses the application of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) in medical imaging, highlighting their effectiveness in tasks such as reconstruction, segmentation, and classification. Approximately 37% of the studies focus on reconstruction and segmentation, benefiting from adversarial training that enhances shape and texture regulation. For instance, in liver segmentation from 3D CT scans, the use of adversarial loss improves performance over traditional methods.

Another 8% of studies involve classification, primarily addressing domain shift issues. GANs are used for data augmentation, particularly for generating small, easily aligned objects like nodules and lesions. This focus is attributed to the lower variation in these images, which stabilizes training, alongside the high computational demands of processing larger images. Although GANs have been applied to synthesize full chest X-ray images, their effectiveness remains limited to simpler tasks and smaller datasets.

The paper argues that GANs hold potential in two areas: enhancing data augmentation by sampling the entire data distribution, and generating rare disease cases through conditional generation methods informed by medical experts. However, the remaining studies on detection and registration are too few to draw significant conclusions.

The authors also outline challenges faced in employing GANs in medical imaging. Current evaluation metrics often fail to correlate with visual image quality, leading to potential misinterpretations of results. Alternatives like learned perceptual image patch similarity (LPIPS) show promise but require further validation across different medical imaging types. The paper raises concerns over the biases introduced in unpaired training data, which can affect the integrity of generated images. This bias can lead to misrepresentation of abnormalities, necessitating careful training practices to ensure local features are preserved.

Finally, the paper suggests that while GANs present exciting future applications in medical imaging, careful consideration must be given to their implementation and evaluation to mitigate risks associated with bias and ensure reliable outcomes.
