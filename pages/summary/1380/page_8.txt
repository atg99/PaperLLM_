Figure 5 illustrates the performance of FEN and baseline models in terms of social welfare, represented by the total number of ghosts consumed by all pac-men, along with metrics such as coefficient of variation (CV), minimum and maximum income among pac-men, and the number of episodes required for convergence. Random pac-men exhibit inherent unfairness, reflected in their CV. The min pac-men struggle to learn effective policies, consistently yielding low consumption values. The Min+Avg model shows only marginal improvements in fairness compared to Avg, as the influence of min remains minimal.

The Independent approach results in the Matthew effect, where pac-men with larger initial sizes disproportionately consume more ghosts, leading to significant income disparities. In contrast, Inequity Aversion offers slightly better fairness but at the cost of lower social welfare. Although Independent features the largest pac-man, this does not guarantee efficient ghost consumption across the group.

FEN outperforms all baselines in both fairness and social welfare, illustrating its effectiveness in mitigating the Matthew effect while enhancing overall ghost consumption. FEN without hierarchy prioritizes fairness over efficiency, particularly in job scheduling contexts, and demonstrates slower learning, taking approximately 6000 episodes compared to FEN's 300 episodes, underscoring the benefits of hierarchical training.

Figure 6 compares learning curves for FEN, FEN with Gossip, and FEN without Hierarchy. The use of the gossip algorithm for decentralized average utility estimation shows that FEN with Gossip performs similarly to FEN, indicating the model's robustness in fully decentralized training environments.

To assess the utility of learned sub-policies, an experiment replaces some sub-policies with random ones, resulting in decreased minimum income and increased CV, confirming that learned sub-policies effectively promote fairness as intended. Visualization of agent positions under various sub-policies reveals that learned policies maintain distance from ghosts, validating the impact of the information-theoretic reward mechanism.
