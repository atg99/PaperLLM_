The paper investigates the effectiveness of a hierarchical approach in job scheduling through the Fair-Efficient Network (FEN) compared to a non-hierarchical version. The centralized policy, which aggregates observations from all agents to determine actions, was found to perform poorly due to the curse of dimensionality, as indicated in Table 1. In contrast, the FEN model, which incorporates a hierarchy, significantly outperformed the non-hierarchical version in achieving a higher mean fair-efficient reward, as shown in Figure 3.

The study highlights that while FEN w/o Hierarchy is fairer than other baselines, its resource utilization is subpar, demonstrating the challenge of learning efficiency from a single policy focused solely on fair-efficient rewards. The hierarchical structure allows for a decomposition of complex objectives, facilitating better learning outcomes. The introduction of hierarchy with other baselines also yielded improvements, particularly in resource utilization for Min and Min+Avg objectives, although these still fell short of the performance achieved by FEN.

Figure 4 illustrates the controller's behavior, revealing that it favors certain sub-policies based on the agents' utility deviations from the average, effectively balancing efficiency and fairness. 

Additionally, the paper explores the Matthew effect, where agents that perform better disproportionately benefit, creating inequality. The scenario involves pac-men agents competing for resources (ghosts) with inherent inequalities based on initial conditions. The results suggest that the hierarchical approach helps mitigate the Matthew effect by enabling agents to learn strategies that balance performance and fairness more effectively. Overall, the findings affirm the proposed hierarchy's role in enhancing learning efficiency in multi-agent systems.
