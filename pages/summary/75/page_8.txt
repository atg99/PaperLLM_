The paper by T. Lees et al. discusses the ability of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to form hydrological concepts and accurately predict discharge in various catchments. The analysis reveals that the largest probe biases occur in wetter or drier catchments, while the smallest biases are found in catchments with average precipitation. This finding, supported by a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, suggests that the LSTM effectively learns physically realistic mappings from inputs to outputs, reflecting underlying hydrological relationships.

The authors highlight two key opportunities for hydrologists arising from their findings. Firstly, the potential to predict intermediate hydrological variables, such as soil moisture and snow depth, is promising, especially given that the LSTM has demonstrated superior accuracy compared to other hydrological models. Secondly, the LSTM may reveal previously unidentified processes or variables that contribute to its performance, which could enhance traditional hydrological models.

Furthermore, the paper emphasizes the importance of exploring LSTM predictions in ungauged basins, potentially expanding the applicability of the model. The authors suggest employing methods like feature importance metrics to uncover significant signals within the LSTM state vector that could inform water management practices. Overall, the research points to valuable insights for improving hydrological modeling through the integration of LSTM networks.
