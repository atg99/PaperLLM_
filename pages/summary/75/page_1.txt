This paper investigates the interpretability of long short-term memory (LSTM) networks in the context of hydrological modeling, specifically focusing on rainfall-runoff processes. While LSTMs have proven effective in predicting river discharge from meteorological inputs, the authors aim to understand what these models have learned and whether they reflect established hydrological concepts.

The study extracts tensors from the LSTM that represent the relationships between inputs (precipitation, temperature, and potential evapotranspiration) and outputs (discharge). The authors assess the hypothesis that LSTMs can replicate real-world hydrological processes and extract information about these processes from the internal states of the model. They specifically analyze the cell-state vector, which serves as the memory component of the LSTM, to explore how it learns to represent water storage, such as soil moisture and snow cover.

Using a regression approach, the authors find strong correlations (R² > 0.8) between the LSTM outputs and target variables, indicating that the model captures information reflective of known hydrological processes, particularly variable-capacity soil moisture stores. The implications of this research are significant: LSTMs can reproduce established hydrological processes, derive representations from data without preconceived theoretical assumptions, and estimate intermediate water stores like soil moisture.

Overall, while the field of machine learning interpretability is still developing, this study provides robust evidence that deep learning methods can enhance both scientific understanding and predictive capabilities in hydrology.
