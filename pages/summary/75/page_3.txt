In the paper by T. Lees et al., the authors explore the learning capabilities of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks in relation to hydrological processes. They evaluate LSTM cell states across 669 catchments in Great Britain to determine if the network can generalize hydrological concepts spatially. The study emphasizes the importance of examining the entire LSTM state vector rather than focusing on individual cell values, as there are no constraints on how information is stored within the LSTM.

The methodology includes training LSTM models with hyperparameters similar to those used in previous studies. The LSTM framework is described using state-space formulations, highlighting how the model’s states and outputs depend on current inputs, previous states, and model parameters. Unlike classical hydrological models, which rely on predefined processes, LSTMs can infer processes directly from data.

The experimental design involved training a single LSTM to predict runoff using daily data from the CAMELS-GB dataset. The model incorporates meteorological variables and static catchment attributes, but does not include snow depth or soil moisture during training. 

To analyze the internal representations learned by the LSTM, the authors introduce the concept of probes—diagnostic tools that connect LSTM cell states to outputs. These probes, which can range from simple linear regressions to more complex models, allow for the examination of the LSTM's learned hydrological mappings. The research aims to confirm whether the LSTM has effectively captured physically realistic relationships, marking a novel application of probing techniques in hydrological modeling.
