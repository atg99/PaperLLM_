The paper by T. Lees et al. explores hydrological concept formation within long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, focusing on the relationship between LSTM cell-state dimensions and snow depth, as well as soil moisture dynamics. The authors present a probe simulation that correlates the LSTM's predictions of snow depth with actual observations from the ERA5-Land dataset for snowy catchments in Great Britain during the years 1998 to 2008. The study highlights the concentration of significant snow processes in specific regions, such as the Grampians and Pennines.

The research emphasizes the LSTM's capability in accurately modeling rainfall-runoff processes, particularly its association with soil moisture, which is crucial for understanding runoff responses, drought assessment, and climate change impacts. The findings indicate that the LSTM performs better in modeling shallower soil moisture layers compared to deeper ones, as shallower layers are more directly related to discharge signals.

The probe analysis serves as a tool for extracting intermediate hydrological concepts from the LSTM's state vector, allowing for interpretation of how the model processes information. While the authors caution against over-relying on probe outputs for predictive purposes, they find it valuable for understanding the anomalies in soil moisture that the LSTM associates with specific discharge outputs. The study also includes a comparison of probe predictions with satellite-derived soil moisture observations, demonstrating the potential of this method in hydrological modeling.
