This paper by T. Lees et al. investigates the interpretability of long short-term memory (LSTM) networks in the context of hydrological modeling, specifically focusing on how these models represent key hydrological concepts like soil water storage and snow processes. The authors aim to determine whether the internal state vector of the LSTM reflects these known hydrological concepts and whether the model's predictions can be understood in terms of physical processes.

The study builds on previous works that have explored the interpretability of neural networks in hydrology, highlighting the importance of understanding the model's behavior to ensure its reliability. The authors reference the work of Peter Young, advocating for the use of flexible data-driven models before applying mechanistic interpretations. They also note that prior research has shown LSTMs can generalize well to out-of-sample conditions, indicating that they capture relevant hydrological information.

The paper emphasizes the growing demand for interpretability in machine learning, particularly in light of increasing reliance on algorithmic decision-making tools. It outlines how interpretability can enhance trust in these systems and discusses existing literature on the topic.

The authors propose a novel approach to examining the internal representations of LSTM rainfall-runoff models, aiming to understand how these models encode hydrological information across multiple memory cells rather than assigning specific processes to individual cells. Their research seeks to provide insights into how LSTM models learn and represent hydrological states, marking a significant step in the interpretability of machine learning applications in hydrology.
