Figure 9 presents an illustration of the NRDS (Normalized Relative Discrepancy Score) methodology. Part A depicts the framework where Gn represents the nth generative model, producing fake samples labeled as Fake n, which are randomly sampled. Both the real and fake samples are utilized to train a binary classifier D. In the testing phase, only fake samples are evaluated, and this process alternates with training. The bottom right of Part A showcases the average output of D for the fake samples from each model.

Part B provides a toy example for calculating NRDS. It features real and fake samples drawn from 2D normal distributions with distinct means but identical covariance. The real samples are marked with zero mean (blue circles), while the fake samples are represented by red "x" and yellow "+" with means of [0.5, 0] and [1.5, 0], respectively. The terms fake-close and fake-far indicate the proximity of the fake samples' means to the real samples' mean. The right side of Part B illustrates the relationship between epochs and the average output of the discriminator for the respective sample sets. The areas under the curves for fake-close (C1) and fake-far (C2) are calculated, resulting in A(C1) = 145.4955 and A(C2) = 71.1057. From these values, NRDS is computed as NRDS 1 = 0.6717 for the model generating fake-close samples, indicating superior performance compared to NRDS 2 = 0.3283 for fake-far samples.

The equation for NRDS is outlined as NRDSi = A(Ci) / Σ A(Cj), where higher NRDS values indicate better model performance. 

Additionally, the paper discusses Adversarial Accuracy and Adversarial Divergence as proposed by Yang et al. These measures are based on the premise that the closeness of generated and real data distributions can be inferred from their conditional distributions given a variable of interest y, such as category labels. However, directly comparing Pg(x|y) and Pr(x|y) is impractical, so the authors suggest comparing Pg(y|x) and Pr(y|x) instead. This is accomplished by training classifiers using human annotations to approximate these distributions. The Adversarial Accuracy metric is then derived from the classification accuracies of the trained classifiers on a validation set, allowing for an assessment of how closely Pg(x) aligns with Pr(x).
