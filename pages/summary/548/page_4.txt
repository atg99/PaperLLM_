The paper discusses the evaluation of Neural Architecture Search (NAS) frameworks, highlighting the necessity of assessing not only the final model performance but also the effectiveness of the search phase itself. The authors differentiate between three phases of evaluation: the search phase, where the goal is to identify the best architecture from a given search space using algorithms like ENAS, DARTS, and NAO; the standard evaluation phase, which involves retraining the best architecture and evaluating it on test data; and a proposed evaluation phase that compares the results from the NAS algorithms to those from a random search policy.

In the search phase, the performance of existing NAS algorithms is heavily influenced by random initialization, leading to variability based on the chosen random seed. The evaluation phase traditionally focuses on the final model's test results, yet the authors argue that evaluating the search process itself is crucial for distinguishing the efficiency and effectiveness of different NAS algorithms.

To establish a baseline, the authors implement a random search policy that assigns uniform probabilities to operations within a Directed Acyclic Graph representation of the architecture. They then compare the performance of the best architecture found through NAS with that of a randomly sampled architecture. To ensure a reliable comparison, the process is repeated across multiple random seeds, allowing for the calculation of mean and standard deviation results.

This method of evaluation emphasizes the importance of a robust search algorithm that consistently outperforms random sampling, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the efficacy of NAS techniques. The authors note that their approach contrasts with previous evaluations that may have relied on a single random architecture, which would not yield reliable measures of performance.
