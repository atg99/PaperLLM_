This paper investigates the search phase of existing Neural Architecture Search (NAS) algorithms by comparing their performance against a random search policy. The study involves sampling architectures uniformly from the same search space as the NAS algorithms and training them with identical hyperparameters. To mitigate randomness, multiple trials are conducted using different random seeds.

Experiments are performed on the Penn Tree Bank and CIFAR-10 datasets, examining state-of-the-art NAS algorithms, including DARTS, NAO, and ENAS. The findings reveal that none of these algorithms significantly outperform random sampling. Welch's t-tests indicate that in RNN space, ENAS and DARTS perform similarly to random architectures, while NAO performs worse. Although results are slightly better in CNN space, all three algorithms still align closely with random sampling. This suggests that the search space is sufficiently constrained, allowing random architectures to yield good results.

The study also highlights a critical issue regarding the architecture rankings produced by NAS algorithms, which do not accurately reflect true performance during evaluation. The use of weight sharing, designed to reduce resource consumption, appears to negatively impact the performance of individual networks. Analysis using the Kendall Tau metric shows low correlation in architecture rankings between weight sharing and non-weight sharing scenarios, further explaining the minimal performance difference between NAS and random search.

The authors argue that the common belief in the similarity of architecture quality with and without weight sharing is incorrect. The negative impact of weight sharing on ranking undermines the robustness and performance of NAS algorithms. Overall, the paper emphasizes the importance of carefully evaluating the search phase of NAS and identifies key characteristics such as the significance of the search space and the detrimental effects of weight sharing. The proposed evaluation framework aims to enhance the design of superior NAS search strategies. The code for this research is publicly available.
