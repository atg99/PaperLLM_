The paper discusses advancements in Neural Architecture Search (NAS), highlighting the limitations of traditional methods and the evolution towards more efficient approaches. It begins by noting that vanilla NAS can only evaluate a limited number of architectures, necessitating substantial computational resources. The introduction of Efficient NAS (ENAS) significantly reduced the training time by sharing parameters across models, allowing for a more efficient search process.

The research trajectory in NAS has primarily focused on two areas: enhancing search algorithms by replacing reinforcement learning with techniques like gradient descent and Bayesian optimization, and expanding NAS applications to fields such as object detection and semantic segmentation. 

The authors emphasize the importance of characterizing the search space, referencing various studies that provide insights into the performance of CNN architectures on standard datasets like CIFAR-10 and ImageNet. Despite these contributions, the paper argues that there is a lack of thorough evaluations of state-of-the-art NAS algorithms.

In their evaluation, the authors aim to deepen understanding of NAS mechanisms by comparing these algorithms against a random search policy that samples architectures uniformly. They critique previous comparisons that favored NAS methods, arguing that a fair evaluation—where both NAS and random architectures are trained for an equal number of epochs—reveals no significant performance advantage for NAS algorithms.

The authors link the discrepancy in performance between the search and evaluation phases to weight sharing mechanisms, contrasting their findings with those of previous studies that suggested a correlation between architectures trained with and without weight sharing. They argue that differences in training schemes and the extent of architecture evaluation contribute to the observed performance outcomes.

The paper concludes by outlining its evaluation framework for the NAS search phase, setting the stage for further exploration of the mechanisms at play in NAS methodologies.
