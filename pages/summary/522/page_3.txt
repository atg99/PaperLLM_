This paper explores contrastive unsupervised representation learning, focusing on the relationship between unsupervised and supervised learning losses. The authors define two primary loss functions: the hinge loss and the logistic loss, which are utilized in supervised classification tasks. The supervised loss for a classifier is presented as a function of a representation function, with a specific emphasis on a mean classifier whose rows are averages of class-specific representations.

The average supervised loss is introduced, which reflects the expected loss across multiple classes. The paper then outlines the training objective for contrastive learning, emphasizing the importance of the chosen loss function and the number of negative samples used. The unsupervised loss is defined, both in population and empirical terms, and a method for minimizing this loss is proposed.

The results indicate that the unsupervised loss serves as a surrogate for the supervised loss, suggesting that minimizing the unsupervised loss can lead to improved supervised performance. The authors provide theoretical guarantees showing that the supervised loss of the learned representation is bounded by the unsupervised loss, with specific conditions relating to the distribution of classes.

Furthermore, the paper discusses the potential to achieve low unsupervised loss on practical datasets, revealing that this can be influenced by the richness of the function class used. The authors conclude that if the function class is sufficiently expressive, it can lead to improved supervised learning outcomes. Overall, the work presents a framework for understanding and improving contrastive unsupervised representation learning through robust theoretical analysis.
