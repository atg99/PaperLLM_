The paper critically examines the assumptions surrounding the unsupervised learning of disentangled representations. It presents theoretical findings indicating that achieving disentangled representations without inductive biases is fundamentally impossible. The empirical study involved six state-of-the-art disentanglement methods across seven datasets, revealing several key insights: (i) a factorizing aggregated posterior does not guarantee uncorrelated representation dimensions, (ii) random seeds and hyperparameters significantly influence outcomes, often necessitating supervision for tuning, and (iii) increased disentanglement does not correlate with improved sample efficiency in learning downstream tasks.

The authors advocate for future research to focus on incorporating inductive biases and both implicit and explicit supervision, as current strategies for hyperparameter selection in unsupervised learning are unreliable. They suggest exploring settings that involve interactions, weak supervision, or temporal structures, which could enhance disentanglement learning.

Additionally, the paper notes the lack of compelling evidence demonstrating the practical benefits of disentangled representations, urging future studies to provide concrete examples of their usefulness, particularly in areas like interpretability and fairness. The authors emphasize the importance of a robust experimental setup across diverse datasets to avoid drawing spurious conclusions and highlight their contribution of the disentanglement_lib library, which includes over 10,000 trained models for benchmarking future research.
