This paper discusses the limitations of kernel methods in regression tasks compared to neural networks, particularly in terms of sample complexity and generalization error. The authors present a convex minimization problem that kernel methods aim to solve for optimal weights. They emphasize that the study does not make assumptions about how the kernel is derived but instead focuses on kernel regression functions of a specific form.

The paper identifies various types of Mercer kernels, including Gaussian, arcsin, recursive, random feature, conjugate, and neural tangent kernels. The authors state a theorem indicating that for sufficiently large dimensions, there exist function classes where kernel methods struggle to achieve low population risk, even when using a number of training samples proportional to the sample complexity required for neural networks.

A key contribution of the paper is the demonstration of a provable separation in generalization error between neural networks trained by stochastic gradient descent and kernel methods. Neural networks can achieve better population risk with fewer samples due to their hierarchical learning capability, whereas kernel methods must learn all components simultaneously, leading to a higher sample complexity.

The authors argue that the inability of kernel methods to efficiently learn complex functions stems from their dependence on the entire complexity of the function rather than breaking it down into simpler components. This results in kernel methods lacking sufficient degrees of freedom when sample size is limited, leading to poor performance in distinguishing between different function forms.

In conclusion, the paper highlights the inherent advantages of neural networks over kernel methods in terms of efficiency and generalization capability, particularly in high-dimensional settings.
