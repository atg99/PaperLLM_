This paper investigates the effects of random Gaussian initialization in the context of training neural networks using stochastic gradient descent (SGD). The initialization of the matrices A, W(0), and V(0) is performed with independent entries drawn from normal distributions. The primary focus is on minimizing a specific `2 loss function between the model output and the target, defined as the objective function Obj(W, V, (x, y)).

The authors analyze a vanilla SGD algorithm, initializing weights W0 and V0 to zero. For each iteration, a random sample is drawn, and updates are performed on the weights based on the gradients of the objective function. The paper presents Theorem 1, which establishes conditions under which the SGD algorithm can achieve a population risk close to the optimal risk with high probability. The result relies on careful parameter choices and the properties of random initialization, leading to a sample complexity bound.

The proof of Theorem 1 is structured into three steps, utilizing diagonal matrices that encapsulate the weight updates. The authors highlight that their findings align closely with PAC-learning principles, although there is an unavoidable additive error term that cannot be minimized arbitrarily.

In summary, the paper provides a thorough analysis of the initialization and training dynamics of neural networks, demonstrating that with appropriate parameter choices, SGD can effectively minimize the loss function, achieving performance close to the optimal risk under certain conditions.
