The paper discusses the distributional effects of artificial intelligence (AI) and robotics, focusing on the implications for employment across different industries and the potential biases in algorithmic decision-making. Existing research identifies both "winners" and "losers" in the workforce due to technological advancements. For instance, Autor and Salomons highlight that productivity increases in specific industries often lead to job losses within those sectors, but positive spillover effects in other industries can compensate for these losses. Mandel's analysis of retail during the rise of e-commerce supports this, showing job creation in fulfillment and call centers outpacing department store job losses.

The paper also explores how skill composition influences the impact of AI. Choudhury et al. find that individuals with computer science or engineering backgrounds are better positioned to leverage AI technologies effectively, leading to superior performance. Felten et al. further demonstrate that occupations requiring software skills experience employment growth in response to AI advancements, while other roles do not exhibit significant changes.

In terms of algorithmic decision-making, the literature indicates that machine learning can improve decision quality and equity. For instance, Kleinberg et al. note that algorithms can outperform human judges in pre-trial detainment decisions. However, concerns arise regarding inherent biases in these algorithms. Research shows that certain algorithms can disproportionately target men over women in job advertisements and exhibit racial biases in judicial contexts. Despite these issues, some argue that algorithmic decision-making may still provide better outcomes compared to traditional human processes. Overall, the paper emphasizes the complex interplay between technological advancements, employment dynamics, and the ethical considerations surrounding algorithmic biases.
