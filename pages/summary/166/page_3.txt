This paper discusses the mechanics of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which involve two main components: a generator and a discriminator. The generator creates samples from a prior distribution, often unstructured such as a high-dimensional Gaussian or uniform distribution, and transforms random input vectors into realistic data. The discriminator evaluates whether the samples are real or fake, providing an estimate of their authenticity.

Both players incur costs during the training process; the generator seeks to minimize its cost by generating samples that the discriminator misclassifies as real, while the discriminator aims to correctly classify the samples. Various formulations of these costs exist, with the original GANs formulation defining the discriminator's cost as the negative log-likelihood of real versus fake labels. The generator's cost can be defined in two main ways: the minimax GAN (M-GAN), which directly opposes the discriminator's cost, and the non-saturating GAN (NS-GAN), which avoids issues of gradient saturation by adjusting the labels used for training.

The analogy of counterfeiters and police is used to illustrate the adversarial nature of the training process, where the generator continuously improves its outputs to create indistinguishable fakes while the discriminator learns to detect them. This dynamic leads to an iterative learning process that enhances the realism of generated samples.

Overall, the paper emphasizes the competitive interplay between the generator and discriminator, highlighting their distinct roles and the evolving nature of their training as they respond to each other's performance.
