In this paper, the authors describe a method for setting a threshold Z-score to enhance the performance of a machine learning-based network intrusion detection system (ML-NIDS). The Z-score is established by standardizing reconstruction distances derived from normal samples in the validation data, focusing on a range between -4 and 4, which encompasses 99.994% of the standard normal distribution. The optimal Z-score threshold (Th) is determined by analyzing its effectiveness in distinguishing between normal and abnormal classes within the validation dataset.

The evaluation of the ML-NIDS is conducted on test data, where each log's reconstruction error is computed and classified as an attack if its Z-score surpasses the established threshold. The model's performance is assessed by comparing predicted labels against actual labels, leading to the calculation of key evaluation metrics, including accuracy, true positive rate (TPR), false positive rate (FPR), and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC).

The study also delves into the design of autoencoder models used in anomaly-based intrusion detection. It emphasizes two main design aspects: model structure, which includes the depth and width of the hidden layers, and latent size, which refers to the dimension of the latent layer. The paper highlights the importance of balancing the latent layer size to avoid underfitting or overfitting, thereby affecting the model's ability to accurately represent the data.

Standard evaluation metrics such as accuracy, TPR, and FPR are reported to facilitate comparisons across different model configurations, with particular attention to the challenges of high false alarm rates in anomaly-based IDS.
