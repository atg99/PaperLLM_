The paper discusses the evolution of autonomy in unmanned systems, highlighting significant advancements and frameworks developed over the years. Initially, the Air Force Research Laboratory aimed to enhance autonomous capabilities, leading to the establishment of the Autonomy Levels for Unmanned Systems (ALFUS) by the National Institute of Standards and Technology. This initiative resulted in a comprehensive list of definitions, metrics for evaluating mission complexity, environmental difficulty, and human-robot interaction, as well as models illustrating transitions between autonomy levels.

The concept of adjustable autonomy emerged, allowing human users or systems to modify autonomy levels during operations. This shift coincided with a resurgence in AI, producing intelligent systems that require effective communication interfaces, enhancing user-system interaction through gestures, voice, and touch. Key concerns such as social acceptability, trust, reliability, and safety became integral to research efforts aimed at improving autonomous systems.

The paper summarizes the historical context of robotics and automation, noting initial slow progress due to limited understanding and resources. It describes the transition from supervisory control to adaptive and adjustable autonomy, supported by advancements in AI and human-machine teamwork (HMT). The challenges of implementing levels of autonomy and control dynamics are emphasized, along with comparative studies assessing the efficiency and robustness of various autonomy modes. Overall, the research reflects a significant progression toward more sophisticated and socially acceptable autonomous systems.
