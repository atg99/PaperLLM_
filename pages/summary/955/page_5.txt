The paper investigates the training efficiency and performance of Hamiltonian Neural Networks (HNNs) compared to baseline models across various tasks. It finds that HNNs achieve similar convergence rates and final losses as baseline models, yet significantly outperform them on the Mean Squared Error (MSE) energy metric. This is attributed to the HNN's ability to conserve a quantity closely resembling total energy, which diverges more slowly than the baseline model that accumulates errors over time.

The conserved quantity in HNNs is not strictly total energy but is sufficiently close for practical purposes. Notably, while the HNN shows better energy conservation compared to the ground truth data for a real pendulum, it is limited by its assumption of a conserved quantity, making it less effective in scenarios involving friction.

The paper then transitions to modeling larger systems, specifically the two-body problem, which involves four (p, q) pairs. The Hamiltonian for this system is defined, and experiments focus on datasets generated from near-circular trajectories with controlled initial conditions. The HNN model effectively scales to this more complex system, demonstrating superior performance in conserving energy compared to baseline models. While both models diverge over time, the HNN exhibits a slower rate of divergence, indicating its robustness in maintaining accuracy over longer integrations.
