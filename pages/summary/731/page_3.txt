This paper discusses various approaches and advancements in reinforcement learning, particularly focusing on two time-scale algorithms and actor-critic methods. Hu and Syed formulated a family of temporal difference (TD) learning algorithms as Markov jump linear systems, analyzing the mean and covariance of estimation errors. Cai et al. demonstrated the global convergence of TD learning with neural network approximations.

The paper highlights the significance of two time-scale stochastic approximation as a framework for reinforcement learning analysis. Recent studies, including those by Dalal et al. and Gupta et al., have provided finite-time convergence rates for two time-scale linear stochastic approximation under independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) assumptions. Xu et al. extended this by proving convergence rates and sample complexity for the TDC algorithm with Markovian samples. However, the non-linear nature of actor updates in actor-critic algorithms limits the applicability of these results.

Actor-critic methods have been extensively studied, with foundational work by Konda and Tsitsiklis establishing asymptotic convergence for two time-scale methods. Bhatnagar et al. and Castro and Meir contributed to convergence results for various actor-critic variations. Recent work has focused on finite-time behavior, with Yang et al. studying global convergence under specific conditions and Qiu et al. framing actor-critic algorithms as bilevel optimization problems.

The paper notes concurrent work analyzing non-asymptotic convergence of two time-scale actor-critic algorithms, highlighting key differences. The authors present a classical two-time scale algorithm that is straightforward to implement, contrasting it with more complex alternatives. Their analysis does not rely on specific function approximation classes, making it potentially applicable to broader contexts, including non-linear approximations with neural networks.

In the preliminary section, the paper outlines the fundamentals of Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), which model reinforcement learning tasks. It defines the components of MDPs, including state and action spaces, transition probabilities, and reward functions, while addressing the average reward setting under ergodicity assumptions.
