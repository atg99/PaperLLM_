The paper discusses the concept of artificial intelligence (AI) as a "technosocial system," emphasizing that AI's technical and social dimensions are deeply interconnected. It explores how societal values and cultural narratives shape the development, perception, and use of AI technologies. The authors argue that the technologies we create are influenced not only by our technical capabilities but also by the cultural stories that inform our imaginations.

For instance, the creation of the personal cell phone was inspired by fictional representations like Captain Kirk's communicator, demonstrating how cultural narratives can drive technological innovation. The paper contrasts American fears of technology, influenced by violent science fiction, with a more positive Japanese cultural imagination, where robots are often seen as friendly entities due to influences such as Shintoism. This cultural perspective may explain the acceptance of robots like Sony's AIBO in Japan.

However, the paper also highlights the complexities of cultural imagination, noting that while there is an ideal of harmonious coexistence with robots, practical applications—like robots as caregivers for the elderly—face resistance due to a preference for human interaction. This indicates that cultural values can unpredictably influence technology acceptance.

The discussion extends to the design and development of AI, underscoring that social contexts and cultural assumptions are integral to AI systems, even when they function correctly. The paper concludes by raising critical questions about who creates AI and for whom it is designed, suggesting that understanding these dynamics is essential for addressing AI ethics across different cultures.
