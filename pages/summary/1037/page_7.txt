The paper discusses the limitations of using the concept of "culture" in understanding differences, particularly in medical and technological contexts. It critiques the "trait list" approach, which oversimplifies cultural influences and can lead to misattributions of behavior. An example illustrates this point: a physician mistakenly attributes a Mexican immigrant's failure to seek medical care for his HIV-positive son to cultural beliefs, when in fact, socioeconomic factors are the underlying cause.

The paper emphasizes the importance of recognizing that cultural differences exist alongside other significant factors, such as economic status and access to technology. It argues for a more nuanced understanding of culture, proposing a working definition that views culture as a dynamic, shared repertoire of understandings shaped by history, politics, and economics. 

Key components of this definition are that culture is shared yet not necessarily agreed upon, and that it is heterogeneous, encompassing a variety of subcultures and individual differences. The authors advocate for moving beyond oversimplified notions of culture to better understand people's experiences and responses to new technologies.
