The paper discusses the evolving landscape of AI ethics, particularly in the context of Singapore and China. It highlights how increased access to information and a slowing economy have led to growing skepticism among citizens regarding their governments' practices. For instance, Singaporeans are becoming more aware that their government's approach to repression is not typical of developed democracies, suggesting a shift towards questioning governmental authority.

In China, there is a notable emergence of public discourse surrounding AI ethics, exemplified by Tencent Digital's article discussing the dual nature of facial recognition technology. This reflects a broader willingness within Chinese policy circles to engage with ethical considerations, highlighted by the introduction of the Beijing AI Principles in 2019.

Additionally, the paper examines the social credit system in China, which is often misunderstood internationally. Contrary to popular belief, there is no unified national social credit score; instead, the system encompasses various initiatives aimed at enforcing laws, improving financial reliability, and reducing dishonest behavior. While some city-level experiments use numerical scoring, the state does not currently employ this system for significant social judgments. Despite misconceptions, the Chinese public generally views the social credit system positively, particularly among younger, educated urban residents who see it as a means to combat fraud and enhance societal trust.
