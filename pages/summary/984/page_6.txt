In the paper by Torfi et al., the authors delve into advancements in natural language processing (NLP) facilitated by deep learning techniques. They emphasize the training of sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) models, which rely on the maximum-likelihood criterion and utilize cross-entropy loss minimization. The model is optimized to generate output sequences by predicting the next token based on previous outputs and the current state.

To enhance output quality, the authors mention beam search, a method that retains multiple output paths rather than selecting a single best output. Model performance is evaluated using task-specific metrics like ROUGE, BLEU, and METEOR, with a particular focus on ROUGE L for its effectiveness in measuring common substrings between generated and ground-truth outputs.

The paper highlights two significant challenges faced by seq2seq models: exposure bias and inconsistencies between training and testing evaluations. Exposure bias arises when the model relies on ground-truth data during training but must depend solely on its predictions during testing, leading to error accumulation. The authors propose scheduled sampling as a solution, gradually transitioning from ground-truth inputs to model-generated samples during training.

The second challenge involves the use of non-differentiable evaluation metrics such as ROUGE, which creates a disconnect between training objectives and evaluation measures. The authors suggest that reinforcement learning techniques, particularly policy gradient methods and actor-critic models, can address these issues. These methods allow the model to learn from its predictions rather than ground-truth data, facilitating a more coherent training and evaluation process.

In actor-critic frameworks, the actor generates outputs while the critic assesses performance, providing feedback to improve actions. However, the authors note the significant challenge posed by the vast action space in NLP applications, which can include tens of thousands of tokens compared to the limited actions in other domains like robotics or Atari games. This complexity complicates the application of actor-critic models in NLP, necessitating further exploration and innovation in this area.
