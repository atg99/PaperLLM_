The paper discusses the current state of neural network pruning, highlighting its potential benefits and limitations. Pruning can enhance the tradeoff between time, space, and accuracy, sometimes even improving accuracy. However, it is generally less effective than switching to superior architectures. The effectiveness of pruning tends to be higher in architectures that are initially less efficient.

A significant issue identified is the lack of controlled comparisons in pruning research. Many studies claim advancements without benchmarking against existing methods, leading to fragmentation in reported results. This problem is exacerbated by the neglect of earlier works in the field, as many contemporary papers fail to acknowledge foundational research from the 1990s and earlier.

The analysis reveals that a substantial portion of recent pruning methods does not outperform established state-of-the-art techniques. Furthermore, there is a lack of comprehensive comparisons, with many papers evaluating only a few existing methods. This limited scope hinders the ability to determine definitive advancements in the field.

Additionally, the study notes significant fragmentation in datasets and architectures used across 81 papers, with numerous combinations yielding inconsistent results. The most common setup, VGG-16 on ImageNet, appeared in only 22 papers, emphasizing the need for larger datasets and models to accurately assess pruning methods' effectiveness. Overall, the paper underscores the necessity for standardized experimental practices to facilitate meaningful comparisons and progress in neural network pruning research.
