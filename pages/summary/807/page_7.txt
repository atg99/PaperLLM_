The paper addresses the current state of neural network pruning, highlighting key challenges in evaluating and comparing different pruning methods. It presents data showing that most research focuses on a limited number of dataset and architecture pairs, with many studies reporting efficiency vs accuracy tradeoffs using only single points rather than comprehensive curves.

A significant concern raised is the impact of confounding variables on accuracy assessments. The authors argue that simply reporting changes in accuracy relative to initial models does not sufficiently control for these confounding factors, which can affect the perceived effectiveness of pruning methods. They illustrate this by showing that even minor accuracy changes can significantly influence the ranking of methods. Additionally, variability in training and fine-tuning settings can introduce discrepancies comparable to those seen across different pruning techniques.

The paper emphasizes the fragmentation in datasets, models, metrics, and experimental practices as barriers to effective comparison. It points out the ambiguity in architectural descriptions, particularly with variations of popular architectures like ResNet, which complicates the replication of studies and the assessment of results. This ambiguity, along with inconsistent reporting practices, further hinders the ability to draw definitive conclusions about the efficacy of various pruning strategies. Overall, the paper calls for a more standardized approach in reporting and evaluating pruning techniques to facilitate better comparisons and advancements in the field.
