The paper discusses the state of neural network pruning, emphasizing the tradeoff between model efficiency and quality. Pruning enhances efficiency, often at the cost of accuracy, and is characterized by a family of models that represent different points on the efficiency-quality curve. Two primary metrics for quantifying efficiency are the number of multiply-adds (FLOPs) and the fraction of parameters pruned, while quality is typically measured through Top-1 or Top-5 image classification accuracy.

The authors conducted a meta-analysis of 81 papers, including 79 pruning studies and two foundational papers. They found that pruning is effective, often compressing models with minimal accuracy loss, and can sometimes improve accuracy with small compression. Moreover, various methods outperform random pruning, particularly for larger amounts of pruning. Intelligent allocation of parameters across layers tends to yield better results than uniform pruning. 

Another key finding is that sparse models generally outperform dense models with the same number of parameters, with some pruned models achieving higher accuracy than their denser counterparts. The paper also highlights a gap in understanding how pruning compares to using more efficient architectures, suggesting that further exploration is needed to determine which approach yields greater benefits across different architectures.
