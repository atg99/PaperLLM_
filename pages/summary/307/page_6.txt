The paper reviews recent research on the impact of neural architectures, particularly BERT, on language model pretraining and its implications for downstream task accuracy and contextualized word representations. Peters et al. (2018) establish that various architectures, including CNNs, Transformers, and RNNs, produce high-quality representations that surpass traditional word embeddings. They highlight a hierarchical structuring of linguistic information within these models. The authors extend this finding to BERT, confirming its ability to represent syntactic information and demonstrating that deeper layers are necessary for modeling long-distance dependencies.

Tenney et al. (2019) introduce edge probing tasks to explore how contextualized representations encode sentence structure, concluding that while syntactic phenomena are well captured, improvements in semantic tasks are limited compared to non-contextual methods. Liu et al. (2019) further investigate the limitations of contextualized embeddings, showing that they struggle with fine-grained linguistic knowledge and emphasizing the importance of pretraining tasks.

Hewitt and Manning (2019) demonstrate that parse trees can be reconstructed from contextual representations, focusing on syntactic structure, while the current work also explores linear structures to highlight BERT's compositional modeling capabilities akin to traditional syntactic analysis.

The findings contribute to a growing body of literature on the interpretability of contextualized word embeddings, confirming that BERT effectively captures structural properties of the English language, including phrase-level information and a hierarchy of linguistic signals. The authors suggest future research could explore the applicability of these results in more syntactically variable domains.
