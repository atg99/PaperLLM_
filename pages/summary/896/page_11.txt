The study by Taylor and Nitschke compares the effectiveness of geometric and photometric transformations in image processing. Geometric transformations included flipping, rotations from -30° to 30°, and cropping, while photometric transformations involved color jittering, edge enhancement, and PCA. They conducted their experiments using 4-fold cross-validation on the Caltech101 dataset, which consisted of 8421 images sized 256 × 256.

Kernel filters, commonly used in image processing, serve to sharpen or blur images by applying an n × n matrix across the image. Blurring can enhance resistance to motion blur, while sharpening can reveal more details of objects. Kang et al. introduced a novel technique called PatchShuffle Regularization, which randomly swaps pixel values within an n × n sliding window. Their experiments showed a reduction in error rate from 6.33% to 5.66% on the CIFAR-10 dataset using this technique, with optimal settings of 2 × 2 filters and a 0.05 probability of pixel swapping, utilizing the ResNet CNN architecture.

Despite its potential, kernel filters remain underexplored for data augmentation. One limitation is their similarity to the mechanisms of CNNs, which utilize parametric kernels that learn to represent images. PatchShuffle Regularization could be implemented as a convolution layer, ensuring spatial resolution is preserved while maintaining pixel values between 0 and 255.

Taylor and Nitschke's results indicated that cropping was the most effective geometric transformation, yielding a top-1 accuracy of 61.95%.
