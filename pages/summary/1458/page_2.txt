The paper discusses the construction of natural language understanding (NLU) datasets, emphasizing the role of annotators in generating examples. It highlights that a small number of annotators often produce the majority of dataset examples, with ratios such as one annotator per 102–103 examples. This skewed distribution raises concerns about data diversity and potential over-estimation of model performance, particularly in creative language tasks.

The study focuses on three specific datasets: MNLI (matched), OPENBOOK QA, and COMMONSENSE QA, detailing the number of examples and annotators involved. For instance, MNLI has 402,517 examples created by 380 annotators, while COMMONSENSE QA has 11,096 examples from 132 annotators. The findings suggest that a small subset of annotators contributes disproportionately to the datasets.

The experimental setup involves using the pretrained BERT-base model to evaluate annotator bias by addressing three main questions: whether models perform better with annotator ID information, if models can identify annotators from examples, and if they generalize across different annotators. Initial experiments involve appending annotator IDs to examples to assess the impact on model performance, aiming to quantify the influence of annotator information on NLU tasks.
