The paper investigates the performance of models on different data splits, focusing on the impact of annotator variability. The main finding is that in the OpenBook QA dataset, performance on the multi-annotator split is significantly lower than on random splits, with drops of up to 23 accuracy points. This indicates that models struggle to generalize to examples from unseen annotators. In other datasets, performance variability is observed, with drops in approximately half of the cases, notably a 10-point drop in Commonsense QA and 5 points in MNLI.

The analysis of single-annotator splits reveals that performance is also lower than random splits in many instances. However, this measure may be misleading due to differences in the difficulty of examples produced by individual annotators. To address this, the authors propose an augmentation experiment to distinguish between annotator bias and difficulty. 

In this experiment, a portion of examples from a low-performing development set is moved to the training set. If the low performance is due to annotator bias, the model's accuracy should increase as it learns from these examples. The results show a significant accuracy improvement (10-20 points) for OpenBook QA and Commonsense QA when examples from effective annotators are included in training, suggesting that the examples are not inherently difficult. In contrast, MNLI shows marginal performance changes, indicating that generalization patterns may be influenced more by example difficulty rather than annotator bias. The findings highlight the importance of understanding annotator effects on model performance and the potential benefits of targeted training data augmentation.
