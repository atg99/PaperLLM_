The paper focuses on univariate decision trees (DTs), which consist of nodes associated with single features and paths leading to terminal nodes that represent predictions. Each non-terminal node has one incoming edge, while terminal nodes are linked to a prediction value from set K. The structure of the DT allows for two sets of paths: those leading to positive predictions (denoted as P) and negative predictions (denoted as Q). A literal in this context is defined as a specific feature-value combination, and generalized literals can represent broader sets of values.

The paper emphasizes the concept of interpretability in decision trees, which is viewed as subjective and varies among authors. The authors argue that interpretability is linked to irreducible sets of feature-value pairs sufficient for making predictions. Univariate DTs are often considered interpretable due to their simplicity, although some recent studies suggest that deeper trees might also be interpretable through counterfactual explanations.

Additionally, the paper introduces the concept of PI-explanations, which are based on prime implicants of a decision function. These explanations are connected to the classification function applied to feature space, providing insight into the prediction made for a specific input. The authors note that the findings can be generalized to various types of features, but the scope of the discussion remains focused on univariate decision trees. Overall, the paper highlights the balance between the complexity of decision trees and the need for succinct explanations that human decision-makers can easily understand.
