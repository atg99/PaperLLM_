This paper investigates the interpretability of decision tree classifiers, revealing that many paths within these trees often contain irrelevant literals, challenging the assumption that decision trees are inherently interpretable. The research indicates that for decision trees generated by state-of-the-art tools IAI and ITI, a significant portion of paths—averaging 32.1% for IAI and 46.9% for ITI—exhibit explanation-redundancy, with some trees exceeding 85% redundancy. Additionally, redundant paths cover substantial portions of the feature space, averaging 20.1% for IAI and 37.7% for ITI, and in extreme cases, up to 98% coverage.

The paper also introduces a linear time test to identify irrelevant literals in decision tree paths and develops a polynomial time algorithm for computing one prime implicant (PI) explanation of a decision tree. The connection between enumerating PI-explanations and minimal hitting sets is established. Experimental results confirm that even irreducible decision trees may contain irrelevant literals, and the proposed algorithms have negligible or comparable run times to tree learning times.

In conclusion, the findings challenge the prevailing belief in the interpretability of decision trees and suggest that reducing explanation-redundancy can be achieved with minimal computational cost.
