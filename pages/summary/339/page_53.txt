The paper discusses the limitations of the model, particularly in generating specific and practical solutions in sensitive domains such as threat analysis. It notes that the model often produces vague or impractical responses, particularly when asked for detailed instructions on creating hazardous materials like radiological devices or biochemical compounds. These inaccuracies can undermine the effectiveness of threat actors by introducing errors.

The paper highlights that the model can provide general information, such as directing users to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) for nucleotide sequences of anthrax toxins, but this information is insufficient for practical applications. Additionally, the model has been trained on a wide variety of data, which may include publicly available personal information, potentially enabling it to identify individuals when combined with external data.

To mitigate privacy risks, the developers have implemented several measures including fine-tuning the model to reject inappropriate requests, removing personal data from training sets, and monitoring user interactions. The paper also addresses the model's utility in cybersecurity tasks, noting its ability to assist with social engineering and data analysis, but points out its significant limitations due to its tendency to "hallucinate" information and its constrained context window, which hinder its effectiveness in more advanced cybersecurity operations.
