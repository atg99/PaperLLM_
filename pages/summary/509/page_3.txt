The paper discusses the concept of graph self-supervised learning (SSL), highlighting the differences between pseudo labels, downstream tasks, pretext tasks, and various learning paradigms. Pseudo labels are generated from data by machines, significantly reducing acquisition costs compared to manual labeling, which is particularly advantageous in scenarios with vast amounts of data. Downstream tasks, such as node and graph classification, evaluate the feature representations learned by models, while pretext tasks, like graph reconstruction, help models learn generalized representations from unlabeled data.

The paper distinguishes between supervised learning, which relies on manual labels, unsupervised learning that does not use any labels, and self-supervised learning, which generates supervision signals from the data itself. The authors introduce important notations and definitions for different types of graphs and graph neural networks (GNNs), explaining how GNNs transform raw node features into compact representations leveraging the graph's underlying connectivity.

A unified framework for graph SSL is proposed, structured around an encoder-decoder model. The encoder learns low-dimensional representations for each node, while the decoder is tailored to specific pretext tasks. The optimization problem for graph SSL is formulated, aiming to minimize the SSL loss function. The generated representations from the trained encoder can then be applied to various downstream tasks, with a separate downstream decoder formulated for these tasks, which involves supervised learning.

The paper categorizes graph SSL methods based on pretext tasks, downstream tasks, and self-supervised training schemes, setting the stage for a detailed exploration of different variants and training approaches in subsequent sections.
