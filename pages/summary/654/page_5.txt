Theorem 2 establishes that a Graph Neural Network (GNN) A, structured as a composition of layers L, can differentiate between non-isomorphic graphs G1 and G2 under certain conditions: the mappings L(l) must be injective, and the readout function R must also be injective. The paper highlights that aggregation-based GNNs, such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), fail to distinguish isomorphic graphs (A(G1) = A(G2)). However, they may erroneously map non-isomorphic graphs to the same embedding with a probability ǫ. 

To measure the distinguishing capability of a GNN, a metric called capacity CA is defined, representing the probability of mapping non-isomorphic graphs to different embeddings. A higher capacity indicates a stronger ability to differentiate between non-isomorphic graphs, while the capacity of the WL test serves as an upper bound for any aggregation-based GNN.

The training process for the GNN is framed as an optimization problem aimed at maximizing this capacity. A greedy layer-wise training approach is proposed, where each layer is optimized sequentially to enhance the GNN's distinguishing power.

Theorem 5 provides a condition under which a layer-wise trained GNN can achieve the same capacity as one trained end-to-end, given that the conventional training results in a powerful enough network. Conversely, Theorem 6 states that if a GNN is trained layer-wise, even if the layer mappings are not guaranteed to be injective, the network's capacity will not decrease as the number of layers increases, ensuring a monotonically non-decreasing capacity with added layers.
