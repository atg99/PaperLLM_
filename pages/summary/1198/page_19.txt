The paper discusses the philosophical implications of unsupervised learning, particularly in the context of clustering algorithms. It addresses skepticism regarding the arbitrary nature of distance functions used in clustering, arguing that while users could manipulate these functions, such practices are indicative of poor statistical methods. Practitioners often rely on established distance measures, which, while allowing for multiple plausible clusterings, do not lead to limitless combinations. The paper suggests that disagreements among rational agents about the best clustering reflect broader scientific challenges, where multiple models may fit the evidence equally well.

Furthermore, the paper emphasizes that unsupervised learning models typically do not operate in isolation; they are often validated through additional methods, as demonstrated in cancer subtyping studies where clustering results are compared to survival curves. This multi-method approach helps mitigate concerns about the validity of unsupervised learning outputs.

The ethical implications of unsupervised learning are also highlighted, especially in sensitive contexts where marginalized groups may be underrepresented in data. The example of genome-wide association studies illustrates how biases in data can perpetuate health inequalities, as a significant majority of subjects are of white European ancestry, despite this group being a small fraction of the global population.

The paper warns that unsupervised learning algorithms not only risk amplifying existing biases but may also introduce new ethical dilemmas. It compares the potential for misleading outcomes from these algorithms to the risks posed by photography, particularly in the context of deepfakes, which can be used maliciously and undermine trust in visual and audio evidence. Overall, the paper calls for a critical examination of unsupervised learning's role in scientific discovery and its broader societal implications.
