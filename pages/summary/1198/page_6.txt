In this paper, D. S. Watson discusses the role of clustering algorithms in addressing the philosophical problem of natural kinds, which involves categorizing entities in a way that reflects their inherent properties. Watson contrasts hierarchical clustering with k-means clustering, emphasizing that hierarchical methods provide a consistent partitioning of data at various resolutions, unlike k-means, which can yield different clusters for different values of k without a recursive relationship.

The paper identifies two main philosophical inquiries: the ontological question of what constitutes a natural kind and the epistemological question of how we can identify them. Watson argues that clustering algorithms provide practical insights into both areas, albeit with varying degrees of effectiveness.

On the epistemological side, he posits that we learn to identify natural kinds through clustering algorithms, whether implemented by computers or humans. He cites examples from various fields, such as clinical medicine and phylogenetics, to illustrate how both prototype-based and recursive partitioning methods are employed to classify entities and support inductive reasoning.

For the ontological question, Watson suggests that natural kinds can be understood as those entities that clustering algorithms ideally reveal. He acknowledges that while clustering algorithms alone cannot ensure the identification of natural kinds, they offer valuable, data-driven strategies for hypothesis formation that can be further tested through scientific inquiry. Overall, Watson's analysis highlights the intersection of computational methods and philosophical discourse in the pursuit of understanding natural kinds.
