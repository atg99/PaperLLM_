The paper by D. S. Watson discusses the three main branches of machine learning (ML): supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning. It emphasizes that while supervised and reinforcement learning receive significant attention in philosophical discourse, unsupervised learning is often overlooked. Supervised learning is associated with predictive tasks like image labeling, while reinforcement learning focuses on optimizing policies, such as in board games. 

The author notes that contemporary philosophers have primarily engaged with the implications of supervised learning, particularly regarding automated decision-making systems and their social impacts. In contrast, unsupervised learning, which involves identifying patterns without labeled outcomes, has not been a mainstream topic among philosophers, despite a few notable exceptions. 

Some discussions around unsupervised learning include clustering algorithms and the ethical concerns surrounding generative models like deepfakes. The paper also references recent work suggesting that unsupervised methods, such as topic modeling, can enhance hermeneutic interpretation by grounding it in objective evidence, although such analyses are often limited to specific applications. 

Overall, Watson highlights a significant gap in philosophical engagement with unsupervised learning, calling attention to its potential implications and applications that remain largely unexplored.
