This paper discusses the philosophy underlying unsupervised learning, particularly the role of autoencoders and latent variable models in capturing complex data relationships. It highlights the effectiveness of word embeddings, which can represent analogical relations in text data, exemplified by the equation "king" - "man" + "woman" ≈ "queen." The paper also addresses the evolution of autoencoders from generic statistical tools to applications in causal discovery, referencing algorithms like LiNGAM and recent advancements in causal representation learning.

The authors suggest the manifold hypothesis as a potential explanation for the success of autoencoders, positing that data in high-dimensional spaces often reside on low-dimensional manifolds. This concept is supported by both theoretical and empirical evidence and may clarify why effective compression techniques can reveal meaningful structures in complex data, such as distinguishing between disease and healthy samples in cancer research.

The philosophical analogy of Plato's cave is employed to illustrate the idea that our observations may misrepresent reality's simplicity. Just as prisoners perceive only shadows of the true forms, our data may obscure a more fundamental reality. The paper argues that abstraction algorithms, akin to philosophical tools, help access this underlying simplicity by differentiating between essential and contingent properties of objects. This discussion connects modern essentialism with historical philosophical thought, emphasizing the ongoing relevance of these concepts in understanding unsupervised learning.
