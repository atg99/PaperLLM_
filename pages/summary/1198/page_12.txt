In this paper, D. S. Watson argues for the role of abstraction algorithms in identifying essential properties. He differentiates between contingent attributes, such as hairstyle, and non-contingent attributes, such as humanity, asserting that the former can change while the latter cannot. The central thesis, EA, posits that we learn to identify essential properties through abstraction algorithms. Watson adopts a functionalist view of computation, suggesting that the physical implementation details are irrelevant, allowing both proponents of antirealism and realism to find merit in his arguments. 

He introduces the claim OA, which states that essential properties are what abstraction algorithms should ideally discover. However, he critiques this notion, arguing that it cannot guarantee the identification of all essential properties, as some may resist reduction to their essential forms. This claim highlights the limitations of our world rather than our cognitive or technological capabilities.

Watson also discusses the significance of understanding various grammatical moods in intelligent behavior, emphasizing the necessity of abstraction for higher-order cognition, such as planning. He concludes that while abstraction algorithms may effectively uncover some essential properties, their applicability is not universally guaranteed and should be assessed empirically on a case-by-case basis.
