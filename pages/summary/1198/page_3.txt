The paper addresses the philosophical neglect of unsupervised learning within the broader context of machine learning (ML). It highlights the significant applications of unsupervised learning, such as fraud detection and image compression, and questions why it has received less attention compared to supervised and reinforcement learning. The author posits that this neglect may stem from a philosophical preference for clearly defined concepts and success criteria, which are often lacking in the diverse and heterogeneous field of unsupervised learning.

Unsupervised learning is characterized by the absence of labeled data or reward signals, aiming to infer the underlying structure of data autonomously. The paper argues that this branch of ML raises distinct philosophical questions regarding the identification of natural kinds, inference of properties, and exploration of possibilities without supervision. The author examines three core unsupervised learning tasks—clustering, abstraction, and generative modeling—arguing that each task embodies a metaphysical hypothesis about the data and the target system.

The author contends that unsupervised learning is ontologically fundamental, more so than supervised or reinforcement learning, which rely on predefined distinctions between inputs and outputs. Additionally, the paper explores the ethical implications and epistemological limitations of unsupervised learning, emphasizing the risk of self-delusion due to the automated nature of these algorithms. The author concludes with a call for further research in this area, underscoring the need to critically engage with the implications of unsupervised learning methodologies.
