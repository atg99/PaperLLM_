The paper discusses the ethical implications and challenges surrounding large-scale vision datasets, particularly focusing on the JFT-300M dataset, which remains largely inscrutable and lacks transparency regarding its curation. The dataset reportedly contains over 300 million images across 18,000 categories, but concerns arise from the sourcing of these images, including the discovery of non-consensual images of children. The authors highlight the risks associated with opaque datasets like JFT-300M and others, such as CelebA-HQ and Open Images, which can lead to biased and controversial outcomes in AI applications.

The authors emphasize the cultural shift within the AI community, where the appropriation of images without consent has become normalized, raising questions about the existence of other secretive datasets. They draw parallels to the case of Chang versus Virgin Mobile, illustrating the limitations of Creative Commons licenses, which address copyright but not privacy or consent issues. This misinterpretation has led to the creation of datasets that exploit these loopholes, resulting in the removal of problematic datasets like MegaFace.

Furthermore, the paper introduces the concept of a "blood diamond effect," suggesting that models trained on ethically dubious datasets carry inherent moral burdens. This is exemplified in the realm of neural generative art, where tools built on these datasets may perpetuate issues of non-consensual image use and privacy violations. The authors argue for a more rigorous ethical framework in the curation and use of datasets to safeguard individual privacy and consent rights in AI development.
