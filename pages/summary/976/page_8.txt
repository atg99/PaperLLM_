The paper discusses the components and levels of language generation, outlining four key tasks: content selection, textual organization, linguistic resources, and realization. Content selection involves determining which information to include, requiring parsing and potential modification of representational units. Textual organization ensures that information is sequenced and linguistically structured according to grammar. Linguistic resources refer to the selection of specific words, idioms, and syntactic constructs necessary for conveying the information. Finally, realization transforms the selected and organized resources into actual text or voice output.

In addition, the role of the speaker is emphasized as a facilitator of the process rather than an active participant in language generation. The speaker maintains a model of the situation, storing relevant history and structuring content, with the primary requirement being the ability to make sense of the situation.

The paper further traces the evolution of Natural Language Processing (NLP) from its early roots in machine translation during the late 1940s, highlighting significant milestones and setbacks. Initial efforts in machine translation faced challenges, leading to a decline in research by 1966. However, advancements in machine translation systems emerged in the following years, alongside the development of AI-influenced systems like BASEBALL, LUNAR, and SHRDLU, which improved linguistic processing capabilities.

The 1980s saw increased research in computational grammar theory, linking linguistic understanding to user beliefs and intentions. By the end of the decade, general-purpose sentence processors and discourse representation theories emerged, facilitating more complex discourse analysis. The period was characterized by the growth of a research community, with the availability of practical resources, grammars, tools, and conferences that focused on speech recognition and information extraction, marking significant progress in the field of NLP.
