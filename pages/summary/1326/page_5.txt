The paper presents an analysis of various neural network architectures, focusing on their memory requirements, computational demands, and the feasibility of adapting them for resource-constrained environments. It examines the distribution of weights, features, and floating point operations (FLOP) across multiple layers in networks such as VGG16, NN1, and DeepFace, highlighting that larger networks exceed memory constraints and have longer processing times.

The authors found that all assessed networks require a minimum of 30 MiB of RAM for weights and 3.2 MiB for features, with smaller networks meeting the 300 MiB memory limit, while larger networks do not. Processing times for all candidate networks were over 1.2 seconds, with VGG requiring significantly more time on the Raspberry Pi platform.

To address these challenges, the paper introduces the concept of "backyard dog" networks, which are derived from a legacy network. The methodology involves extracting features from each layer of the legacy network, beginning with an RGB image input. This process allows for the identification of relevant image characteristics at each layer, balancing the need for robustness in performance with computational resource constraints. The authors propose a layer-by-layer assessment to determine the optimal depth of the network, ultimately creating a truncated version of the legacy system that can operate within the imposed limitations while still delivering reasonable recognition accuracy.
