The paper explores various approaches to solving combinatorial optimization problems, specifically focusing on the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP), Delaunay Triangulation, and Convex Hull problem variants. The authors introduce a pointer network model that learns to predict approximately optimal solutions through supervised learning from previously solved instances. This model demonstrates some capacity to generalize across variable-sized problem instances, particularly in the 2D Euclidean TSP, where the input consists of city coordinates and the output is a permutation representing a tour.

Building on earlier work, Nowak et al. applied a supervised learning method using graph neural networks for the Quadratic Assignment Problem (QAP), achieving promising accuracy on smaller instances but lacking generalization to larger ones. Joshi et al. enhanced this concept by employing a Graph Convolutional Network to tackle larger instances of the 2D Euclidean TSP, noting that their method is more sample-efficient than reinforcement learning (RL) approaches but does not generalize as well.

The paper then shifts to reinforcement learning, highlighting Bello et al.'s adaptation of the pointer network architecture for TSP solving. They introduced an actor-critic framework, using expected tour length as a reward signal, and developed methods for generating tours from the trained model. Nazari et al. further innovated by eliminating the sequential RNN encoder, focusing instead on combinatorial problems with dynamic data, applying an actor-critic method that considers state changes over time.

The paper also discusses improvements in RL frameworks, such as Kool et al.'s use of a Graph Attention Network that significantly enhances accuracy on the 2D Euclidean TSP compared to previous models. Ma et al. proposed a multi-level RL framework for TSP variants with hard constraints, where lower layers learn feasible policies and higher layers focus on optimizing objectives.

Overall, the paper presents a comprehensive overview of advancements in both supervised learning and reinforcement learning methods for solving complex combinatorial problems, highlighting their strengths and limitations in generalizing to larger instances and handling constraints.
