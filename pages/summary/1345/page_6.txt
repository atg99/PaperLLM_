The paper discusses the varying levels of trust in self-driving cars (SDCs) among different groups of drivers. A group of knowledgeable drivers preferred manual control, highlighting their mistrust in autonomous systems, while another group showed more trust and did not override the system. Howard's research indicates that most consumers view SDCs positively due to their ease of use, particularly in stressful driving situations like heavy traffic. However, concerns about costs, liability, and loss of control were prevalent, with income and gender influencing attitudes.

Carlson et al. conducted a statistical analysis that found similar trust concerns across domains, emphasizing the importance of past performance and reliability in SDCs and medical systems like IBM Watson. Kyriakidis et al. surveyed 5000 participants worldwide, revealing optimism about the popularity of fully automated cars by 2050 but also significant safety concerns, including hacking and legal issues. Educated participants from developed countries expressed discomfort with data transmission from SDCs.

Further exploration into building trust in SDCs highlighted the necessity of thorough testing and high accuracy in machine learning functions, which currently cannot meet near-perfect standards. Butakov and Ioannou proposed that user comfort and trust could improve if autopilot systems closely resembled traditional vehicle dynamics, suggesting a customizable approach to autopilot features.

The paper also includes a table summarizing various studies on trust in human-robot interaction, demonstrating that trust is influenced by factors such as performance, reliability, design, and user control. Overall, the findings underscore the critical role of trust and safety in the acceptance and development of autonomous systems.
