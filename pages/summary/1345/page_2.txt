The paper discusses various notions of trust, particularly in the context of human interaction with autonomous systems. Trust is defined formally, allowing for its measurement and quantification, which is essential for technological systems to respond appropriately to human trust levels. The Oxford dictionary defines trust as a firm belief in the reliability, truth, or ability of someone or something, which is adapted in this context to refer to human trust in autonomous systems such as robots, self-driving cars, and software agents.

The paper categorizes research on trust into four areas: trust in human-robot interactions, self-driving cars, and autopilot systems. Muir's analysis from a psychological perspective highlights the evolution of trust in decision support systems (DSS), emphasizing the importance of user trust in these systems for effective decision-making. Key factors for calibrating trust include improving user perception of the system's reliability, modifying trustworthiness criteria, and continuously identifying and fixing poor trust calibrations.

Madhaven and Wiegmann propose a framework for increasing human trust in autonomous aids by leveraging psychological traits influencing trust. Their research identifies critical factors such as favoritism and subjective bias that affect human-robot trust relationships. Additionally, studies on factory automation by Lee and Moray, and Muir investigate trust variations during interactions with semi-automatic systems, revealing that perceived competence directly influences user trust. Users are likely to take manual control if they perceive incompetence, leading to decreased trust levels.

Overall, the findings suggest that understanding and predicting human trust patterns can enhance the effectiveness and reliability of autonomous systems in various applications.
