This paper explores the limitations of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) in image generation, specifically focusing on mode dropping in scene generation. The authors propose a two-level analysis: the distribution level and the instance level. At the distribution level, they utilize Generated Image Segmentation Statistics to compare segmented object classes in generated images versus ground truth images. They find that certain object classes, such as people and cars, are represented by fewer pixels in the generated images, indicating omissions in the distribution.

At the instance level, the authors introduce a new method called Layer Inversion, which allows for the identification of specific failure cases where the GAN fails to generate certain object classes. This method involves inverting a layer of the generator rather than the entire network, enabling the analysis of complex GANs and revealing features that the generator cannot accurately render.

The findings reveal that the omitted object classes are not merely distorted or rendered poorly; they are completely absent from the generated images. This suggests that GANs may ignore certain object classes that are too challenging to generate while still producing high-quality visual outputs. The authors position their work as a complementary approach to existing evaluation metrics, providing insights into the specific limitations of GANs in capturing semantic content.

The paper also situates its contributions within the broader context of GAN research, highlighting prior work on network inversion and visualization, and emphasizing the focus on understanding generative models rather than discriminative classifiers. Overall, the authors aim to enhance the understanding of how GANs operate and identify their weaknesses in generating complex scene compositions.
