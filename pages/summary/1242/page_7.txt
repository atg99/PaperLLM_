The paper discusses the tree-growing algorithm used in random forests, focusing on how nodes are split to optimize prediction accuracy. Initially, a simple random sample of predictor variables, denoted as mtry, is selected, and the algorithm evaluates potential splits by centering response values around the average of their respective subnodes, aiming to minimize the sum of squared deviations. The process continues until nodes can no longer be split due to size constraints or lack of variation.

Additionally, the paper explains the calculation of predictions in a random forest framework. For each tree in the ensemble, the prediction for a given case is determined by identifying which terminal node it falls into and averaging the response values of that node. This averaging accounts for instances where a training case may appear multiple times due to bootstrap resampling, leading to a weighted average of the training responses. The overall prediction is then presented as a weighted average across all trees, with non-negative weights that sum to one.

The tuning parameters mtry and nodesize are crucial, as they influence the balance between bias and variance in predictions. Larger mtry values typically reduce bias but may increase variance, while smaller nodesize values can enhance prediction accuracy. The paper suggests that the prediction intervals derived from the random forest model perform effectively across various settings of these parameters.
