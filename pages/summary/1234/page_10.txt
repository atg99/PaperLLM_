This paper discusses the development of generalized random forests, focusing on improving computational efficiency during the tree-growing process. The authors emphasize the importance of the split-selection step, which is typically the most computationally intensive part of tree growth. They propose using a regression splitting criterion that allows for the evaluation of all potential split points with a single data pass. This is contrasted with directly optimizing the original criterion, which can be complex and resource-heavy.

The paper draws parallels between tree splitting and change-point detection, suggesting that splits can be viewed as occurring at detected change points in the underlying function. The authors also verify that the error associated with their approximate criterion is within acceptable bounds, implying that their method will not introduce significant inefficiencies.

Furthermore, they outline a strategy for constructing a forest that provides consistent estimates of a target function. This involves training trees on subsamples of the data, which has been shown to enhance statistical performance in forest-based regression methods. Overall, the approach aims to combine the strengths of various statistical techniques to achieve reliable and efficient modeling through generalized random forests.
