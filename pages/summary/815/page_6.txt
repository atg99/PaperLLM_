In this section of the paper, the authors investigate the accuracy evaluation process in the context of model pruning. They observe that during fine-tuning, the weight distribution shifts only slightly, yet there is a significant difference in inference accuracy. This suggests that the weights themselves may not be the primary factor affecting accuracy at the evaluation stage. Additionally, there is a noted poor correlation between low-range accuracy and fine-tuned accuracy, indicating that evaluated accuracy may not be a reliable guide for selecting pruning candidates.

The authors identify that the batch normalization layer plays a crucial role in this discrepancy. Pruning candidates retain only a subset of parameters from the full model, which alters the layer-wise feature map data. However, the evaluation still utilizes the batch normalization statistics from the full model, leading to outdated values that can severely diminish evaluation accuracy. This misalignment ultimately disrupts the relationship between evaluation accuracy and the final accuracy of the pruned models.

To address these challenges, the authors propose that a brief fine-tuning of pruning candidates before evaluation can yield more accurate results, although this approach is time-intensive given the expansive search space. They present a quantitative analysis to support their claims and clarify the issues associated with vanilla evaluation methods that rely on global batch normalization statistics. The methodology for calculating these statistics during training is outlined, highlighting the importance of using updated values rather than global statistics when assessing pruned networks.
