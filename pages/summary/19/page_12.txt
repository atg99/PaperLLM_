The paper published in Artificial Intelligence Review discusses the evolution of deep Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and their significance in the field of artificial intelligence. It begins by outlining the foundational work of Hubel and Wiesel in the late 1950s, which paved the way for cognitive models and ultimately led to the development of CNNs. The authors categorize the advancements in CNN architectures into five distinct eras, starting from the late 1980s.

In the initial era, CNNs were applied to visual tasks, with a significant milestone being the introduction of the first multilayered CNN, ConvNet, by LeCun et al. in 1989. This model was based on Fukushima's Neocognitron but utilized supervised training through the backpropagation algorithm, marking a shift from unsupervised reinforcement learning. LeCun's ConvNet demonstrated effectiveness in recognizing handwritten digits and zip codes, laying the groundwork for modern 2D CNNs. An improved version, LeNet-5, was proposed in 1998 and became instrumental in document recognition tasks, particularly in optical character recognition. The commercial adoption of CNNs began in the early 1990s, with notable applications in ATMs and banking.

Despite its successes in specific tasks, LeNet-5 struggled with broader image recognition challenges, highlighting the need for further advancements in CNN architectures. The paper sets the stage for a detailed exploration of the architectural evolution of CNNs in subsequent sections.
