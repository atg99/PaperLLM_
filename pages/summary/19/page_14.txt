In the early 2000s, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) faced stagnation due to a lack of understanding of their internal mechanics, leading to their classification as black boxes. The complexity of CNN architectures and the heavy computational requirements hindered effective training, with the backpropagation algorithm being perceived as inadequate for reaching global minima on error surfaces. Consequently, CNNs were deemed less effective than handcrafted feature extraction methods, and the absence of comprehensive datasets limited their application in domains like object detection and video surveillance. During this period, statistical methods, particularly support vector machines (SVM), gained popularity due to superior performance.

Despite the general decline in interest, some research groups continued to refine CNNs. Notably, in 2003, Simard et al. enhanced CNN architecture, achieving competitive results against SVMs on the MNIST dataset. This advancement sparked renewed interest in CNNs, leading to their application beyond optical character recognition to areas such as face detection in video conferencing and crime regulation. Additionally, CNN systems were commercialized for customer tracking, and their potential in medical image segmentation and robot vision was explored.

From 2006 to 2011, the revival of CNNs was marked by the development of deeper architectures, although training remained challenging due to time consumption and limited hardware resources. Early training methods often struggled with issues like exponential decay and explosion when using typical activation functions like sigmoid. This period laid the groundwork for the subsequent resurgence of CNNs in various applications.
