OpenAI recently launched GPT-4, an advanced version of its language model that powers ChatGPT. This iteration boasts significant enhancements, including the ability to process images alongside text and an impressive performance on the US bar exam, scoring in the ninetieth percentile compared to the tenth percentile of its predecessor. However, access to GPT-4 is currently limited to paying subscribers, leaving many researchers frustrated by their inability to explore its underlying technology, training data, and operational mechanisms. 

Concerns arise within the scientific community regarding OpenAI's lack of transparency, which some argue stifles research potential and hinders safety assessments. Critics, like Sasha Luccioni from HuggingFace, emphasize that closed-source models limit scientific progress. Andrew White, a chemical engineer who has tested GPT-4, noted that while initial interactions were underwhelming, the model's capabilities significantly improved when linked to scientific data.

Despite the advancements, there are worries about the potential misuse of the technology, including the generation of hazardous chemical synthesis and the propagation of false information, known as hallucination. OpenAI claims to have implemented safety measures, but without insight into the training data, many experts remain skeptical about these assurances.

Concerns about bias and accountability also loom large, especially as AI systems become predominantly owned by major tech companies. Researchers advocate for collaborative efforts to establish guidelines for the responsible use of AI technologies. A summit is planned at the University of Amsterdam to address these pressing issues, involving various international organizations.

While GPT-4 is poised to revolutionize scientific inquiry, experts stress that it will not replace human researchers but rather augment their capabilities by integrating various data sources and computational tools.
