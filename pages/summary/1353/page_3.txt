This article discusses the foundational aspects of Internet architecture established in the 1970s and its evolution over the decades. Initially funded by DARPA, the Internet was designed with military priorities, emphasizing survivability, flexibility, and performance rather than commercial considerations. The ARPANET was the first to implement the TCP/IP suite, laying the groundwork for modern Internet protocols.

The article outlines seven goals for the Internet architecture, with the first three being fundamental: 

1. **Survivability**: Ensuring continued communication despite network disruptions, protecting state information to facilitate ongoing conversations without needing to reestablish connections. 
2. **Variety of service types**: Supporting multiple communication services distinguished by varying requirements for speed, latency, and reliability, leading to the separation of the TCP and IP layers. 
3. **Variety of networks**: Accommodating diverse network technologies, including both military and commercial infrastructures.

The remaining four secondary goals include distributed resource management, cost-effectiveness, ease of host attachment, and accountability in resource usage. Over time, these goals have been addressed through advancements in management protocols and the business models of Internet Service Providers.

A significant debate in the 1980s was the end-to-end principle, which argued against implementing reliability functions at the network level. Instead, it posited that such functions should be managed by applications at the endpoints, as they are best equipped to handle tasks like duplicate message detection and data encryption. This principle profoundly influenced Internet security architecture and led to developments such as the IP-security sublayer and the growth of the VPN market. Today, security measures like the secure sockets layer for HTTP traffic are rooted in this end-to-end design philosophy.
