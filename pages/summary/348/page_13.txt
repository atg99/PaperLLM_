The paper discusses the evaluation of a chain-of-thought prompt template for multiple-choice questions, emphasizing a step-by-step reasoning process. The authors conducted experiments comparing curated few-shot exemplars from qualified clinicians with a baseline of randomly selected exemplars on GPT-4 and GPT-3.5. The results indicated negligible performance differences across tested datasets, suggesting that expert curation may not be essential for achieving high performance in contemporary large language models (LLMs).

The paper also addresses the issue of memorization in GPT-4, which could occur if benchmark data is included in the training set. Given the lack of transparency regarding GPT-4's training data, the authors developed a heuristic algorithm called the memorization effects Levenshtein detector (MELD) to identify potential data leakage. This method evaluates the similarity between generated outputs and original data to infer memorization. While MELD demonstrates high precision in detecting matches, its recall remains uncertain. The findings revealed no evidence of training data memorization in official USMLE datasets, but strong evidence was found in other datasets like SQuAD 2.0, indicating varying levels of memorization across different data sources.
