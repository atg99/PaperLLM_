The paper discusses the computational efficiency and characteristics of Random Forests (RF), emphasizing that the computation time increases linearly with the number of trees, allowing for parallel training on multiple CPU cores, as implemented in packages like ranger. The splitting rule, a core property of RF, is debated as it can also be categorized as a hyperparameter. Breiman's original RF utilizes Gini impurity for classification and weighted variance for regression to select the best split, potentially introducing variable selection bias favoring variables with many possible splits.

Conditional Inference Forests (CIF) aim to mitigate this bias by employing a two-step process that selects the variable with the smallest p-value first and then identifies the best split based on a linear test statistic. Hyperparameters related to p-value thresholds are also mentioned, with implications for split selection efficiency. Randomized splitting methods, such as those in the extraTrees package, enhance computational efficiency and introduce additional randomness to the trees.

The paper notes that no splitting rule has been universally proven superior, as performance can depend on dataset characteristics. Extremely randomized trees are highlighted as the fastest option, while CIFs tend to have the longest runtime. Additionally, the paper examines RF variable importance, which quantifies the significance of variables in predictions. It critiques the Gini variable importance measure for its bias towards variables with many categories and supports the use of permutation variable importance as a more reliable alternative.
