Generative models, such as Gaussian mixture models, assume that the data is generated from a mixture of distributions. By accurately modeling these distributions, one can infer the relationship between unlabeled data and their corresponding labels. This method relies on the assumption that the underlying generative process is correctly identified.

Another classical approach is the use of cluster-based methods, which operate under the cluster assumption. These methods group similar data points together and assume that points within the same cluster share the same label. By identifying clusters within the data, the algorithm can effectively label unlabeled samples based on their proximity to labeled instances.

Additionally, the low-density separation principle emphasizes that decision boundaries should be placed in low-density regions of the data space, avoiding high-density areas where clusters may be split. This helps maintain the integrity of clusters and supports more accurate predictions.

The manifold assumption posits that points located close to each other in a low-dimensional manifold will likely have similar labels. This concept helps to mitigate the curse of dimensionality by allowing algorithms to operate in lower-dimensional spaces where data is more densely populated.

In summary, semi-supervised learning encompasses various methods and assumptions aimed at improving classification accuracy by incorporating unlabeled data. The effectiveness of these methods is contingent upon the validity of their underlying assumptions regarding data distribution and structure.
