The paper discusses the effectiveness of self-supervised learning (SSL) strategies, specifically comparing contrastive and predictive learning objectives. It highlights the goal of SSL to maximize task-relevant information while minimizing task-irrelevant information. The authors present two key theorems: Theorem 1 establishes that when the amount of information is minimal, self-supervised representations can approximate the performance of supervised ones. However, as information increases, these representations may not always yield optimal downstream results, a phenomenon noted in previous studies.

Theorem 2 further explains the relationship between task-irrelevant information and compression gaps, indicating that sufficient self-supervised representations contain more irrelevant information compared to minimal representations. This suggests that the shared information between input and self-supervised signals can be substantial when downstream tasks require limited information.

The authors connect their findings to prior SSL objectives, particularly contrastive and predictive learning. They assert that contrastive learning effectively extracts task-relevant information by maximizing mutual information between representations and self-supervised signals. The paper proposes the use of contrastive predictive coding as a practical deployment strategy, emphasizing its low variance and effectiveness in capturing necessary information for downstream tasks. The overall contribution lies in clarifying how self-supervised learning can be optimized and its implications for practical applications in machine learning.
