The paper discusses the architecture and functioning of Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, highlighting their differences from traditional feed-forward neural networks (FFNNs). RNNs allow for cycles in the network, enabling information to persist across time steps, which is essential for processing sequences. The equations governing RNNs illustrate how hidden states and outputs are computed based on input sequences and previous hidden states.

Training RNNs can be challenging due to the vanishing gradient problem, where gradients become too small to effect weight updates, hindering learning. Techniques such as Back-Propagation Through Time (BPTT) and Real-Time Recurrent Learning (RTRL) are employed for training, with BPTT unfolding the network into a multilayer FFNN for each sequence processed. The paper notes that RNNs are suitable for time series prediction but often struggle with long-term dependencies.

To address these limitations, LSTM networks incorporate memory cells and forget gates, allowing them to learn from longer sequences (over 1000 time steps) without the vanishing gradient issue. The paper references research demonstrating the effectiveness of LSTM-RNNs in supervised classification tasks, particularly in intrusion detection, where performance metrics like the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and Area Under the Curve (AUC) are used to evaluate model effectiveness. The AUC provides a measure of a model's predictive accuracy, with values ranging from 0 (completely incorrect) to 1 (completely correct).
