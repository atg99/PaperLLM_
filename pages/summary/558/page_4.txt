The paper discusses a novel approach to neural architecture search, focusing on the design and functionality of intermediate nodes within a layered architecture. Each layer's output node is denoted as Cl, with the input nodes being the outputs from the two preceding layers, Cl-2 and Cl-1. The functionality of an intermediate node j is defined using a weighted sum of its connected nodes' outputs, incorporating a softmax function for operation selection based on a mixing weight vector. This method allows for flexible architecture design, accommodating varying spatial resolutions across layers, unlike previous methods that required uniform dimensions.

The authors introduce the concept of a residual cell, which includes not only the outputs from intermediate nodes but also the input to the cell, facilitating the learning of residual mappings. This design is shown to outperform traditional direct cells that concatenate all intermediate outputs.

Candidate operations for the feature and matching networks are tailored to their specific functions, with certain operations from prior models being deemed unnecessary without degrading performance. The feature net focuses on extracting local features for pixel-wise comparisons, while the matching net employs a different set of operations.

The network-level search space is defined by the arrangement of cells and their configurations, particularly focusing on variations in feature dimensionality and information flow. The authors propose a four-level trellis structure with specific downsampling rates, which empirically demonstrates effective performance across various benchmarks. The initial layers consist of a structured stem with convolution layers designed to efficiently process input images.
