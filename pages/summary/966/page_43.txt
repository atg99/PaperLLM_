The paper discusses the relationship between function approximation and estimation errors in the context of reinforcement learning. It begins with the definition of a loss function, Ln(θ), which measures the discrepancy between predicted rewards and actual observed rewards. The assumption is made that the feature space has full column rank, leading to a minimum loss of zero when the optimal function is found.

However, if the rewards are noisy, the approximation to the true value function, V, will be inaccurate, leading to a large estimation error. This phenomenon, known as overfitting, occurs when the model fits the noise in the data rather than the underlying function. Choosing a smaller dimensionality for the function space can mitigate overfitting but may increase the approximation error, thus highlighting a trade-off between these two types of errors.

The paper introduces a parameter vector, θ*, which minimizes the expected loss, L(θ). A bound is provided relating to the estimation and approximation errors, indicating that the first term increases with higher dimensionality, while the second term typically decreases. The convergence of Ln(θ) to L(θ) is explained through the law of large numbers, but it is noted that this does not guarantee that the minimizer of Ln(θ) will yield a small loss according to L(θ), emphasizing the need for uniform closeness between the two functions.

In conclusion, the paper illustrates the delicate balance between model complexity and error minimization in reinforcement learning, while providing bounds on the estimation and approximation errors that arise from the choice of feature space dimensionality.
