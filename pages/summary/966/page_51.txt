The paper discusses the efficiency of exploration strategies in Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), particularly contrasting random exploration with systematic exploration. It highlights that a random policy may require an average of 3(2n - n - 1) steps to reach state n from state 1, whereas a systematic exploration policy can achieve this in O(n) actions. The focus shifts to an explore-then-exploit learner that suffers from high regret due to the exponential number of steps before it transitions to exploitation, even when employing simple exploration strategies based on action value estimates.

The work references Kearns and Singh's E3-algorithm, which explores an unknown stochastic MDP and halts upon identifying a satisfactory policy for the current state, proving that it requires a polynomial number of interactions in discounted MDPs. The R-max algorithm by Brafman and Tennenholtz refines E3, yielding similar results. Domingo's adaptive sampling further enhances E3's efficiency under specific conditions. However, both E3 and R-max need knowledge of the "-mixing time of the MDP to function effectively in undiscounted scenarios, which complicates their application when this information is unavailable.

The paper also touches on the limited understanding of active learning algorithms in practical contexts, citing experimental results from Simsek and Barto. It transitions to online learning in MDPs, where one objective is to minimize regret—the difference between the total reward of the optimal policy and that of the learner. The UCRL2 algorithm is introduced as a means to address regret minimization within finite MDPs, assuming bounded rewards between 0 and 1 and that all policies eventually visit all states with certainty, characterizing the MDP as unichain.
