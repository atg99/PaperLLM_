The derivatives of the log-likelihood are derived from the joint and conditional expectations, where the former is computationally intensive due to the need to consider all possible values for hidden and visible units. To address this challenge, contrastive divergence is employed as a method to approximate these joint expectations.

Contrastive divergence operates by first sampling a point from the visible layer using Gibbs sampling, which is then used to compute the expected values for the hidden layer. This approach focuses on minimizing the gradients with respect to the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) by iteratively adjusting the model based on a single observation at a time. Rather than attempting to prevent the generation of all incorrect outputs, the model is trained to avoid generating specific negative samples, thereby gradually improving its ability to produce correct observations.

The algorithm for training an RBM using contrastive divergence involves initializing model parameters, sampling mini-batches from the training data, performing Gibbs sampling to obtain hidden states, calculating gradients, and updating parameters using gradient descent. The efficiency of this method is supported by empirical evidence indicating that only a small number of Gibbs sampling iterations are necessary, with some applications successfully using just one iteration. Overall, contrastive divergence serves as a practical and effective technique for training RBMs by simplifying the computation of joint expectations and facilitating the gradient descent optimization process.
