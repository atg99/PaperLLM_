This paper investigates the trade-offs between model accuracy and calibration, particularly in the context of selective prediction scenarios, such as medical diagnosis. The analysis focuses on various model families, including MLP-Mixer, ViT, and BiT, assessing their classification errors and expected costs when making predictions or choosing to abstain.

The findings reveal that calibration correlates with accuracy in out-of-distribution benchmarks, suggesting that better-calibrated models may not always be the most effective choice. Specifically, when comparing two BiT variants—one with higher accuracy (R152x4) and the other with better expected calibration error (ECE) (R50x1)—the model with superior accuracy consistently incurs a lower overall cost across various abstention rates, particularly up to 70%. This outcome highlights the practical importance of prioritizing accuracy over calibration in many scenarios.

The paper addresses the complexities and limitations of measuring calibration, noting potential biases in estimating ECE due to binning methods and finite sample sizes. It emphasizes that while calibration is a critical aspect of model evaluation, the specific cost structures and application contexts must guide practitioners in their model selection. Ultimately, the study advocates for choosing the most accurate model available, as it generally leads to better outcomes in practical applications, despite differences in calibration.
