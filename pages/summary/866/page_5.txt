The paper presents an empirical evaluation of feature importance assessment methods using various data sets. The experiments were conducted on diverse data sets, including DLBCL, Genes, p53, Chess, pd-speech, aps-failure, biodeg, optdigits, and madelon, each with distinct characteristics in terms of features and instances. The primary aim was to assess the effectiveness of Self-Attention Networks (SANs) in identifying relevant features and their impact on classification performance.

In the experiments, a synthetic binary classification data set was created, consisting of 100 features, with only 50 being relevant. The classification was performed using three repetitions of three-fold cross-validation. Attention values were aggregated based on correct predictions, focusing on their relevance in distinguishing between classes.

Results indicated that SANs exhibited higher attention on positive instances, suggesting their capability to detect relevant signals. Furthermore, comparisons of feature importance approaches revealed that attention-based methods closely align with traditional techniques like ReliefF and Random Forest, particularly in larger data sets. The classification performance demonstrated that SANs excelled in larger data sets, outperforming other methods by significant margins, while exhibiting competitive results in smaller data sets.

The discussion highlights the advantages of SANs in handling propositional data, particularly their ability to identify important features. However, the architecture's space complexity poses challenges, especially with an increasing number of attention heads in data sets with many features. Overall, the findings emphasize the potential of SANs in feature importance estimation, while also noting the strengths of established methods in low-resource scenarios.
