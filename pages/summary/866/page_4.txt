The paper discusses the time complexity involved in computing feature importance using Self-Attention Networks (SANs) for both individual instances and sets of instances. Three SAN implementations are analyzed: two instance-based methods that compute attention for each instance and aggregate results, and a global method that extracts the attention vector post-training. The first method has linear complexity relative to the number of test instances, while the global method reduces complexity to O(1) after training, making it suitable for large-scale applications.

The paper provides a comparative overview of computational complexities for various feature importance estimation algorithms, including ReliefF, Random Forest, and Mutual Information, across learning and post-learning stages. Notably, the SAN's complexity is O(|F|²·|I|) during the learning phase and O(1) or O(|I|) afterward, depending on whether attention vectors are computed post-training.

The experimental evaluation consists of two parts: measuring feature ranking similarity using the FUJI score and assessing feature ranking quality through classification performance with Logistic Regression. The FUJI score, a fuzzy adaptation of the Jaccard index, provides a more stable comparison of feature rankings by considering feature importances directly. The experiments also explore how the top n features influence the performance of the Logistic Regression classifier, highlighting the importance of selecting relevant features for effective classification.

Overall, the paper emphasizes the computational efficiency of SANs in estimating feature importance and their effectiveness in classification tasks, supported by structured empirical evaluations.
