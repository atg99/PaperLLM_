The paper discusses the use of Self-Attention Networks (SANs) for computing feature importance in machine learning models. It begins with an overview of SAN architecture, highlighting the attention layer where feature relations are established through weights. The authors argue that self-relations of features indicate their importance. 

The paper proposes three methods for computing feature importance. The first method, Instance-level aggregations, averages attention outputs across all instances. The second, Counting only correctly predicted instances, focuses on those instances that are accurately predicted, which reduces noise in the feature importance estimation. The third method, Global attention layer, directly uses the attention layer’s weights, assuming they inherently contain the necessary information on feature importance.

The authors also address the theoretical aspects of SANs, particularly their space and time complexity. The computation of the attention vector is identified as the most resource-intensive part, with a complexity of O(|F|^2) for a single attention layer. This complexity can increase with multiple attention heads. Despite the high dimensionality of data, the paper asserts that SANs can effectively handle large feature sets, and suggests that sparsity at the input level could further optimize performance.

Overall, the study provides a comprehensive exploration of how SANs can be utilized for feature importance estimation, backed by theoretical insights into their computational efficiency.
