Contrastive learning has emerged as a significant method in natural language processing (NLP), initially introduced by Mikolov et al. in 2013. They developed a framework that utilized co-occurring words as semantically similar points and employed negative sampling to enhance the learning of word embeddings. This approach improved the quality of learned representations while maintaining computational efficiency. Subsequent advancements, such as those by Arora et al., established a theoretical basis for contrastive learning, demonstrating its effectiveness in classification tasks using unlabeled data.

Recent models like CONPONO have focused on discourse coherence and sentence ordering, outperforming established models like BERT despite having fewer parameters. Contrastive learning has also shown substantial benefits across various NLP tasks, including cross-lingual pretraining and language understanding. For instance, the INFOXLM model enhances cross-lingual transferability by maximizing mutual information between machine translation pairs, outperforming previous methods.

Models like CERT and DeCLUTR have targeted sentence-level semantics and universal sentence embeddings, respectively. CERT demonstrated improved performance over BERT on multiple tasks in the GLUE benchmark, while DeCLUTR surpassed the popular InferSent method in sentence embedding quality.

Despite the promising empirical results, there is a call for more theoretical analysis to solidify the foundations of contrastive learning in NLP. Studies indicate that certain methods may not adequately capture nuanced viewpoints and categorical instances, suggesting that further research is needed to enhance understanding and application in the field.
