This paper discusses the advancements in self-supervised learning techniques, particularly focusing on generative models and contrastive learning (CL). It highlights the effectiveness of tasks such as image inpainting, colorization, and super-resolution in learning robust representations using pseudolabels. Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), introduced in 2014, laid the groundwork for various successful architectures but presented challenges such as non-convergence and the need for synchronization between the generator and discriminator.

In contrast, contrastive learning is described as a discriminative approach that aims to group similar samples closer together while distancing diverse samples. This is achieved through a similarity metric that evaluates feature representations extracted from an encoder network. During training, augmented versions of samples are treated as positive, while other samples are considered negative. The model learns to differentiate between these using a pretext task, ultimately gaining quality representations that can be transferred to downstream tasks.

An experiment by Epstein in 2016 is cited to support the idea that the brain can differentiate objects with only partial information, emphasizing the effectiveness of rough representations in visual recognition. This insight underpins the principles of contrastive learning as illustrated in the paper.
