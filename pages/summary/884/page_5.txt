The paper discusses the challenges of stylization performance in neural networks, particularly focusing on the role of small entropies in the optimization process. It identifies two main explanations for the observed poor performance. 

The first explanation highlights the sensitivity of the L2 distance metric to outliers, which leads to a focus on creating similar peaks in the Gram matrices of the generated images. When the Gram matrices derived from the style image are low in entropy and "peaky," the optimization process tends to overfit to these few strong activation pairs, neglecting other important entries. This results in a degradation of stylization quality, as the network fails to capture long-range correlations vital for texture and style, which are typically represented in deeper layers.

The second explanation frames stylization as a knowledge distillation problem, where the goal is to minimize the distance between the activations and Gram matrices of a 'student' network and a 'teacher' network. The paper argues that higher entropy distributions facilitate learning by producing gradients of lower variance. Thus, optimizing for higher entropy in the activations and Gram matrices can enhance the stylization process.

The paper further examines why residual networks produce peaky activations and Gram matrices, attributing this to the architecture of the bottleneck ResNet block. The design choice of adding residuals after the batch normalization and ReLU layers leads to large activation values, as the output of a ReLU layer is always positive. This creates a situation where large activations can proliferate through the network layers, resulting in a "whack-a-mole" effect where correcting one activation can inadvertently amplify others.

Overall, the paper underscores the importance of understanding the interplay between activation distributions and network architecture in achieving effective stylization outcomes.
