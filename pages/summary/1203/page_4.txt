The paper discusses the differences between two methodologies for composing components in image generation: COMET and MONET. While both methods utilize summation, MONET operates in the image domain, whereas COMET sums cost functions, allowing for non-linear combinations of components. This non-linearity enables the modeling of more complex relationships, resulting in a generated output that reflects the collective minimization of individual energy functions associated with each component.

In the section on unsupervised decomposition, the paper outlines how COMET identifies composable energy functions from an input image. The authors describe a training process that involves recomposing the input image through a learned neural network encoder. Due to the computational challenges of directly evaluating the arg min operation, the authors propose an approximation method using gradient optimization over several steps. The algorithm iteratively refines the output by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between the generated sample and the original input.

Additionally, the paper addresses the control of local versus global decompositions within the generated output. By employing low latent dimensionality and positional embeddings, COMET can be biased towards local factor decompositions, which encourages the model to focus on specific image patches.

The authors also present a complexity-theoretic rationale for composing energy functions, indicating the potential advantages of this approach in managing intricate relationships among components. Overall, the paper highlights the effectiveness of the COMET framework in achieving disentangled representations without the need for additional objectives or priors.
