This paper explores the function space F, which consists of functions mapping component sets to output vectors. It identifies two subspaces: Fenergy, which includes functions of the form arg minx P1≤k≤K E(x; zk), and Fmask, which consists of functions of the form P1≤k≤K mk(zk)fk(zk). The paper argues that Fenergy is a strict superset of Fmask, demonstrating that compositions of energy functions are more expressive than those of segmentation masks. 

The proof establishes that any function in Fmask can be represented as a function in Fenergy, while there are functions in Fenergy that cannot be captured by Fmask due to their dependency on all components rather than fixed values assigned by specific components. 

Furthermore, the paper discusses computational efficiency, asserting that learning the energy function E(x; z) is more efficient than learning a function f(z) that approximates arg minx E(x; z). This distinction is rooted in the complexity theory, particularly in how verifying constraints (via energy functions) is easier than generating solutions (via decoders). 

To illustrate this, the authors encode a 3-SAT problem into an energy function E(x; z), showing that evaluating E(x; z) can be done in polynomial time, while computing f(z) is exponential in complexity, under the Exponential Time Hypothesis. 

The paper concludes that it is computationally advantageous to learn energy functions rather than decoders, as the capacity required to represent the latter grows significantly with the number of components. Additionally, as more energy functions are composed, the complexity of representing the optimization process increases exponentially.
