This paper examines the effects of intermediate-task training on low-level syntactic probing tasks and their relationship with target task performance. The findings reveal minimal improvement in SentEval probing tasks and significant gains in acceptability judgment probing tasks, except for AJ-CoLA, aligning with prior research indicating negative transfer effects in certain contexts. Performance varies across higher-level syntactic and semantic tasks, with some intermediate tasks like SocialIQA and QQP exhibiting negative transfer, while others enhance performance in specific probing tasks.

The analysis also highlights the occurrence of degenerate runs, where models fail to surpass chance performance, particularly affecting baseline runs on acceptability judgment tasks. Intermediate-task training generally reduces the likelihood of these degenerate runs, suggesting a positive transfer effect. However, negative transfer from certain intermediate tasks can lead to an increase in degenerate runs, impacting overall performance.

The study further investigates the correlation between probing and target task performances, employing Spearman correlation due to the diverse metrics involved. It finds that acceptability judgment probing tasks show little correlation with target tasks, except for AJ-CoLA. Notably, specific SentEval tasks demonstrate correlations primarily driven by negative transfer. The paper suggests that fine-tuning RoBERTa on intermediate tasks may lead to forgetting essential masked language modeling skills, proposing future improvements through integrating the MLM objective into training or constraining parameter changes to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. 

Overall, the results underscore the complex interplay between intermediate tasks and their effects on probing and target task performances, revealing the importance of task selection and training methodologies in optimizing model capabilities.
