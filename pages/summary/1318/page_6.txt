This paper discusses the levels of automation (LOA) as defined by Endsley and Sheridan, presenting a detailed comparison of their frameworks. Endsley categorizes levels from manual control to full automation, while Sheridan's framework ranges from human control to automated decision-making. The paper emphasizes the implications of these levels for human-AI relationships and function allocation in task execution. 

It highlights that in simpler tasks, static function allocation is effective, but in complex, uncertain environments, the need for dynamic function allocation becomes crucial. The authority of the function allocation agent, whether human or AI, is explored, questioning who should authorize changes in function allocation and under what conditions. Inagaki's perspective on automation's authority in safety-critical situations is discussed, suggesting that automation should have the right to act autonomously when human intervention may not be timely or possible.

The author identifies three key factors that should influence the decision-making authority of machines: time criticality, the skills required for judgment, and the severity of potential consequences. The paper concludes that in scenarios where immediate action is necessary, the machine may need to take control to prevent adverse outcomes. This analysis provides insights into designing AI systems that effectively balance human and machine roles in decision-making processes.
