The paper discusses the evolution of automation from basic to advanced systems, emphasizing the significant advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) that enable machines to perform tasks traditionally requiring human intelligence. It highlights the ability of AI to respond to critical situations, such as collision avoidance in vehicles, and contrasts this capability with the historical dynamic where humans controlled tools without challenge from machines.

The authors note that AI has long promised to independently solve problems and engage with humans, but its practical applications have often fallen short of expectations, leading to periods of stagnation known as "AI death." However, recent technological advancements in sensors, communication, and computing power have revitalized AI's potential, particularly through the concept of micro-services. This approach allows for specialized functions to be performed by independent services, as demonstrated by notable AI developments like the robot Sophia, which gained citizenship.

The paper also addresses the complexity of defining AI, suggesting that "computational intelligence" may be a more fitting term. The author provides two definitions of AI: the first focuses on the design of algorithms that enable machines to understand their environment, assess risks and opportunities, learn, and transfer knowledge. The second simplifies the concept to the automation of cognition aimed at enhancing decision-making skills.

Overall, the paper lays the groundwork for exploring the relationship between AI and humans, as well as the implications of AI's evolving capabilities in society. It emphasizes the need for humans to play a role in safeguarding against potential risks associated with AI advancements.
