The paper discusses two paradigms for understanding symbols and their relationships within networks of artificial neurons: the logic-inspired paradigm and the brain-inspired paradigm. In the logic-inspired approach, symbols lack internal structure, with meaning derived from their relationships represented through symbolic expressions or relational graphs. Conversely, the brain-inspired paradigm translates external symbols into internal vectors of neural activity, which possess a rich similarity structure. This allows for the modeling of relationships among symbols by learning specific activity vectors and non-linear transformations to address missing elements in symbol strings. Notable examples include Rumelhart et al.'s initial work on toy data and Bengio et al.'s application on real sentences, culminating in advanced models like BERT that utilize self-attention mechanisms.

A key advantage of using neural activity vectors for concept representation is the facilitation of automatic generalization. For instance, if the vectors for Tuesday and Thursday are similar, their effects on other neural activity vectors will also be similar. This supports the notion that analogical reasoning is a primary cognitive mode, with logical sequential reasoning emerging as a later development. The paper emphasizes the implications of these paradigms for understanding reasoning processes.
