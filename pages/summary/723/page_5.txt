The paper discusses advancements in policy gradient methods, particularly focusing on Natural Policy Gradient (NPG) as introduced by Sutton et al. in 1999. It quantifies function approximation results based on transfer error related to distribution shifts. The convergence rates of NPG are influenced by four critical quantities: excess risk (statis), average squared approximation error (approx), a condition number (τ), and a divergence measure (D1).

In the context of log-linear policies, the paper highlights that statis can approach zero with increasing sample sizes at a statistical rate of O(1/√N). The approximation error (approx) is zero in realizable cases, leading to polynomial convergence guarantees when τ is bounded. It is noted that a universal initial measure can ensure τ remains polynomially bounded concerning the feature dimension, independent of the state space size.

The authors suggest that other incremental algorithms, such as CPI, PSDP, and MD-MPI, may benefit from refined analyses that reduce dependency on worst-case density ratios. NPG's advantage lies in its explicit parametric policy representation, which remains succinct compared to boosting-style methods that increase representation complexity with each iteration. This simplicity may contribute to NPG's broader practical adoption.

Overall, the paper emphasizes the potential for sharper analyses in policy gradient methods and their implications for future research directions.
