The paper discusses the theory behind Policy Gradient Methods, focusing on the relative condition number and its implications for the Natural Policy Gradient (NPG) algorithm. It introduces a bounded average relative condition number for all iterations, which plays a crucial role in analyzing the performance of the NPG method.

Theorem 29 presents a result on agnostic learning using NPG, under specific assumptions regarding state and state-action distributions. It establishes a relationship between the transfer error and statistical error, providing a bound on the expected performance difference between the comparator policy and the learned policy.

Key differences between NPG and Q-NPG are noted, particularly the absence of polynomial dependence on the action space size in the NPG rate, contrasting with Q-NPG. The analysis highlights that NPG's approach to fitting the advantage function bypasses certain errors that arise in Q-NPG, which requires bounding errors related to advantage estimates.

Furthermore, a comparison with Theorem 16 reveals that in a noiseless, tabular setting, the statistical and bias errors are zero, leading to a simpler and faster convergence rate than in the function approximation setting addressed by NPG. This slower rate in NPG is attributed to the absence of a monotonicity property that is present in the tabular case.

In summary, the paper provides insights into the performance guarantees of NPG, emphasizing the role of the relative condition number and the differences in convergence behavior compared to Q-NPG in various settings.
