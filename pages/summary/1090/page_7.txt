This paper discusses Target Propagation in Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) as a solution to the challenges posed by vanishing and exploding gradients, which hinder the learning of long-term dependencies. The method involves setting local targets at each time step, enabling lower layers of the network to continuously learn and contribute to adjustments in hidden-to-hidden synaptic weights. This approach can theoretically lead to a reduction in global error, particularly when the mapping function G is a perfect inverse of F, as proven in prior research.

However, the paper highlights that achieving a perfect inverse may be computationally intensive and unstable, prompting the adoption of an approximate inverse instead. This change introduces uncertainty regarding whether local updates will effectively decrease the overall error.

Furthermore, the authors argue that target propagation aligns more closely with biological processes. Unlike traditional backpropagation, which requires error signals to travel backwards through synapses, target propagation optimizes towards local targets through feedback pathways. This is reminiscent of how certain neurons in the brain integrate feedforward and feedback information, suggesting a more biologically plausible learning mechanism. The concept of error-driven learning is further elaborated, drawing on models that divide the error signal into components that determine synaptic changes based on feedback connections.

Overall, the paper emphasizes the potential of target propagation to enhance learning in RNNs while also presenting a more biologically inspired framework for understanding synaptic plasticity.
