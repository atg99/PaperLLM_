The paper discusses the limitations of recurrent neural networks (RNNs), particularly in relation to long-term dependencies. While RNNs can outperform static networks for short sequences, they struggle with longer contexts due to issues of vanishing and exploding gradients. These phenomena hinder the network's ability to learn from temporally distant events. The authors reference foundational work by Bengio et al., which identifies that the spectral radius of the recurrent weight matrix is critical in determining whether gradients vanish or explode.

The paper also critiques the biological plausibility of backpropagation in RNN training, highlighting that it does not align with observed neurophysiological processes. The requirement for neurons to send distinct output and error signals is deemed unrealistic, and the method by which backpropagation addresses the credit assignment problem raises questions about its biological validity. Various strategies have been proposed to mitigate the gradient issues, such as architectural changes and optimization techniques, but the underlying concerns about the biological realism of the training methods remain prominent.
