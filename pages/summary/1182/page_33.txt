The paper discusses the concept of random walks in graph representation learning, specifically focusing on biased random walks and their applications in learning node embeddings. It formalizes the process of learning embeddings \( z_i \) for nodes \( x_i \) based on the probability of visiting other nodes during a random walk of length \( T \). The loss function optimized during this process is derived from the probabilities of node visits, but calculating it can be computationally expensive due to the need to evaluate all nodes in the graph.

Different methods, such as DeepWalk and node2vec, provide various optimizations to approximate this loss. DeepWalk uses a hierarchical softmax for efficiency, while node2vec introduces hyperparameters \( p \) and \( q \) to balance between breadth-first and depth-first search strategies in random walk generation. These embeddings can be utilized in semi-supervised learning (SSL) to train classifiers on labeled nodes, with approaches like Planetoid combining supervised and unsupervised losses for improved performance.

However, the paper notes limitations of random walk-based methods, including the lack of parameter sharing (resulting in unique embeddings for each node) and their transductive nature, which restricts embedding generation to nodes encountered during training. These issues are particularly significant for dynamic or large-scale graphs.
