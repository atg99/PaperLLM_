The paper discusses the complexities of providing meaningful explanations, highlighting that expectations for explanation types vary depending on the context and individual preferences. This variability complicates the task of generating consistent and satisfactory explanations.

The principle of explanation accuracy is examined, which asserts that a system's explanation should accurately reflect the reasons behind its outputs. This mirrors human decision-making, where introspection often fails to provide a true account of the mental processes involved. The concept of the "introspection illusion" is introduced, emphasizing that individuals frequently fabricate reasons for their decisions post hoc, rather than articulating genuine pre-decision reasoning. Neuroscience findings support this, indicating that neural markers for decisions can arise before conscious awareness.

The phenomenon of "choice blindness" illustrates how people may misremember their decisions and provide inaccurate justifications for choices they did not actually make. This suggests that, similar to algorithms, human decision-making and explanation accuracy are separate entities. Humans may achieve high accuracy in their decisions yet struggle to accurately verbalize the reasoning behind them.

The principle of knowledge limits is also addressed, stating that systems operate effectively only under designed conditions and when they have sufficient confidence in their outputs. This principle emphasizes the importance of metacognition, or awareness of one's own cognitive processes, in understanding the limitations of explanation accuracy and decision-making.
