The paper discusses four principles that guide the development of explainable AI systems, focusing on the distinctions between the existence, meaningfulness, and accuracy of explanations. It highlights ongoing disagreements regarding the relative importance of these factors, emphasizing that the context of application and user requirements will influence which principles take precedence.

The overview of explainable AI algorithms is categorized into two main types: self-interpretable models and post-hoc explanations. Self-interpretable models serve as their own explanations, allowing users to understand the model's decisions through its structure, with decision trees and regression models being common examples. These models can provide both global and local explanations.

On the other hand, post-hoc explanations are generated by separate tools to clarify how an algorithm works, often without needing internal knowledge of the algorithm. The paper notes the existence of various explanation subtypes and references surveys for further exploration.

The discussion includes the accuracy-interpretability trade-off associated with self-interpretable models, suggesting that these models may be less accurate than post-hoc approaches. However, some researchers contest this view, asserting that interpretable models can achieve high accuracy without sacrificing comprehensibility. Overall, the paper underscores the complexity of balancing various principles in the pursuit of effective explainable AI systems.
