This section discusses the principle of explainability in decision-making systems, particularly focusing on human explanations of judgments and decisions. It asserts that while humans can produce various types of explanations, verbalizing these explanations can hinder decision-making processes. As individuals gain expertise, their decision-making becomes more automatic and less conscious, making verbal explanations challenging. This is similar to the tension in AI, where higher accuracy may reduce explainability.

The text highlights that certain assessments may be more accurate when they remain implicit rather than requiring explicit judgment. An example provided is lie detection, where implicit categorization improves accuracy compared to explicit judgments.

The next principle emphasizes that explanations must be meaningful and understandable to the intended audience. This involves ensuring that the audience can reach the same conclusion intended by the explainer and agree on that conclusion. The section draws an analogy with forensic scientists explaining evidence to juries, noting significant gaps in understanding that render many explanations misleading. This indicates a challenge in enhancing the clarity and effectiveness of explanations in various contexts.
