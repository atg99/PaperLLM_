The section discusses two main branches of ethics: descriptive ethics and normative ethics. Descriptive ethics focuses on empirical insights into human behavior, while the text primarily emphasizes normative ethics, which evaluates actions based on moral correctness. Normative ethics categorizes actions as morally right or wrong, asserting that stealing, for instance, is universally considered wrong.

Within normative ethics, a significant distinction is made between deontological and consequentialist theories. Deontological ethics assesses the ethical nature of actions based on intrinsic characteristics, such as intention and adherence to moral principles, rather than solely on the outcomes of those actions. The term "deontology" comes from the Greek word for duty, highlighting its focus on obligations.

An example of deontological ethics is illustrated through the practice of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) among companies. Although many companies engage in CSR initiatives to improve their public image and address social or environmental issues, critics argue that these efforts are often insincere and primarily motivated by profit maximization. This critique underscores the importance of intentions in deontological assessments, where the motivations behind actions are deemed more significant than the actions or their results themselves.
