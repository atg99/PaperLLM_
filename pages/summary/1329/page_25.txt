The paper discusses the interplay between science and fiction in the portrayal of artificial intelligence (AI) and robots in popular media. It highlights how works like the movie "I, Robot" and the TV show "Westworld" shape public perceptions and expectations of AI. These portrayals often depict robots with intelligence levels that surpass those of humans, leading to unrealistic expectations when people encounter real AI systems, which can result in disappointment.

The paper critiques common tropes such as the "Frankenstein Complex," where AI systems become a threat to humanity, suggesting that it is illogical to assume that intelligent systems would replicate humanity's historical mistakes. Additionally, it points out the paradox of highly intelligent AI desiring to become human, often linked to their perceived lack of emotions.

The discussion extends to the implications of these fictional representations in policy-making, citing the European Parliament's recommendation to consider electronic personalities as premature. The case of the robot "Sophia," which was granted citizenship in Saudi Arabia, is presented as a publicity stunt rather than a meaningful advancement in AI, as Sophia's capabilities are limited to scripted dialogues and do not reflect true intelligence or adaptability.

The paper concludes by emphasizing the need to differentiate between the fictional narratives and the realities of AI, cautioning against anthropomorphizing robots to the extent of granting them legal personhood, which should be motivated by practicality rather than existential concerns.
