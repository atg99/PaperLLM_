The paper discusses the military uses of Autonomous Weapons Systems (AWS) and presents arguments from both proponents and opponents regarding their ethical implications. 

Opponents highlight concerns about AWS's ability to distinguish between combatants and non-combatants, citing Noel Sharkey's example of a child with an ice-cream cone versus a young adult with a gun. They argue that AWS may fail to accurately identify targets, leading to potential civilian casualties.

In terms of proportionality, proponents assert that the concept is ambiguous within International Humanitarian Law (IHL) and that decisions often depend on context. They argue that while opponents claim AWS cannot calculate proportionality—essentially weighing acceptable collateral damage against military advantage—proponents contend that such calculations are complex and typically require human input from various experts.

The issue of moral responsibility is another critical point of contention. Opponents argue that machines cannot be held morally accountable, suggesting this as a reason to ban AWS. In contrast, proponents assign responsibility for the actions of AWS to the humans who design and deploy them. This raises the "problem of many hands," complicating accountability in cases of adverse outcomes. Some suggest that legal frameworks, such as "strict liability," could hold the state responsible for the actions of AWS.

Overall, the debate centers on the ethical implications of using AWS in military contexts, focusing on discrimination, proportionality, and accountability.
