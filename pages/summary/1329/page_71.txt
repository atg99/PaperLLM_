The paper discusses the complex landscape of privacy issues related to artificial intelligence (AI), emphasizing the evolving nature of privacy rights in the United States. It argues that governmental control over privacy is often justified as a crime prevention strategy but can also serve broader control purposes. The Supreme Court has interpreted privacy contextually, influenced by factors such as a person's environment, age, and mental capacity.

Businesses, traditionally neutral in this debate, are caught between customer privacy demands and governmental obligations. For instance, Apple has resisted government requests for access to locked iPhones, underscoring the tension between protecting customer privacy and complying with legal demands. Historically, the balance of power has been skewed, with both governments and businesses lacking the technological means to effectively monitor citizens. However, advancements in data storage and collection technologies over the past five decades have shifted this dynamic, allowing for extensive data collection on a global scale.

The emergence of AI companies has further complicated privacy issues, as these entities increasingly recognize the value of user data for product development and targeted marketing. The paper raises critical questions about the necessity of data collection, suggesting that it may be driven by motives beyond mere utility. Ultimately, the erosion of privacy rights is a significant concern in the context of AI and data collection practices.
