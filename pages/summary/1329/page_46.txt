The paper discusses the distinction between explicability and transparency in AI systems, emphasizing that maximal transparency may not be beneficial and could even introduce new issues. It highlights that while software transparency may not be intelligible to non-experts and poses risks to competition, explicability—defined as intelligibility and accountability—is crucial, particularly in moral applications of AI. This allows users and affected individuals to understand AI decision-making processes.

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation mandates a "right to information," requiring explanations for algorithmic decisions, which presents challenges for complex machine learning methods like neural networks. While some argue that empirical testing suffices, moral and legal contexts necessitate clear justifications for decisions made by AI systems.

The paper also references ongoing research into "explainable AI," aimed at providing insights into neural network decision-making. However, practical examples, such as the use of the COMPAS system in assessing recidivism risk, illustrate the complexities involved. In the Loomis versus Wisconsin case, the court upheld that judges were not solely reliant on COMPAS scores for sentencing, indicating a need for accountability beyond algorithmic outputs. Ultimately, accountability measures, such as log files, are essential for ensuring trust and fairness in AI systems.
