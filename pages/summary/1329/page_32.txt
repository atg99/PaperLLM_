The relationship between ethics and law is often perceived as distinct, where ethics begins where law ends. This perspective can be contested, as legal rules frequently embody ethical principles. For instance, laws against environmental pollution reflect ethical norms. Additionally, ethics can function as a form of "soft law," guiding businesses to adhere to ethical standards to protect their reputation and stock value, even when not legally mandated. Such adherence can become a unique selling point, exemplified by companies offering "fair trade" products.

Machine ethics explores the complexities of designing ethical artificial intelligence capable of making moral decisions. Unlike humans, machines lack phenomenology, feelings, and moral intuition. While they can process data related to emotions, they do not experience consciousness or genuine feelings. The development of lifelike robots, such as Sophia, is often viewed as more of a marketing strategy than a genuine technological breakthrough. Despite the interest in creating moral machines, critics argue that roboticists have not provided compelling reasons for their development. A comprehensive moral theory is essential for guiding AI in making ethical decisions, defining what constitutes moral significance in various situations.
