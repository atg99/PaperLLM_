The paper discusses the concept of autonomous weapons systems (AWS) and their implications, including the distinction between non-lethal autonomy and lethal capabilities. Non-lethal autonomy refers to functions such as autonomous take-off, landing, and navigation, which are generally not viewed as morally controversial. In contrast, the term "killer robots" is debated, with some critics arguing it is a manipulative term used by advocacy groups like the Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, which seeks an international ban on lethal AWS.

The use of AWS raises several arguments, particularly concerning discrimination in targeting. While proponents acknowledge that machines may not discriminate as effectively as humans in general, they argue that in specific instances, such as with Identification Friend or Foe (IFF) technology, machines could outperform human operators. The paper cites the tragic incident of Malaysia Airlines flight MH 17, which was mistakenly shot down by a human-operated missile system lacking IFF, suggesting that an advanced autonomous system might have prevented such errors.

Advancements in technology, particularly in vision and sensory systems, are closing the gap between machines and humans in object discrimination. This raises ethical questions regarding the potential obligation to utilize superior autonomous systems in combat scenarios. However, the paper notes the ongoing challenge for machines to accurately interpret human behavior, which complicates their deployment in conflict situations.
