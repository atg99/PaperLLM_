The paper discusses the evolving definitions and concepts of artificial intelligence (AI), emphasizing its dynamic nature. Kaplan and Haenlein describe AI as a system's ability to interpret data, learn, and adapt to achieve specific goals. Poole and Mackworth focus on computational agents that act intelligently, highlighting criteria such as appropriate actions, flexibility, learning from experience, and making choices within limitations. Russell and Norvig categorize AI into four schools of thought: machines that think like humans, act like humans, act rationally, and think rationally.

The core idea uniting these definitions is the study and creation of intelligent agents capable of achieving goals while considering their perceptual and cognitive limitations. The paper notes that while flexibility and learning enhance intelligence, there are contexts, such as medical procedures, where AI should not learn new behaviors. Different approaches to AI emphasize varying aspects, with expert systems focusing on human-consultable knowledge and machine learning systems aimed at discovering new insights. Each perspective presents unique strengths and weaknesses.
