The virtual store setting could be a representation of fashion retail, furniture retail, 
but also a supermarket, a restaurant or other place at a certain destination, like a museum 
or hotel (e.g., Hwang, Yoon  & Bendle, 2012; Dad, Davies  & Rehman, 2016; Mazursky 
& Vinitzky, 2005; Waterlander, Jiang, Steenhuis  & Mhurchu, 2015; Kim & Krishnan, 
2015; Krasonikolakis, Vrechopoulos, Pouloudi  & Dimitriadis, 2018). In virtual store s, 
researchers tend to study mainly the ambient conditions (e.g., colours, décor, lighting, 
background music), space (e.g.,  layout, equipment, furniture), artefacts (e.g., symbols, 
style décor), information provided, and the social component (e.g., crowd, employees, 
customers, avatars).  
Giving some examples , Krasonikolakis, Vrechopoulos  & Pouloudi (2014) find 
that core store features and security and privacy are the most important store selection 
factors in virtual environments and that sales in virtual worlds are predicted by the 
frequency of visiting and the time spent within such stores. Dad, Davies and Rehman 
(2016) propos e a framework to explore the effect of 3D virtual reality retail stores' 
environment cues (physical, social, socially symbolic and natural dimensions) on 
shoppers' behaviour. Dad, Rehman, Kear and Davies (2018) focus their empirical study 
on the effect of music (atmospheric cue) in 3D virtual retail stores on shoppers' emotions 
and behaviour. Van Herpen, van den Broek, van Trijp and Yu (2016) highlight that virtual 
reality can improve realism in responses to shelf allocation. Consumers tend ed to buy
